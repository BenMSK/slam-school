1. From a fixed dictionary to an open one
@@ 11
Every map so far stored geometry, and sometimes appearance. Chapter 16 added semantics, but the perception models it used were trained <strong>closed-set</strong>: the dictionary of classes was decided when the model was trained, so it is fixed and finite. <span class="src">Ch. 17 · p. 490</span> A detector trained on 80 classes will never report an 81st.
@@ 12
A <strong>foundation model</strong> is a machine-learning model trained on a dataset so vast that we can reasonably expect it to generalize to a wide variety of operating conditions. It needs two things: an architecture that can efficiently update a very large number of parameters, and a very large dataset, for example one of internet scale. <span class="src">§17.1.1 · p. 491</span> Such models can learn <strong>open-world</strong> representations (the book treats open-set and open-vocabulary as synonyms): representations that may generalize to any class, object type, or concept one might meet. <span class="src">Ch. 17 · p. 490</span>

Filling the gap: why gradients vanish, and what a residual connection does
@@ 33
Training adjusts each parameter along the gradient of the loss. For a chain of layers \(\mathbf{x}_{l+1} = F_l(\mathbf{x}_l)\), the chain rule gives the gradient at the first layer as a product of one factor per layer:
@@ 34
If each factor shrinks a vector by about half, 20 layers shrink it by \(0.5^{20} \approx 10^{-6}\). The early layers get almost no signal: that is the vanishing gradient the book mentions on p. 492. A residual layer computes \(\mathbf{x}_{l+1} = \mathbf{x}_l + F_l(\mathbf{x}_l)\) instead, so each factor becomes \(\mathbf{I} + \partial F_l / \partial \mathbf{x}_l\). The identity term is the "direct pathway from one layer to the next": through it the gradient reaches early layers unshrunk, whatever \(F_l\) does.

3. Contrastive learning: pull the pair together, push the rest away
@@ 36
Text has an obvious self-made task: hide a word. A random image patch carries no such specific meaning, so images needed another idea. <span class="src">§17.1.1 · p. 492</span> <strong>Contrastive learning</strong> takes an input and a second version of it that should mean the same thing (a <strong>positive</strong>), and writes a loss that moves their representations together while moving all other inputs in the batch (the <strong>negatives</strong>) away. In SimCLR the positive is the same image after an augmentation such as flipping or removing patches. <span class="src">§17.1.1 · pp. 492–493</span> In CLIP the positive pair is an image and its caption, collected from the internet, which puts the two modalities in one feature space. <span class="src">§17.1.1 · p. 494</span>
@@ 37
Filling the gap: the loss itself (InfoNCE)
@@ 38
The book describes the loss in words. Here it is. Take a batch of \(N\) image–caption pairs. Encode image \(i\) as a unit vector \(\mathbf{u}_i\) and caption \(j\) as a unit vector \(\mathbf{v}_j\), and let \(s_{ij} = \langle \mathbf{u}_i, \mathbf{v}_j \rangle\) be their cosine similarity. For image \(i\), treat "which of the \(N\) captions is mine?" as a classification problem and take its cross-entropy:
@@ 39
\(\tau \gt 0\) is the <strong>temperature</strong>: a small \(\tau\) makes the comparison sharper. CLIP-style training averages this over all images and adds the mirror-image term for captions choosing among images. SimCLR uses the same form with two augmented views in place of image and caption.
@@ 40
<strong>Numbers.</strong> \(N = 4\), \(\tau = 0.2\), true pair \(s_{ii} = 0.8\), three wrong captions at \(0.2\). Then \(\exp(0.8/0.2) = e^{4} = 54.60\) and \(\exp(0.2/0.2) = e = 2.718\), so
@@ 41
If all four similarities were equal, the fraction would be \(1/4\) and the loss \(\log 4 = 1.386\). That is the value for a model that has learned nothing.
@@ 42
<strong>Which way does it push?</strong> Write \(p_{ij}\) for the fraction inside the log with \(s_{ij}\) on top (a softmax over row \(i\)). Since \(\ell_i = -s_{ii}/\tau + \log \sum_j \exp(s_{ij}/\tau)\),
@@ 43
with \(\delta_{ij} = 1\) if \(i = j\) and 0 otherwise. For the true pair the derivative is \((p_{ii} - 1)/\tau \le 0\): gradient descent raises \(s_{ii}\). For a wrong pair it is \(p_{ij}/\tau \ge 0\): descent lowers \(s_{ij}\), and hardest for the wrong captions that currently look most similar.

@@ 46
Shuffle four photos and four captions on a table. The training game is to slide each photo next to its own caption and away from the other three. Nobody ever says what a "dog" is. After hundreds of millions of rounds, photos and captions about the same thing end up in the same place.

Two limits on scope. First, most methods in the chapter are pure <em>mapping</em>: they assume posed camera frames from an upstream SLAM system, so the problem becomes how to embed foundation-model features into the map. Foundation features are also used for place recognition (the book cites AnyLoc, built on DINO features), but that is not this chapter's subject. <span class="src">§17.1.2 · p. 495</span> Second, the power of these models comes from internet-scale data, and as of the book's April 2026 edition sensors such as LiDAR and radar have no readily available data at that scale. <span class="src">§17.1.1 · p. 494</span>
@@ 65
<strong>"An open-vocabulary detector gives an open-vocabulary map."</strong> Only if you could re-run it on the original images for every new query. The map itself kept just the words chosen during mapping.
@@ 66
<strong>"Unsupervised means the model learns from nothing."</strong> There is still a target: the hidden word, or the identity of the matching caption. It is made from the data instead of by an annotator.

Q1. A batch has two image–caption pairs. For image 1 the similarity to its own caption is 1.0 and to the other caption 0.0, with τ = 0.5. What is its loss?
@@ 68
The loss is −log(e² / (e² + e⁰)) = log(1 + e⁻²) = log(1.135) = 0.127. It is 0.693 = log 2 only when both similarities are equal.

Closed-set perception has a dictionary fixed at training time. Foundation models, trained on vast data, aim at open-world representations.
@@ 88

@@ 89
The contrastive loss is a cross-entropy over "which item in the batch is my partner"; its gradient raises the true pair's similarity and lowers the others in proportion to how confusing they are.
