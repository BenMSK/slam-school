1. Range from time of flight
@@ 13
A LiDAR emits a laser pulse, waits for the reflection, and measures the delay. Light travels to the surface and back, so the range is half the round trip: <span class="src">§5.1.1 · p. 120</span>
@@ 14
With \(c \approx 3\times10^{8}\) m/s, a delay of 200 ns gives \(r = 3\times10^{8} \times 200\times10^{-9} / 2 = 30\) m. Turn it around: to resolve 1 cm in range the electronics must resolve \(2 \times 0.01 / (3\times10^{8}) \approx 67\) picoseconds.
@@ 15
One beam gives one distance. Sweep an array of \(B\) beams through a full turn with \(M\) steps and you get a <strong>range image</strong> \(\mathbf{R} \in \R^{B\times M}\): one range per beam and per step. An <strong>RGB-D camera</strong> returns the same kind of data on a pixel grid, a <strong>depth map</strong> \(\mathbf{I}_D \in \R^{H\times W}\) next to the colour image. Early RGB-D cameras project an infrared pattern and read depth from its distortion, which sunlight washes out, so they are indoor sensors. <span class="src">§5.1.1 · pp. 120–121</span>

The result is a <strong>point cloud</strong> \(\mathsf{P} = \{\mathbf{p}_1, \dots, \mathbf{p}_N\}\) in the sensor frame. 3. What should a dense map store?
@@ 33
The chapter is built on three questions: which quantity to estimate, which geometric abstraction to use, and which data structure to store it in. <span class="src">§5.2 · pp. 122–123</span> For the first question there are two common answers, <strong>occupancy</strong> (today) and <strong>distance</strong> to the nearest surface (tomorrow).
@@ 34
An occupancy grid cuts space into cells \(m_k\). Each cell is either occupied or empty, we do not know which, and the map stores the probability \(p(m_k \mid \mathbf{z}_{1:t}, \mathbf{x}_{1:t})\) that it is occupied given all measurements \(\mathbf{z}_{1:t}\) and all sensor poses \(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}\). A value near 1 means occupied, near 0 means free, and 0.5 means the data say nothing. Mapping is then a binary classification problem, solved once per cell. <span class="src">§5.2.1 · pp. 123–124</span>

Worked example. Say a hit means \(p = 0.7\), so \(l = \log(0.7/0.3) = +0.847\), and a pass means \(p = 0.4\), so \(l = \log(0.4/0.6) = -0.405\). A cell that starts at \(l = 0\) and is hit three times reaches \(l = 2.542\), which is \(p = 1/(1 + e^{-2.542}) = 0.93\). Two later beams that pass through it bring it to \(l = 1.731\), \(p = 0.85\). No measurement is ever stored: the single number \(l\) summarizes all of them.

@@ 64
Two limits of the grid follow from its assumptions. Treating cells as independent ignores that walls are continuous, so a grid cannot guess the occupancy of an unobserved cell from its neighbours. And the cell size is fixed before mapping starts, so the resolution is the same everywhere. <span class="src">§5.2.1 · p. 124</span> Days 4 and 5 return to both.
@@ 65
<strong>"θ in (5.2)–(5.4) is measured from the z axis."</strong> Here it is measured from the horizontal plane, which is why \(z = r\sin\theta\). The physics convention (angle from the z axis) swaps the sine and cosine of \(\theta\).
@@ 66
<strong>"A cell at p = 0.5 is half full."</strong> It means no information. The cell is either occupied or free; the probability describes what we know.
@@ 67
<strong>"The inverse sensor model is the sensor's noise model."</strong> A noise model is \(p(\mathbf{z} \mid m)\), from world to measurement. The inverse model is \(p(m_k \mid \mathbf{z}_t)\), from measurement to world, and it is usually designed by hand.

Q2. A beam with azimuth 90° and inclination 30° returns r = 20 m. Where is the point in the sensor frame?
@@ 75
Ground distance 20 cos 30° = 17.3 m, all of it along y because the azimuth is 90°. Height z = 20 sin 30° = 10 m. The second option is what you get if you measure the inclination from the z axis.
@@ 76
Q3. A cell starts at p = 0.5. A hit means p = 0.7 and a pass means p = 0.4. After two hits and one pass, what is its occupancy probability?
@@ 77
Odds multiply: (7/3) × (7/3) × (2/3) = 3.63, so p = 3.63 / 4.63 = 0.78. In log-odds: 0.847 + 0.847 − 0.405 = 1.289. Averaging the three probabilities (0.60) is not Bayes' rule.
