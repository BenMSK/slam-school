Yesterday the FIM of a simplified pose graph turned out to be a Laplacian. So the design criteria of Day 3 become graph quantities: the determinant is the weighted number of spanning trees, and the smallest eigenvalue is tied to the algebraic connectivity. A robot can evaluate these before it moves, which is what makes them useful for planning and for pruning.

where \(S\) runs over all choices of \(n-1\) edges and \(\mathbf{A}_S\) keeps only their columns. Two facts finish the proof. If the edges in \(S\) contain a loop, adding their columns around the loop with suitable signs gives zero, so the columns are dependent and \(\det\mathbf{A}_S=0\). If they contain no loop, they form a spanning tree, and then \(\det\mathbf{A}_S=\pm 1\) (peel off a leaf of the tree: its row has a single \(\pm 1\); expand along it and repeat). So each spanning tree contributes exactly \(\prod_{e\in T}w_e\) and everything else contributes nothing.

3. Which measurement is worth adding?
@@ 36
These graph scores have two practical advantages over the FIM itself. They are cheaper: in 3-D pose-graph optimization the Laplacian has one row per pose where the FIM has six. And they need neither a SLAM solution nor actual measurements, only the list of who would observe whom. <span class="src">§6.2.2 · p. 178</span> That is the situation of <strong>active SLAM</strong>, where the robot chooses its path to maximize the accuracy it expects, and of <strong>measurement selection</strong>, where a long-running system keeps only its most informative edges.
@@ 37
Filling the gap: the value of one more edge
@@ 38
A new edge between poses \(i\) and \(j\) with weight \(w\) changes the Laplacian by a rank-one term, \(\mathbf{L}_w\to\mathbf{L}_w+w\,\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}\T\), where \(\mathbf{a}\) is the new column of the incidence matrix. where \(R_{ij}=\mathbf{a}\T\mathbf{L}_w^{-1}\mathbf{a}\) is the effective resistance between \(i\) and \(j\) in the <em>current</em> graph. In estimation terms \(R_{ij}\) is the variance per axis of the current estimate of \(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i\). So the rule is: measure what you are most uncertain about. On an 8-pose chain with unit weights, the ends are 7 edges apart, \(R_{70}=7\), and closing that loop adds \(\log 8=2.08\); poses 2 and 0 have \(R_{20}=2\), and that loop adds \(\log 3=1.10\). This is the book's "a larger loop helps more", with numbers.

4. Further reading: what works and what is open
@@ 43
Section 6.3 is a map of the literature. The certifiable approach began with 2-D SLAM, and SE-Sync gave the first fast algorithm and the first exactness guarantee under bounded noise. Certifiable algorithms now exist for rotation averaging, PGO, landmark-based, multi-robot, and range-aided SLAM, and for several front-end problems such as 3-D registration, two-view geometry, perspective-n-point, and calibration. <span class="src">§6.3 · pp. 178–179</span> The open problems fall into three groups.
@@ 44
Topic
@@ 45
What is open
@@ 46
Coverage
@@ 47
Some problems have no certifiable algorithm. In visual SLAM the perspective projection is a ratio of polynomials; it can be made polynomial with extra variables, but one per keypoint measurement is impractical. IMU models have also resisted.
@@ 48
Speed
@@ 49
Outside the problems where the Riemannian Staircase applies, the SDP needs interior-point methods (fine for small front-end problems, too slow for a back-end) or special-purpose solvers that are still slow next to local solvers. Current work shrinks the SDP or removes constraints.
@@ 50
Theory
@@ 51
Certificates are per instance: computed after solving. There is little general understanding of when a relaxation will be exact.
@@ 52
Outliers
@@ 53
Common practice wraps a certifiable solver in a graduated non-convexity loop (<a href="#d35">Week 6 · Day 5</a>), which is robust in experiments but not certified. Directly certifiable robust solvers use the moment relaxation and are slow. An alternative solves locally and only <em>checks</em> optimality afterwards.
@@ 54
pp. 179–180
@@ 55
Odometry backbone
@@ 56
Robust formulations usually trust odometry and protect only loop closures. That fails when visual tracking breaks, and in multi-robot SLAM, where no odometry links the robots. Making odometry robust too gives loose relaxations.
@@ 57
Mostly outliers
@@ 58
If most measurements are wrong, even the optimal robust estimate can be wrong. One direction returns several hypotheses with a guarantee that one is right ("list-decodable" regression).
@@ 59
Uncertainty
@@ 60
The covariance of Days 3–4 is only as good as the measurement covariances fed in, which are often guesses; some work learns them. With outliers the true spread of estimates can have several peaks, which one covariance cannot describe.
@@ 61
pp. 180–181
@@ 62
Chapter 6 asked two questions about the answer a SLAM system gives. "Is this the best answer the data allow?" can now be checked for many problems, though not yet for cameras and IMUs, and not yet fast when outliers are in the cost. "How good is the best answer?" depends on how the measurements are wired together, and you can estimate it from the wiring alone, provided you were honest about how noisy each measurement is.
@@ 63
<strong>"More spanning trees means every pose is well estimated."</strong> The determinant is an overall volume. A graph can have many trees and still one weakly attached pose; the E- and A-criteria look at that.
@@ 64
<strong>"The Laplacian scores are exact for real SLAM."</strong> They are exact for the simplified problem of Day 4. For full PGO they approximate the FIM criteria, closely under certain conditions.
@@ 65
<strong>"A certified optimum has a trustworthy covariance."</strong> The certificate concerns the cost. The covariance still rests on the noise model, and outliers or wrong noise levels break it.
