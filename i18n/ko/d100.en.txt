2. Pure translation: tilt hides in the accelerometer bias
@@ 31
The book's intuition is one sentence: if the system does not rotate, gravity can be confused with accelerometer bias. <span class="src">§11.3.3 · p. 325</span> Here is the algebra behind it.
@@ 32
Filling the gap: which rotations of the solution can the bias absorb?
@@ 33
The accelerometer model (11.1), without noise, is \(\mathbf{a}_m(t) = \mathbf{R}(t)\T\big(\mathbf{a}^w(t) - \mathbf{g}\big) + \mathbf{b}^a\). Rotate the whole solution by a fixed rotation \(\mathbf{Q}\): orientation \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}(t)\), acceleration \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{a}^w(t)\), landmarks \(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{p}_f\). The camera cannot tell, as on Day 3. The rotated solution predicts the accelerometer reading
@@ 34
This equals the actual reading for all \(t\) if and only if
@@ 35
A bias cannot change from one instant to the next, so the right-hand side has to be constant in time. There are two ways.
@@ 36
\(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{g}\): the second term vanishes. This is yaw, unobservable for every motion.
@@ 37
\(\mathbf{R}(t)\) is constant: the body never rotates. Then <em>any</em> \(\mathbf{Q}\) works, with a suitably shifted bias. All three rotations are unobservable.
@@ 38
As soon as the body rotates, \(\mathbf{R}(t)\T(\mathbf{Q}\T - \mathbf{I})\mathbf{g}\) turns with it, a real bias does not, and the two separate.
@@ 39
<strong>In numbers.</strong> For a tilt of 1° the vector \((\mathbf{Q}\T - \mathbf{I})\mathbf{g}\) has length \(9.81 \times 0.01745 \approx 0.171\ \text{m/s}^2\). So a robot that only translates cannot tell "I am tilted by 1°" from "my accelerometer bias is off by 0.17 m/s²". The two explanations fit the past equally well, but they predict different futures: once the robot does turn, a wrong choice shows up as a wrong acceleration, and \(\tfrac12 \times 0.171 \times 10^2 \approx 8.6\) m of position error after 10 s without visual correction.

Filling the gap: when can the bias absorb a change of scale?
@@ 48
Scale the whole solution by \(s\): positions, velocities, and landmarks are multiplied by \(s\), so the acceleration is \(s\,\mathbf{a}^w(t)\). Orientations do not change, and neither do bearings. The scaled solution predicts the accelerometer reading \(\mathbf{R}\T(s\,\mathbf{a}^w - \mathbf{g}) + \mathbf{b}'\). It equals the actual reading \(\mathbf{R}\T(\mathbf{a}^w - \mathbf{g}) + \mathbf{b}^a\) if and only if
@@ 49
Again the right-hand side must be constant. So scale is unobservable exactly when \(\mathbf{R}(t)\T\mathbf{a}^w(t)\), the acceleration expressed in the body frame, is constant. Zero acceleration (constant velocity) is the simplest case. Differentiating the scaled solution with respect to \(s\) at \(s = 1\) gives the extra null vector, in the state order of (11.37):
@@ 50
These are the four blocks the book names when it says the scale of "position, velocity, acceleration bias, and features" is lost. <span class="src">§11.3.3 · p. 325</span>
@@ 51
The book's table says "constant acceleration" without naming a frame. This derivation says the frame is the body frame. If the body does not rotate, the two readings agree. If it does, the lab below lets you check that it is the body-frame acceleration that counts.
@@ 52
<strong>How the scale is recovered when it can be.</strong> Suppose the camera gives the path as \(\mathbf{q}(t)\) in unknown units, so \(\mathbf{p}(t) = s\,\mathbf{q}(t)\). For a body that is not rotating, the accelerometer reads \(s\,\ddot{\mathbf{q}}(t)\) plus a constant (gravity and bias). The constant cancels in a difference between two instants. If \(\ddot{\mathbf{q}}\) changes by 0.5 units/s² while the accelerometer reading changes by 1.0 m/s², then \(s = 1.0/0.5 = 2\) metres per unit. With constant acceleration the difference is \(0 = s \cdot 0\), which holds for every \(s\).

4. No parallax: pure rotation, and driving at a feature
@@ 54
A moving camera measures depth through <strong>parallax</strong>: the change in a point's bearing caused by the camera's sideways displacement. For a displacement \(b_\perp\) perpendicular to the ray and a depth \(\rho\), the bearing changes by about
@@ 55
If the camera only rotates, \(b_\perp = 0\) for every point. If it drives straight at a point, \(b_\perp = 0\) for that point. In both cases \(\Delta\beta = 0\) whatever \(\rho\) is, so the depth is unconstrained. This is \(\mathbf{H}_b\mathbf{p}^c_f = \mathbf{0}\) from Day 2, with no second viewpoint to break the tie.
@@ 56
The book distinguishes two kinds of loss. <span class="src">§11.3.3 · pp. 325–326</span>
@@ 57
<strong>System scale</strong> (row 2): one number rescales the trajectory, the velocity, the accelerometer bias, and all features together.
@@ 58
<strong>Feature scale</strong> (rows 3 and 4): only the depths of the features are free. With three landmarks and no translation, that is three separate free depths.
@@ 59
One condition is attached to rows 2 to 4: the features must be much farther away than the offset between camera and IMU, \(\norm{\mathbf{p}^c_f} \gg \norm{\mathbf{p}^c_b}\). <span class="src">§11.3.3 · p. 326</span> The reason is that a camera mounted away from the IMU swings on a small arc when the body rotates, which creates a little parallax. With a 5 cm offset and features 5 m away the effect is of order 1%, too small to be useful.

@@ 85
A bias is a constant that the estimator is allowed to choose. Anything that would change the accelerometer reading by a constant can therefore be explained away. A tilt changes it by a constant if the body never turns. A change of scale changes it by a constant if the acceleration never changes. Give the estimator something that varies, a turn or a change of acceleration, and the bias can no longer cover for it.

6. Why this matters in practice
@@ 87
The book notes that degenerate motions "might lead to large errors in some directions of the state space" and to navigation failures, and that they should be avoided or flagged. <span class="src">§11.3.3 · p. 325; §11.3 · p. 320</span> Real motion is rarely exactly degenerate, but it is often close: a vehicle cruising in a straight line has nearly constant velocity and nearly no rotation. Close to degenerate means an eigenvalue that is small instead of zero, and the variance along that direction is proportional to one over the eigenvalue. The estimate of scale or tilt is then formally defined and practically useless.
@@ 88
The opening of §11.4 states the positive side. Camera and IMU are complementary: the IMU captures fast accelerations and rotations, the camera observes the surroundings and removes most of the inertial drift, and the IMU in turn makes the scale of a monocular system observable, provided the motion is not degenerate. A system built from cameras and an IMU is called <strong>visual-inertial odometry (VIO)</strong>, and a visual-inertial SLAM system once loop closures are added. <span class="src">§11.4 · p. 326</span>
@@ 89
<strong>"An IMU always gives a monocular system its scale."</strong> Only when the acceleration changes. At constant velocity the accelerometer reads gravity plus bias and nothing else.
@@ 90
<strong>"Constant velocity is safe because nothing is accelerating."</strong> It is the special case \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{0}\) of row 2, and without rotation it is also row 1.
@@ 91
<strong>"Pure rotation loses the scale of the trajectory."</strong> There is no trajectory to scale: the position does not change. What is lost is the depth of each feature.
@@ 92
<strong>"A degenerate motion ruins the whole estimate."</strong> It adds specific directions to the nullspace. Bearings, relative orientation, and the shape of the path are still determined.
