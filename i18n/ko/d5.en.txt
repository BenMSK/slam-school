A solver never computes \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\). It factors a matrix into triangular or orthogonal pieces and then substitutes. <strong>Cholesky</strong> factors the information matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\). <strong>QR</strong> factors \(\mathbf{A}\) itself and is more accurate. The <strong>SVD</strong> tells you, through the condition number, how much accuracy there is to lose.

@@ 34

The first says that the \(\mathbf{R}\) of QR is the Cholesky factor of \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) (up to the signs of its rows), which is the book's equation (1.33). The book states that QR is more accurate and more stable than Cholesky on the normal equations, and about twice as slow when there are many more rows than columns. <span class="src">§1.3.3 · pp. 31–32</span> Section 4 shows where the accuracy comes from.

4. The SVD and the condition number
@@ 47
The <strong>singular value decomposition (SVD)</strong> writes any \(m\times n\) matrix as \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{V}\T\), where \(\mathbf{U}\) and \(\mathbf{V}\) have orthonormal columns and \(\mathbf{S} = \diag(s_1,\dots,s_n)\) holds the <strong>singular values</strong> \(s_1\ge s_2\ge\dots\ge s_n\ge 0\). (Most texts call the middle matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\); here that letter is taken by covariances.) In words: \(\mathbf{A}\) rotates, stretches each axis by \(s_i\), and rotates again. The number of non-zero singular values is the <strong>rank</strong>. The ratio of the largest stretch to the smallest is the <strong>condition number</strong>:

Filling the gap: why κ is the error amplifier
@@ 55
Take a square system \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) and disturb the right-hand side by \(\delta\mathbf{b}\). The solution moves by \(\delta\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\delta\mathbf{b}\). The inverse stretches by at most \(1/s_{\min}\), so \(\norm{\delta\mathbf{x}} \le \norm{\delta\mathbf{b}}/s_{\min}\). And \(\mathbf{A}\) stretches by at most \(s_{\max}\), so \(\norm{\mathbf{b}} = \norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}} \le s_{\max}\norm{\mathbf{x}}\), that is \(1/\norm{\mathbf{x}} \le s_{\max}/\norm{\mathbf{b}}\). @@ 56
A relative error in the data can grow by a factor of up to \(\kappa\) in the answer. Computer arithmetic makes relative errors of about \(10^{-16}\) in double precision and \(10^{-7}\) in single precision at every step, so as a rule of thumb you lose about \(\log_{10}\kappa\) of your digits.
@@ 57

@@ 58

<strong>"A tiny determinant means the matrix is ill-conditioned."</strong> The matrix \(0.001\,\mathbf{I}\) has a tiny determinant and κ = 1. Conditioning is about the ratio of singular values, not their size.
@@ 87
<strong>"QR and Cholesky give different answers."</strong> In exact arithmetic they give the same \(\mathbf{R}\) and the same \(\mathbf{x}\). They differ only in how rounding error enters.
@@ 88
<strong>"Ill-conditioning is a bug in the solver."</strong> It is a property of the problem: some direction of \(\mathbf{x}\) is barely measured. A better solver loses fewer digits, but only better measurements fix it.
