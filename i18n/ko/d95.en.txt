1. When the bias estimate changes
@@ 14
The increments \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}, \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}, \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}\) were computed by subtracting a bias estimate \(\bar{\mathbf{b}}_i = \{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g_i, \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a_i\}\) from every sample. The biases are unknowns of the optimization, so the solver will revise them: \(\mathbf{b} \leftarrow \bar{\mathbf{b}} + \delta\mathbf{b}\). Re-integrating would bring back the cost preintegration was meant to remove. The book instead expands to first order in \(\delta\mathbf{b}\): <span class="src">§11.2.2.3 · pp. 314–315</span>
@@ 15
The vectors are corrected by adding. The rotation is corrected by multiplying with a small rotation on the right, because rotations do not form a vector space (<a href="#d21">Week 4 · Day 3</a>). The five Jacobians are evaluated at \(\bar{\mathbf{b}}_i\), computed once during preintegration, and then stay constant. The book gives no formulas for them and refers to the original paper. <span class="src">§11.2.2.3 · p. 315</span>

If the body does not rotate these are \(-\Delta t_{ij}\mathbf{I}\) and \(-\tfrac12\Delta t_{ij}^2\mathbf{I}\): a bias of \(\delta b\) removes \(\delta b\,\Delta t_{ij}\) of velocity and \(\tfrac12\delta b\,\Delta t_{ij}^2\) of position, as for any constant acceleration.

<strong>Gyroscope bias, in the plane.</strong> The angle increment is \(\Delta\tilde\theta_{ij} = \sum_k(\tilde\omega_k - b^g)\Delta t\), so \(\partial\Delta\theta_{ij}/\partial b^g = -\Delta t_{ij}\), again exact. But a changed gyroscope bias also changes every \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\) that rotates an accelerometer sample. This one is a true first-order approximation, since \(\mathbf{R}(\theta)\) is not linear in \(\theta\). The neglected part is proportional to \((\delta b^g)^2\).

<strong>In 3-D</strong> (Forster et al.) the structure is the same: \(\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}/\partial\mathbf{b}^g = -\sum_k\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{k+1\,j}\T\mathbf{J}^k_r\Delta t\), which is (11.28) with the bias error playing the role of a noise that is the same in every sample, and \(\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}/\partial\mathbf{b}^g = -\sum_k\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a)^\wedge\,\tfrac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^g}\Delta t\). 2. The preintegrated IMU factor
@@ 25
Yesterday's model (11.23) says "computed increment = function of states, plus Gaussian noise with covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}\)". As in <a href="#d8">Week 2 · Day 2</a>, the residual is the difference between the two sides, with the bias-corrected increments of (11.30) in place of the raw ones: <span class="src">§11.2.2.4 · p. 315</span>
@@ 26
Stacked, they form \(\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{I}_{ij}} \in \R^9\), and the factor adds \(\norm{\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{I}_{ij}}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}} = \mathbf{r}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\) to the least-squares objective. The rotation residual is the rotation vector of "measured increment inverse times predicted increment": zero when the states reproduce the measurement. The other two are plain differences. The factor touches the rotation, position, and velocity at both keyframes and the bias at \(i\).
@@ 27
A planar number: Day 4's widget stored \(\Delta\tilde\theta_{ij} = 19.90^\circ\) with \(\sigma = 0.115^\circ\). If the current states say the robot turned \(\theta_j - \theta_i = 20.50^\circ\), the residual is 0.60°, which is 5.2σ, and it adds \(5.2^2 \approx 27\) to the cost. The optimizer will pull the two orientations toward a 19.9° difference, or shift the gyroscope bias to explain the gap.

3. A model for the bias itself
@@ 37
Inside one interval the bias was treated as constant (11.15). Across intervals it is allowed to move, slowly. The book models it as integrated white noise: <span class="src">§11.2.2.4 · pp. 315–316</span>
@@ 38
where the discrete noises have covariances \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{bgd} = \Delta t_{ij}\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{bg})\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{bad} = \Delta t_{ij}\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{ba})\). This is the random walk of <a href="#d92">Day 2</a>: the variance of the change grows in proportion to the elapsed time. Compare with the sample noise, whose covariance is <em>divided</em> by \(\Delta t\). The corresponding factor penalizes bias changes between consecutive keyframes:
@@ 39
Example: a gyroscope bias random walk of \(2\times10^{-5}\) rad/s²/√Hz and keyframes 0.25 s apart give a standard deviation of \(2\times10^{-5}\times\sqrt{0.25} = 1\times10^{-5}\) rad/s for the change between keyframes. A proposed jump of \(10^{-4}\) rad/s would be a 10σ event and cost 100. Without this factor each keyframe's bias could absorb any disagreement between IMU and camera. With it, the bias has to be a slowly varying curve, which is what makes it estimable.

Filling the gap: first order against second order
@@ 56
Over one step the rectangle rule replaces \(a(t)\) by \(a(t_k)\). By Taylor expansion the true integral is \(a(t_k)\Delta t + \tfrac12\dot a(t_k)\Delta t^2 + \dots\), so each step is wrong by about \(\tfrac12\dot a\,\Delta t^2\). There are \(T/\Delta t\) steps, so the accumulated error is of order \(\Delta t\): halve the step, halve the error. The trapezoid rule, which is what integrating a piecewise-linear signal amounts to, cancels that term and leaves a per-step error of order \(\Delta t^3\), hence an accumulated error of order \(\Delta t^2\): halve the step, quarter the error.
@@ 57
The methods the book surveys, in brief: <span class="src">§11.2.3 · pp. 316–319</span>
@@ 58
<strong>Upsampling.</strong> Fit a Gaussian process (<a href="#d27">Week 5 · Day 3</a>) to the samples and query it at a higher rate, then integrate with the rectangle rule as before.
@@ 59
<strong>Closed-form integration under a motion assumption.</strong> Standard preintegration assumes the acceleration is constant in the world frame during a step. Assuming it constant in the body frame ("local" acceleration) matches real motion better; the book quotes about 5% better visual-inertial odometry accuracy on the EuRoC dataset.
@@ 60
<strong>Continuous acceleration.</strong> Represent the rotation-corrected samples \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_k = \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k\) by a continuous function and integrate it analytically: piecewise-linear gives the trapezoid rule, and a Gaussian process with a squared-exponential kernel can be integrated exactly because integration is a linear operation.
@@ 61
<strong>Continuous rotation.</strong> The hard part. The exact solution of \(\dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\omega}^\wedge\) is an infinite product of small rotations (11.35), with no general closed form because rotations do not commute. One approach works on the rotation vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), with \(\mathbf{R} = \Exp(\mathbf{r})\), whose dynamics are \(\dot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{J}_r(\mathbf{r})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}^b_b\) (11.36), and fits a Gaussian process to \(\dot{\mathbf{r}}\) using the gyroscope readings. The book reports accuracy gains of at least an order of magnitude over the standard discrete scheme. <span class="src">§11.2.3.3 · p. 319</span>
@@ 62
Rotation errors matter more than they look, for the reason found on Day 3. Go back to <a href="#d93">the dead-reckoning widget</a>, set every bias and noise slider to zero, and switch the gyroscope output to "instantaneous sample": the error after 20 s jumps from 6 cm to 2.8 m. Nothing is wrong with the sensor. The rectangle rule on the pitch rate leaves a tilt error of about a tenth of a degree while the vehicle is on the hills, and gravity does the rest.
@@ 63
<strong>"After a bias update the preintegration must be redone."</strong> For small updates the stored Jacobians correct it to first order. A full re-integration is only worth its cost once the bias has moved so far from \(\bar{\mathbf{b}}_i\) that the neglected second-order term matters.
@@ 64
<strong>"The bias covariance shrinks with longer intervals, like averaging."</strong> It grows: \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{bgd} = \Delta t_{ij}\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{bg})\). A random walk wanders further given more time.
@@ 65
<strong>"The IMU factor constrains absolute position."</strong> It constrains how the state at \(j\) relates to the state at \(i\). Absolute position and yaw must come from elsewhere, which is next week's topic.

Q4. The sampling rate of a smooth acceleration signal is raised four-fold. By roughly what factor does the error of piecewise-linear (trapezoid) integration fall?
@@ 81
The trapezoid rule is second order: error proportional to Δt². A step four times smaller gives an error 16 times smaller. The rectangle rule would improve only four-fold.
