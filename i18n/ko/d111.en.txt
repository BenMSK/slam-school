Asking a network for the pose makes it relearn geometry. Asking it for matches lets it do what networks are good at, recognizing that two image patches show the same thing, and leaves the geometry to the factor graph. RAFT is the cleanest example: it compares every pixel of one image with every pixel of the other once, stores the result, and then refines its flow estimate by looking up that table again and again.

1. Predict the measurements, not the state
@@ 13
In the notation of Part I, SLAM estimates the state \(\mathbf{x}\) (poses, depths) from measurements \(\mathbf{z}\) (for example feature matches) by solving for \(\mathbf{x}^{\text{MAP}}\). Yesterday's networks went from images straight to \(\mathbf{x}\) in one forward pass. To do that well, a network has to learn multi-view geometry from scratch: how depth, pose, and intrinsics relate. The alternative is to let the network predict \(\mathbf{z}\) and keep the optimization. <span class="src">§13.2 · p. 374</span>
@@ 14
Finding the same corner in two photos is a recognition problem. Working out how the camera moved from a set of matched corners is a geometry problem with a known solution. Give each job to whoever is good at it.

3. Optical flow, and RAFT's correlation volume
@@ 42
<strong>Optical flow</strong> is the per-pixel motion between two frames: a vector \(\mathbf{f}(u, v)\) for every pixel, saying where that pixel went. It is dense matching, and the flow field can serve as measurements in a factor graph. Classical methods computed it by minimizing a data term (matched regions should look alike) plus a regularization term (the motion field should be plausible). <span class="src">§13.2.3 · pp. 375–376</span>
@@ 43
<strong>RAFT</strong> (Recurrent All-Pairs Field Transforms) is the learned method the book describes, because DROID-SLAM is built on it. It has three parts. <span class="src">§13.2.3 · pp. 376–378 · Fig. 13.7</span>

4. Lookup and update: the iteration
@@ 64
RAFT keeps a flow estimate \(\mathbf{f}\), starting from zero. One iteration does two things. <span class="src">§13.2.3 · pp. 377–378 · Fig. 13.8</span>
@@ 65
<strong>Lookup.</strong> For pixel \((u, v)\) of image 1, the current guess of its match in image 2 is \((u', v') = \mathbf{f}(u, v) + (u, v)\). RAFT reads the correlation volume on a small grid around that guess,
@@ 66
with bilinear interpolation, since \((u', v')\) is not a whole pixel. It does the same at each coarser level around the rescaled position \((u'/2^l, v'/2^l)\), and concatenates everything into one feature vector. The fine level tells it about small displacements, and the coarse levels about large ones.
@@ 67
In one dimension: pixel \(u = 10\) with current flow \(3.4\) has \(u' = 13.4\). With \(r = 2\) the lookup reads positions \(11.4, 12.4, 13.4, 14.4, 15.4\) of row 10 of \(\mathbf{C}^0\); for instance the middle one is \(0.6\,\mathbf{C}^0[10][13] + 0.4\,\mathbf{C}^0[10][14]\). At level 1 it reads five positions around \(13.4/2 = 6.7\), which together span about 10 pixels of the original row. With \(r = 3\) in two dimensions, a lookup reads \(7 \times 7 = 49\) values per level, 196 in total, out of the millions stored.
@@ 68
<strong>Update.</strong> A <strong>convolutional GRU</strong> (a gated recurrent unit whose linear layers are replaced by convolutions) takes four inputs, each a 2-D feature map: its hidden state from the previous iteration, the context features, the current flow, and the correlation features just looked up. It outputs a new hidden state and a flow update: <span class="src">p. 378</span>
@@ 69
The same weights are used in every iteration (<strong>weight tying</strong>). The book notes that tied weights and bounded activations encourage convergence to a fixed point, and that the convolutions pass information between neighbouring pixels. RAFT is trained on synthetic data with ground-truth flow and generalizes well to real images. <span class="src">pp. 376, 378</span>

Try it: press Step a few times, or Play. Then set "Pyramid levels" to 1 and run again.
@@ 71
Notice that with the starting settings (true shift 5 px, radius 2, three levels) the mean flow error goes 5.00 → 0.89 → 0.10 px in two iterations and stays near 0.1. The finest window (pink) cannot reach a match 5 px away, but the coarse levels (amber) can, and they pull the estimate in until the fine level takes over. With one level, the error is still about 3 px after 12 iterations: most pixels never find their match. The update rule in this widget is a fixed formula (a soft-argmax of the values read, then smoothing over neighbouring pixels), where RAFT has a learned Conv-GRU. The correlation volume, pyramid, and lookup follow the equations above.
@@ 72
RAFT behaves like an optimizer whose update rule was learned. Compare it with Gauss-Newton (<a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a>): keep an estimate, look at the local evidence around it, compute a correction, repeat. The book puts it this way: RAFT combines deep learning with traditional first-order optimization. <span class="src">p. 376</span> That design is what allows DROID-SLAM, tomorrow, to put a real Gauss-Newton step inside the same loop.
@@ 73
<strong>"The correlation volume is recomputed in each iteration."</strong> It is built once. Iterations only index into it, which is why a dozen of them are affordable.
@@ 74
<strong>"The pyramid shrinks both images."</strong> Only the last two dimensions, those of image 2, are pooled. Each pixel of image 1 keeps its own full-resolution entry.
@@ 75
<strong>"The lookup returns the best match."</strong> It returns raw correlation values around the current guess. Deciding which way to move is the update operator's job.
@@ 76
<strong>"Sparse matchers are obsolete."</strong> The book lists the trade: keypoints reduce computation; dense matching helps where texture is limited. <span class="src">p. 375</span>

Q1. RAFT's feature maps are 40 × 50. How many entries does the level-0 correlation volume have?
@@ 78
There are 40 · 50 = 2000 pixels in each feature map, and the volume holds one number for every pair: 2000² = 4,000,000.

Q2. In a 1-D version, pixel u = 20 has current flow −2.5 and the lookup radius is r = 1. Which positions of image 2 are read at level 0?
@@ 80
The guess is u′ = 20 − 2.5 = 17.5, and the neighbourhood is u′ + Δu for Δu in {−1, 0, 1}. The values are read by interpolation.
