A moving object in view can be handled at three levels. Throw its measurements away and localize on the rest. Or keep them and add the object to the factor graph as a rigid body with its own pose and velocity at every time step. Or build a dense model of it.
@@ 10
1. Three levels of ambition
@@ 11
The first methods found the measurements explained by the sensor's own motion, with statistical tests, RANSAC, or the Hough transform, and discarded the rest as outliers. Later work also filled in what the moving object had hidden, because a map without holes localizes better. The alternative is to <strong>track</strong> the objects inside SLAM, which computer vision calls <strong>multi-body structure from motion</strong>; it was first judged too expensive, has recently become feasible, and may be needed to avoid collisions. Beyond tracking lies dense <strong>reconstruction</strong> of the objects, which manipulation needs. <span class="src">§15.2 · pp. 423–424</span>

2. Removing dynamic objects
@@ 13
Discarding is simple and still effective: the persistent parts of the scene are usually what the map is for, and geometric estimation assumes rigidity, so moving parts show up as measurements that do not fit a rigid model. The book groups the cues into six families; current systems combine several. <span class="src">§15.2.1 · pp. 424–426</span>

@@ 36
Tracking objects alongside SLAM is also called <strong>multi-object tracking (MOT)</strong>. The central modelling decision is that <em>each dynamic object is a rigid moving body</em>. The front-end extracts features as before and uses semantic masks to assign points to objects. <span class="src">§15.2.2 · pp. 426–427</span>

Filling the gap: equations (15.3) and (15.4) with numbers
@@ 48
<strong>Why the increment multiplies on the right.</strong> The velocities are expressed in the object frame, and a motion described in a body's own frame is applied after the current pose: the predicted pose is \(\mathbf{T}^w_{k,i}\, \Delta\mathbf{T}\).
@@ 49
<strong>How to read \(h^{-1}\).</strong> Both \(\mathbf{T}^w_{k,i+1}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\) and \(\mathbf{T}^w_{k,i}\Delta\mathbf{T}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\) are homogeneous points with last entry 1, so their difference has last entry 0. Read (15.3) as the difference of the two Euclidean points: a 3-vector, zero when the pose estimate and the motion model agree.

Notice: with the default settings the measurements are off by 0.36 m (RMS) and the estimate by 0.19 m, and through the six hidden steps the estimate stays within 0.10 m of the truth with no measurement there. Set the allowed velocity change to 0.02 m/s: the estimate straightens, and its error (0.43 m) is worse than the raw measurements. 4. Dense dynamic SLAM
@@ 57
Dense dynamic SLAM also wants a dense reconstruction that includes the moving things. Most systems split the work into a <strong>localization step</strong>, which tracks the robot against the static background with the methods above, and a <strong>mapping step</strong>, which represents the background and the dynamic entities. <span class="src">§15.2.3 · p. 428</span>
@@ 58
Notes from the book
@@ 59
<strong>Moving object segmentation (MOS)</strong>
@@ 60
Label every point or pixel as moving or not; ignore the moving ones or keep them as a low-level record
@@ 61
Detection must be dense. Geometric cues: tracking residuals (StaticFusion) or consistency with the fused map (Dynablox). Semantic cues: mask classes such as people. Learned classifiers may not generalize out of domain.
@@ 62
<strong>Parametric models</strong>
@@ 63
If the kind of object is known, estimate the parameters of a shape model as factor-graph variables
@@ 64
Used most for people: skeletons or SMPL meshes
@@ 65
<strong>Simultaneous tracking and reconstruction</strong>
@@ 66
A separate dense model per moving object, usually assumed rigid, plus one for the background
@@ 67
Needs no prior model. Objects are mostly TSDF volumes (<a href="#d44">Week 8 · Day 2</a>).
@@ 68
Simultaneous tracking and reconstruction mostly follows four steps per image: detect the objects (segmentation refined by geometry and motion cues), track the camera against the background, track each object against the current image (usually with direct methods), and fuse each object's pixels into its own model. <span class="src">§15.2.3.3 · pp. 430–431</span> The price is computation, and a feedback loop: a tracking error fuses misaligned measurements into the object's model, and the worse model degrades later tracking. The rigid assumption can be relaxed to articulated and deformable objects (Day 5). Finally, the reconstructed objects improve the robot's own pose only in corner cases, for example when more than 70% of the view is occluded and good motion models of the objects exist. <span class="src">§15.2.3.3 · pp. 431–432</span>
@@ 69
<strong>"Mask every car and person and the problem is solved."</strong> A parked car is a good landmark, and a closed-set network does not know every movable thing. Semantics says what could move; geometry says what did.

Moving objects can be removed, tracked, or reconstructed, depending on what the task needs.
@@ 91
Removal uses six kinds of cue: proprioception, robust estimation, multi-view residuals, semantics, domain knowledge, motion-aware sensors. Each has a failure case, so systems combine them.
@@ 92
Tracking models each object as a rigid body: a pose and velocity per time step, points fixed in the object frame, and the factors (15.1), (15.2), and (15.3)–(15.4).
@@ 93
Dense dynamic SLAM localizes against the background and maps background and objects, by segmentation, parametric models, or per-object dense models.
