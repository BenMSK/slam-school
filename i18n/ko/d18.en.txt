Week 3 review: sparsity, elimination, and the Bayes tree
@@ 3
After today you can go from a factor graph to its matrix patterns, eliminate it by hand into a Bayes net and a Bayes tree, marginalize with the Schur complement, and say what an incremental update has to recompute.
@@ 4
A SLAM least-squares problem is sparse because each factor touches few variables: factors are block-rows of \(\mathbf{A}\), variables are block-columns, and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) has a nonzero block only between variables that share a factor. Variable elimination removes one variable at a time, leaving a conditional (a row of \(\mathbf{R}\)) and a new factor on its neighbours; with Gaussian factors this is sparse QR or Cholesky, and the result is a Bayes net solved by back-substitution. Removing a variable joins its neighbours, which is fill-in on the graph and the Schur complement in the matrix, so the ordering decides the cost. Grouping the conditionals into cliques gives the Bayes tree, in which a new factor affects only the path from its variables to the root. iSAM2 uses that to update the solution incrementally.

Q4. Eliminate x from the rows "x = 1" and "x − y = 0" (unit noise). What is the conditional mean of x given y?
@@ 39
The x column is (1, 1), so R = √2 and the first row is √2·x − y/√2 = 1/√2. Dividing by √2 gives x = (1 + y)/2, the average of the two things said about x.

Q5. G is a ring a – b – c – d – a. How many fill-in edges does eliminating a first create?
@@ 41
The neighbours of a are b and d, which are not joined. Eliminating a adds the edge b – d. After that the rest is a triangle and needs nothing more.

@@ 43
Conditioning in covariance form is a Schur complement: 2 − 1·(1/2)·1 = 1.5. The marginal variance, 2, is read off directly.

Two scalars with three unit-noise factors: \(x = 0\), \(y - x = 1\), \(y = 1.6\). Eliminate \(x\), then \(y\), by QR, and back-substitute. Say in words what the new factor on \(y\) means.
@@ 58
The factors touching \(x\) are the first two. With columns \((x, y \mid b)\) the stacked rows are \((1, 0 \mid 0)\) and \((-1, 1 \mid 1)\). The \(x\) column is \((1, -1)\), so use \(\mathbf{q}_1 = (1, -1)/\sqrt2\) and \(\mathbf{q}_2 = (1, 1)/\sqrt2\):
@@ 59
The first row is the conditional: \(x = (y - 1)/2\), precision 2. The second is the new factor \(\tau(y)\): \(y = 1\) with variance 2. In words, "the prior on \(x\) plus one odometry step puts \(y\) at 1", and the two unit variances add.
@@ 60
Now \(y\) has the rows \((0.707 \mid 0.707)\) and \((1 \mid 1.6)\). \(R = \sqrt{0.5 + 1} = 1.225\) and \(d = (0.707 \times 0.707 + 1 \times 1.6)/1.225 = 1.714\).
@@ 61
Back-substitution: \(y^* = 1.714/1.225 = 1.4\), then \(x^* = (1.4 - 1)/2 = 0.2\). Check with the normal equations: \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b} = (-1, 2.6)\), and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1} = \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) gives \((0.2, 1.4)\).

(a) Marginalize \(x_2\) out and give the information matrix of \((x_1, x_3)\). (b) Condition on \(x_2\) instead. (c) Given that \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), check (a) and compare the variances of \(x_3\) in (a) and (b).
@@ 63
(a) Schur complement with \(a = x_2\): \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{aa} = 2\), and \(x_2\) is linked to \(x_1\) and \(x_3\) by \(-1\) each, so subtract \(\tfrac12\) in all four positions:
@@ 64
The zero between \(x_1\) and \(x_3\) has become \(-0.5\): fill-in.
@@ 65
(b) Delete the row and column of \(x_2\): \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Given \(x_2\), the other two are independent.
@@ 66
(c) The marginal covariance is the block \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), with determinant 2 and inverse \(\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), which is the matrix of (a). The variance of \(x_3\) is 3 in the marginal and \(1/1 = 1\) in the conditional: knowing \(x_2\) removes the uncertainty accumulated along the chain before it.

A robot has poses \(x_1, \dots, x_4\) with a prior on \(x_1\) and odometry between consecutive poses, and a landmark \(l\) seen from \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). (a) Eliminate in the order \(l, x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4\) and give the separators. (b) Build the Bayes tree. (c) Which cliques does a loop closure between \(x_4\) and \(x_1\) affect? (d) Instead, a new pose \(x_5\) arrives with odometry from \(x_4\). Update the tree, eliminating the affected variables in the order \(x_3, x_4, x_5\).
@@ 68
(a) \(l\): its factors involve \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), separator \(\{x_1, x_2\}\) (already joined by odometry, no fill-in). \(x_1\): prior, odometry and the new factor, separator \(\{x_2\}\). \(x_2\): \(\{x_3\}\). \(x_3\): \(\{x_4\}\). \(x_4\): empty.
@@ 69
(b) In reverse order: \(x_4\) starts the root. \(x_3 \mid x_4\) joins it: root \(x_3, x_4\). \(x_2 \mid x_3\): the root's variables \(\{x_3, x_4\}\) differ from \(\{x_3\}\), so a new child \(x_2 : x_3\). \(x_1 \mid x_2\): the clique of \(x_2\) has \(\{x_2, x_3\}\), which differs from \(\{x_2\}\), so a new child \(x_1 : x_2\). \(l \mid x_1, x_2\): the clique of \(x_1\) has exactly \(\{x_1, x_2\}\), so \(l\) joins it. The tree is a chain: root \(x_3, x_4\), then \(x_2 : x_3\), then \(l, x_1 : x_2\).
@@ 70
(c) \(x_1\) is frontal in the leaf and \(x_4\) in the root. The path from the leaf to the root is the whole tree, so all three cliques and all five variables are redone.
@@ 71
(d) \(x_4\) is frontal in the root, so only the root is detached. It becomes a factor on \((x_3, x_4)\); add the new factor on \((x_4, x_5)\). Eliminate \(x_3\): separator \(\{x_4\}\). \(x_4\): separator \(\{x_5\}\). \(x_5\): empty. Tree: \(x_5\) starts the root, \(x_4\) joins it, and \(x_3 \mid x_4\) becomes a child because \(\{x_4, x_5\} \neq \{x_4\}\). Re-attach the orphan \(x_2 : x_3\) under the clique where \(x_3\) is frontal. Result: root \(x_4, x_5\), then \(x_3 : x_4\), then \(x_2 : x_3\), then \(l, x_1 : x_2\). Three of six variables were re-eliminated, and the lower two cliques were not touched.
