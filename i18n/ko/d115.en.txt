@@ 10
A map does not have to be assembled by hand-designed fusion rules. If an image can be rendered from the map by a procedure whose output changes smoothly when the map's parameters change, then the difference between the rendered image and a real photo has a gradient with respect to those parameters, and following it improves the map. Neural radiance fields (NeRF) and 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) are two map representations built for that loop.
@@ 11
1. From stored geometry to learned functions
@@ 12
The maps of <a href="#d44">Week 8 · Day 2</a> store geometry directly: points, or a grid of occupancy values. When deep learning moved from images to 3D, the first attempts fed those structures to networks. The book names the problem: they are discrete, and they struggle to describe a closed, continuous surface of arbitrary shape compactly, which gets in the way of gradient-based optimization. <span class="src">§14.1.1 · p. 398</span>
@@ 13
The alternative is to let the shape <em>be a function</em>. A <strong>multi-layer perceptron (MLP)</strong> is a small neural network: a stack of layers, each of which multiplies its input by a matrix, adds a vector, and applies a simple nonlinearity. Its matrices and vectors are the <strong>weights</strong>. Feed it a 3D coordinate and train it to output one number: either <strong>occupancy</strong> (is this point inside something?) or the <strong>signed distance function (SDF)</strong>, the distance to the nearest surface with a sign that says inside or outside. The surface is the set of points where the SDF is zero. DeepSDF does exactly this with an 8-layer MLP, optionally with an extra input vector (a latent code) that selects which shape is meant. <span class="src">§14.1.1 · p. 398 · Fig. 14.1</span>
@@ 14
A hand-written example of what such a network has to learn: for a sphere of radius 1 centred at the origin, \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \norm{\mathbf{x}} - 1\). Then \(f(0.5, 0, 0) = -0.5\) (inside), \(f(2, 0, 0) = 1\) (outside), and \(f(0.6, 0.8, 0) = 0\) (on the surface). A trained MLP plays the role of \(f\) for shapes with no formula.

Numbers: \(c = 0.9\), \(b = 0.1\), \(\alpha = 0.5\) give \(C = 0.5\). The photo says \(C_{gt} = 0.7\), so \(L = 0.04\) and \(\partial L/\partial\alpha = 2(-0.2)(0.8) = -0.32\). Early differentiable renderers softened rasterization for meshes. Volume rendering then took over as the stronger tool for gradient-based optimization, because it provides gradients over 3D space even before the exact surface position is known. <span class="src">§14.1.2 · p. 399</span>

Filling the gap: why "gradients before the surface is found" matters
@@ 67
Put two samples on one ray, with opacities \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2\) and grey levels \(c_1, c_2\), over a black background. Light from sample 2 must pass sample 1, so
@@ 68
Suppose the current scene is wrong: sample 1 is empty (\(\alpha_1 = 0\)) and sample 2 is a solid dark wall (\(\alpha_2 = 1\), \(c_2 = 0.2\)), so \(C = 0.2\). The photo shows a bright object, \(C_{gt} = 0.8\), and sample 1 has colour \(c_1 = 0.8\). Then \(\partial C/\partial\alpha_1 = 0.8 - 0.2 = 0.6\) and \(\partial L/\partial\alpha_1 = 2(0.2 - 0.8)(0.6) = -0.72\). The derivative is not zero even though nothing is there yet: the loss asks for matter at an empty point.
@@ 69
A hard surface gives no such signal. If a pixel sees an opaque surface, sliding that surface slightly along the ray does not change the pixel at all, so the derivative with respect to the surface position is zero, except at object outlines where the pixel jumps. That is why mesh-based methods had to soften their rasterizers, and why volumes are easier to optimize.

4. NeRF and 3DGS at a glance
@@ 71
<strong>NeRF</strong> (2020) stores the scene in a single MLP and renders by ray marching: for each pixel it steps along the ray and asks the MLP for opacity and colour at each sample. The MLP takes a 3D location and a viewing direction, passed through a positional encoding so that it can represent fine detail. It is trained from <strong>posed</strong> colour images (images whose camera poses are already known) by minimizing the rendering loss, and it produces high-quality <strong>novel view synthesis</strong>: images from viewpoints that were never photographed. <span class="src">§14.1.3 · pp. 399–400</span>
@@ 72
NeRF's bottleneck is training speed. Later work replaced or assisted the MLP with explicit volume structures, voxel grids and hash tables, and reached comparable quality without an MLP. The book draws the lesson that the key ingredient is differentiable volume rendering and not the network. Even so, marching a ray for every pixel remains slow to render. <span class="src">§14.1.3 · p. 400</span>
@@ 73
<strong>3D Gaussian splatting</strong> (2023) keeps the idea of semi-transparent matter but changes both the storage and the renderer. The scene is a large set of semi-transparent Gaussian blobs, blended by alpha blending, and rendered by differentiable rasterization: the renderer loops over the blobs and projects each onto the image, where NeRF loops over rays. Because most of a scene is empty, this is much faster to render, with training speed similar to the fastest volumetric NeRF variants. <span class="src">§14.1.3 · p. 401</span>

@@ 106
Implicit neural representations store a shape as a function (occupancy or SDF) computed by an MLP: continuous and compact, but they need 3D ground truth to train.
@@ 107
Differentiable rendering removes that need: render, compare with real images, and send the gradient of the loss back to the scene parameters, which can include camera poses.
@@ 108
Surfaces are rasterized, volumes are ray-marched. Volumes give useful gradients everywhere along a ray, before any surface is located.
@@ 109
NeRF is an MLP rendered by ray marching. 3DGS is a set of semi-transparent Gaussians rendered by rasterization, which is much faster to render.
