Using \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\boldsymbol{\delta}\T\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\delta})=2\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\delta}\) for symmetric \(\mathbf{M}\) and \(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}(\mathbf{c}\T\boldsymbol{\delta})=\mathbf{c}\), the gradient is \(2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}-2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\). Setting it to zero gives (1.25). The stationary point is a minimum, and the only one, because for any \(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\) we have \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}=\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}^2\gt 0\) when \(\mathbf{A}\) has full column rank: the cost curves upward in every direction.
@@ 40
Rewritten as \(\mathbf{A}\T(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}^*-\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{0}\), the equations say that the leftover error is perpendicular to every column of \(\mathbf{A}\). "Normal" means perpendicular.
@@ 41
The matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\triangleq\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) is the <strong>information matrix</strong>, which the book also calls the Hessian. It is symmetric and positive definite, so it has a <strong>Cholesky factorization</strong> \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\) with \(\mathbf{R}\) upper triangular, equation (1.26). Then \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\,\boldsymbol{\delta}^*=\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\) is solved in two easy steps: first \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\) for \(\mathbf{y}\) by forward substitution (1.27), then \(\mathbf{R}\,\boldsymbol{\delta}^*=\mathbf{y}\) by back substitution (1.28). No matrix is ever inverted. <span class="src">§1.3.3 · p. 31</span>

5. QR factorization: the same triangle without forming the information matrix
@@ 51
The alternative factors \(\mathbf{A}\) itself, equation (1.29): <span class="src">§1.3.3 · pp. 31–32</span>
@@ 52
where \(\mathbf{Q}\) is an \(m\times m\) <strong>orthogonal</strong> matrix (\(\mathbf{Q}\T\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}\)), \(\mathbf{d}\in\R^n\), and \(\mathbf{e}\in\R^{m-n}\). In practice \(\mathbf{Q}\T\) is applied as a sequence of <strong>Householder reflections</strong>, one per column, each of which zeroes everything below the diagonal in that column. \(\mathbf{Q}\) itself is never built: \(\mathbf{b}\) is carried along as an extra column and comes out as \(\mathbf{Q}\T\mathbf{b}\).

@@ 54
The second term does not contain \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\). The first can be made exactly zero by solving the triangular system \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\delta}^*=\mathbf{d}\), equation (1.32). So \(\norm{\mathbf{e}}^2\) is the smallest value the cost can take. And this \(\mathbf{R}\) is the Cholesky factor again, up to the sign of each row, because \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}\T & \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{Q}\T\mathbf{Q}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}\\ \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}=\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\), equation (1.33). <span class="src">§1.3.3 · p. 32</span>
@@ 55
For dense matrices the Cholesky route costs \((m+n/3)\,n^2\) floating-point operations, \(n^3/3\) of them for the factorization itself, and the QR route costs \(2\,(m-n/3)\,n^2\). Both are \(O(mn^2)\) when \(m\gg n\), and QR is slower by a factor of about 2, but more accurate and more stable. <span class="src">§1.3.3 · pp. 31–32</span> The book states that QR is more accurate without saying why. The standard reason is that forming \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) squares the condition number of the problem, so twice as many digits are lost to rounding. QR works on \(\mathbf{A}\) directly and avoids that.
@@ 56
Either way, the linear problem is solved by turning the information matrix or the Jacobian into a triangular "square root" \(\mathbf{R}\). The book calls this family of methods <strong>square-root SAM</strong>. <span class="src">§1.3.3 · p. 32</span>
@@ 57
<strong>"Solving the normal equations means computing the inverse of AᵀA."</strong> It is factored and then solved by substitution. Inverting is slower, less accurate, and destroys sparsity.
@@ 58
<strong>"δ* is the new estimate."</strong> It is the correction. The new estimate is \(\mathbf{x}^0+\boldsymbol{\delta}^*\). In today's example they coincide only because \(\mathbf{x}^0=\mathbf{0}\).
@@ 59
<strong>"Whitening is cosmetic."</strong> Without it, a row in millimeters would outvote a row in radians purely because of its units.
