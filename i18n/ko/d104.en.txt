Encoders measure the joint angles \(\mathbf{q}\). <strong>Forward kinematics</strong> turns \(\mathbf{q}\) into the pose of each foot relative to the body, and its derivative, the <strong>Jacobian</strong>, turns joint rates into foot velocity. All of leg odometry is built from these two functions. The Jacobian's transpose also links forces at the foot to torques at the joints, which is how a robot can feel the ground without a sensor in its foot.

@@ 12

2. The Jacobian: from joint rates to foot velocity
@@ 28
Differentiate (12.2) with respect to time. The result is linear in the joint velocities \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}\): <span class="src">§12.1.4 · p. 337</span>
@@ 29
\(\mathbf{J} \in \R^{6 \times 12}\) is the <strong>Jacobian</strong> of foot \(f\). Its top three rows \(\mathbf{J}_v\) give the foot's linear velocity relative to the base, its bottom three \(\mathbf{J}_\omega\) the angular velocity. (The book warns that some references stack the angular block first.) A foot does not care about the joints of the other legs, so 6 of the 12 columns (9 of 12 on a quadruped) are zero. The book writes the non-zero block as \(\bar{\mathbf{J}}\); for the second leg of a humanoid, \(\mathbf{J}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0}_6 & \bar{\mathbf{J}}_2 \end{bmatrix}\) with \(\bar{\mathbf{J}}_2 \in \R^{6 \times 6}\), equation (12.4). <span class="src">§12.1.4 · pp. 337–338</span>

Filling the gap: joint velocities are differences, and differences are noisy
@@ 36
Encoders measure angles, not rates. The book says \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}\) is "typically estimated by numerical differentiation", and later warns that this can degrade the velocity estimate. <span class="src">§12.1.4 · p. 337; §12.2.2 · p. 345</span> Here is the size of the effect. Take two readings \(\Delta t\) apart, each with an independent error of standard deviation \(\sigma_q\). The rate estimate \((q_k - q_{k-1})/\Delta t\) has variance \(2\sigma_q^2/\Delta t^2\), so
@@ 37
With \(\sigma_q = 0.01^\circ\) and readings every millisecond, \(\sigma_{\dot q} = 1.41 \times 0.01 / 0.001 = 14^\circ\)/s. Sampling faster makes it worse. This is why joint rates are filtered, and why the Jacobian route to velocity is noisier than its formula suggests.

Filling the gap: why the transpose of the Jacobian appears
@@ 51
Hold the base still and let the ground push on the foot with force \(\mathbf{f}\). The power it delivers is force times foot velocity, \(\mathbf{f}\T \mathbf{v}_f = \mathbf{f}\T \mathbf{J}_v \dot{\mathbf{q}} = (\mathbf{J}_v\T \mathbf{f})\T \dot{\mathbf{q}}\). Joint torques \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{eq}}\) deliver power \(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{eq}}\T \dot{\mathbf{q}}\). For the two to be equal for every \(\dot{\mathbf{q}}\),
@@ 52
So a force at the foot acts on the joints exactly like the torques \(\mathbf{J}_v\T \mathbf{f}\). Example with today's leg: the ground pushes straight up with \(\mathbf{f} = (0,\ 100)\) N. Then
@@ 53
To stand still, the motors must cancel this: \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = -\mathbf{J}_v\T\mathbf{f} = (0,\ 20)\) N·m, which is (12.5) with all accelerations zero and \(\mathbf{h}\) ignored. Check it with levers: the force passes through the hip (zero hip torque) and 0.2 m behind the knee (0.2 m × 100 N = 20 N·m). Read backwards, this is a force sensor: measure the knee torque and you know the ground force. <a href="#d106">Day 4</a> uses exactly that.

<span class="src">§12.1.6 · pp. 339–342</span> An \(n\)-bit absolute encoder resolves \(360^\circ / 2^n\): 8 bits give \(360/256 = 1.41^\circ\), the book's example. To see what that means, multiply by the lever arm. An error of \(1.41^\circ = 0.0246\) rad at the hip of our 0.8 m leg moves the computed foot position by about \(0.8 \times 0.0246 = 2\) cm; with 12 bits (\(0.088^\circ\)) it is 1.2 mm.
@@ 74
The <strong>Gray code</strong> orders binary words so that neighbours differ in one bit. In plain binary, going from 7 to 8 changes four bits (0111 to 1000); if the detectors do not switch at the same instant, the encoder can briefly report a wildly wrong angle. In Gray code the same step is 0100 to 1100, one bit. Contact sensors are cheap but respond more slowly than force sensors, and both need cables down the leg and take every impact, so the book notes they are found mostly on small indoor quadrupeds. <span class="src">§12.1.6 · pp. 340–342</span>
@@ 75
<strong>"The Jacobian of a foot is a small square matrix."</strong> In the book it is \(6 \times 12\), one column per joint of the whole robot, mostly zeros. The small square matrix is its non-zero block \(\bar{\mathbf{J}}\).
@@ 76
<strong>"A straight leg is just another pose."</strong> It is singular. Joint rates cannot move the foot along the leg, and a force along the leg produces no joint torque, so torque sensing cannot see it.
@@ 77
<strong>"An incremental encoder knows the joint angle."</strong> It knows the change since it was switched on. Something else must supply the starting angle.
