Filling the gap: why direct systems store inverse depth
@@ 47
Equation (7.6) has one depth \(d_z\) per selected pixel. Implementations such as LSD-SLAM and DSO store its reciprocal instead, the <strong>inverse depth</strong> \(\rho_z = 1/d_z\) (not to be confused with the robust kernel \(\rho(\cdot)\)). Put \(d_z = 1/\rho_z\) into the warp of Day 2. The 3-D point is \(\pi^{-1}(z)/\rho_z\); in the other frame it is \(\big(\mathbf{R}\,\pi^{-1}(z) + \rho_z \mathbf{t}\big)/\rho_z\); and projection ignores the common factor \(1/\rho_z\):
@@ 48
Two things are now easy to see.
@@ 49
<strong>Far points are harmless.</strong> A point at infinity is \(\rho_z = 0\), an ordinary number. Its warp \(\pi(\mathbf{R}\,\pi^{-1}(z))\) depends on rotation only, which is true of very distant scenery.
@@ 50
<strong>The image is nearly linear in \(\rho_z\).</strong> For a sideways motion \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\), \(\mathbf{t} = (b, 0, 0)\), the pixel moves by exactly \(u' - u = f\,b\,\rho_z\). With \(f = 500\) px and \(b = 0.1\) m: a point at 2 m (\(\rho = 0.5\)) moves 25 px, a point at 20 m (\(\rho = 0.05\)) moves 2.5 px. A matching error of \(\pm 0.5\) px is \(\pm 0.01\) in \(\rho\) at any distance. In depth the same error is \(2 \pm 0.04\) m for the near point, and from 16.7 m to 25 m for the far one: lopsided, and badly described by a Gaussian. Gauss-Newton and Gaussian noise models behave well in the variable in which the measurement is close to linear, and that variable is \(\rho_z\).

3. Dense reconstruction from one camera
@@ 52
BA yields camera motion and a sparse set of points. Augmented reality and robots often need a surface. One family of real-time methods estimates a depth \(h(\mathbf{z})\) for <em>every</em> pixel of a reference image by minimizing <span class="src">§7.6 · p. 216, eq. (7.10)–(7.11)</span>
@@ 53
The first term is the photometric error of Day 2 for every pixel: take pixel \(\mathbf{z}\) of the reference image \(I_0\), place it in 3-D at depth \(h\), project it into each neighbouring image \(I_i\), and compare brightness. The camera poses are taken as known here. The second term is a <strong>total variation</strong> regularizer weighted by \(\lambda\). It makes the depth map spatially smooth and fills regions with no usable texture with a surface like a soap film. <span class="src">§7.6 · p. 217</span> Such methods are called <strong>variational</strong>: the unknown is a whole function \(h\), and they run in real time because the per-pixel work parallelizes on a GPU.
@@ 54
Filling the gap: why \(|\nabla h|\) and not \(|\nabla h|^2\)
@@ 55
Look at one image row with a depth edge: \(h\) rises by 1 m between a near object and the wall behind it. If the rise happens in one pixel, total variation charges \(|1| = 1\). If it is smeared over four pixels in steps of 0.25, it charges \(4 \times 0.25 = 1\): the same. A squared penalty charges \(1^2 = 1\) for the sharp edge and \(4 \times 0.25^2 = 0.25\) for the smeared one, so it always prefers to blur. Total variation only counts the total rise and fall, and lets the data decide where the edge is. That is why it keeps object boundaries crisp while smoothing flat regions.

4. Depth cameras: fusing and tracking
@@ 57
An <strong>RGB-D camera</strong> measures a depth for every pixel (by structured light or time of flight), usually together with a colour image. It sits between a camera and a LiDAR: a full 2-D array of depths at once, but mostly indoors (sunlight interferes with its infrared light) and up to about 5 m. <span class="src">§7.7 · p. 217</span>
@@ 58
<strong>Mapping.</strong> KinectFusion stores the surface implicitly. Space is divided into voxels. Each depth image \(i\) is turned into a <strong>signed distance function</strong> \(d_i(\mathbf{x})\): for every voxel \(\mathbf{x}\), the distance to the measured surface along the viewing ray, positive in front of it and negative behind. The fused map is a weighted average <span class="src">§7.7 · p. 218, eq. (7.12)</span>
@@ 59
and the surface is the set of points where \(D = 0\). The weights \(\omega_i\) express how certain each measurement is and typically decay with distance. In practice the distances are <strong>truncated</strong> (clipped at \(\pm\tau\), and not updated far behind the surface), so that each measurement only affects the map near the surface it saw. This is a TSDF, a truncated signed distance function.
@@ 60
Filling the gap: why a weighted average is the maximum-likelihood estimate
@@ 61
Fix one voxel with true signed distance \(D\). Depth image \(i\) reports \(d_i = D + n_i\) with independent Gaussian noise \(n_i \sim \N(0, \sigma_i^2)\). The negative log-likelihood of the readings is \(\sum_i (d_i - D)^2 / (2\sigma_i^2)\) plus a constant. Set its derivative with respect to \(D\) to zero:
@@ 62
That is (7.12) with weights equal to inverse variances. Example: readings of 4 cm and 6 cm with \(\sigma = 1\) cm and one of 9 cm with \(\sigma = 2\) cm give \(D = (4 + 6 + 0.25 \cdot 9)/2.25 = 5.4\) cm. The average can be kept up to date without storing old images: keep \(D\) and the weight sum \(W\) per voxel, and for a new reading set \(D \leftarrow (W D + \omega d)/(W + \omega)\), \(W \leftarrow W + \omega\).
@@ 63
Try it: one viewing ray through a surface at 2 m. Raise the number of depth images to 30. Then set the noise to 20 cm and the truncation to 5 cm.
@@ 64
Notice: each depth image contributes a clipped ramp that crosses zero at its own noisy depth. Their average crosses zero at the mean of the readings, so the fused surface is closer to the truth than a typical single reading (about 3 cm off against 5 cm with five images, under 1 cm with thirty). With a truncation much smaller than the noise the ramps no longer overlap where they cross zero, different voxels average different readings, and the fused surface moves away from the mean.

A uniform voxel grid uses too much memory for large scenes. Voxel hashing and octrees store only the voxels near surfaces; Kintinuous moves the fusion volume along with the camera; ElasticFusion closes loops by deforming the whole dense map. <span class="src">§7.7 · p. 219</span>

5. Adding an IMU, GPS, or WiFi
@@ 69
An <strong>inertial measurement unit (IMU)</strong> measures angular velocity and linear acceleration at high rate. It gives reliable short-term relative motion and metric scale. There are two ways to combine it with vision. <span class="src">§7.8.1 · pp. 219–220</span>
@@ 70 attr
Left, loosely coupled fusion: the camera and the IMU each produce their own pose estimate, and a Kalman filter combines the two poses. Right, tightly coupled fusion: camera measurements and IMU measurements both enter one optimization over all residuals.

Loose coupling fuses finished pose estimates. Tight coupling fuses the raw measurements, so each sensor can help where the other is weak.
@@ 81
<strong>Loosely coupled:</strong> each sensor produces its own pose estimate, and a Kalman filter fuses the poses. Simple, and good enough to fly small quadrotors, but less precise.
@@ 82
<strong>Tightly coupled:</strong> the measurements themselves are combined in one estimator. The first such systems were extended Kalman filters that integrate the IMU in the prediction step and use keypoints in the update (the MSCKF). The alternative is a sliding-window or batch optimizer on a factor graph with visual and inertial factors side by side, as in ORB-SLAM3 and OKVIS2. Chapter 11 treats this in detail.
@@ 83
An IMU senses gravity, so roll and pitch become observable, and its accelerations are metric, so scale does too. The gauge freedom of Day 4 drops from 7 unknowns to 4: global position and yaw. <span class="src">§7.8.1 · pp. 220–221</span> The book adds a practical remark: some of the most accurate visual-inertial systems treat inertial integration as the backbone and use vision mainly to correct its fast-growing drift.
@@ 84
What remains undetermined is global: where on Earth, facing which way. <strong>GPS</strong> supplies absolute position outdoors and anchors the map to global coordinates, which matters for vehicles. Indoors, <strong>WiFi</strong> signals give a coarse position that complements visual localization against a map. <span class="src">§7.8.2 · pp. 221–222</span>
@@ 85
6. Where the field is moving
@@ 86
§7.9 points to two trends. Learned keypoints and learned matchers are replacing hand-crafted ones: SuperGlue matches keypoints with attention over both images at once and may leave a keypoint unmatched, and MASt3R matches with the help of a 3-D reconstruction from the image pair; MASt3R-SLAM builds a SLAM pipeline on it. Open issues are computation on small platforms and training data. The other trend is scope: dynamic and deformable scenes, and learned replacements for more and more stages of the pipeline. Part III of the book returns to these. <span class="src">§7.9 · pp. 222–223</span>
@@ 87
<strong>"Direct methods are simply more accurate."</strong> Under their conditions: photometric calibration and a global shutter. With a rolling shutter, keypoint methods cope better.
@@ 88
<strong>"TSDF fusion stores all depth images."</strong> It stores two numbers per voxel, the running average and the running weight.
@@ 89
<strong>"An IMU removes all ambiguity."</strong> It fixes scale, roll and pitch. Global position and yaw stay free until GPS or a known map fixes them.
@@ 90
<strong>"Loosely coupled means the sensors are not synchronized."</strong> It means they are fused at the level of pose estimates instead of raw measurements.
