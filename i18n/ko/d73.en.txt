A LiDAR is a laser with a stopwatch. It measures how long a pulse takes to come back, converts that to a distance, and pairs the distance with the direction the beam was pointing. The result is a <strong>point cloud</strong>: accurate in range, sparse and uneven in coverage, and collected over a tenth of a second instead of at one instant. Each of those three properties shapes an algorithm later this week.

So centimetre accuracy needs timing at the level of tens of picoseconds. Two consequences follow. The range error is set by the clock and the pulse shape, so it stays roughly the same size whether the wall is 5 m or 50 m away. And anything that makes the echo harder to time, such as sunlight raising the background level, directly costs accuracy. The book states the second point as a lower signal-to-noise ratio. <span class="src">§8.1 · p. 225</span>
@@ 25
TOF is not the only principle. Two continuous-wave schemes borrowed from radar are also used: <strong>AMCW</strong> (amplitude-modulated continuous wave) and <strong>FMCW</strong> (frequency-modulated continuous wave). An FMCW LiDAR sweeps the frequency of its light and can read the relative velocity of the surface from the frequency shift of the return, which helps in scenes with moving objects, at a higher cost and complexity. <span class="src">§8.1 · pp. 225–227</span> Next week's radar chapter works through FMCW in detail.

@@ 69
In 3D the same happens in both directions. Figure 8.2 shows a 64-beam sensor with a 104° vertical field of view. If its beams were evenly spaced they would be \(104/63 \approx 1.65^\circ\) apart, so at 20 m neighbouring rings would be \(20 \times 1.65 \times \pi/180 \approx 0.58\) m apart. Because both gaps scale with \(r\), the number of points per square metre on a surface facing the sensor falls like \(1/r^2\). That is the pattern in Figure 8.2: dense near the device, sparse far away. <span class="src">§8.1 · p. 226</span>
@@ 70
Imagine spinning in a dark room with a laser pointer and a tape measure that reads instantly. Every few degrees you write down how far the dot is. Plot those distances around a centre and you have drawn the room. Nearby walls get many dots, far walls only a few, because the same turn of your wrist moves the dot farther on a distant wall.

4. A scan is not a snapshot
@@ 72
One full sweep is called a <strong>scan</strong>, and scans carry time stamps so they can be processed in order. <span class="src">§8.2 · p. 227</span> But the sweep itself takes time. At a scan rate of 10 Hz it lasts 0.1 s. <span class="src">§8.2.2.1 · p. 231</span> A camera with a global shutter freezes the scene; a spinning LiDAR does not. A car at 15 m/s travels 1.5 m while its LiDAR turns once, so the first and last points of the same scan were measured from places 1.5 m apart. If the software treats them as measured from one place, straight walls come out bent and closed rooms do not close. This is <strong>motion distortion</strong>.
@@ 73
Go back to the widget and raise the sensor speed. The beams still hit the true walls, but the dots are drawn as if the sensor had stayed at its starting position. At 5 m/s the sensor moves 50 cm during the sweep, and the last points of the scan are drawn half a metre short of the wall they hit. Day 3 shows how odometry systems undo this.
@@ 74
<strong>"A LiDAR scan is like a depth image: dense and regular."</strong> The points are spread by angle, so they are dense near the sensor and sparse far away, and a multi-beam sensor leaves wide gaps between rings.
@@ 75
<strong>"Range accuracy gets worse in proportion to distance."</strong> The timing error does not depend on distance. What degrades with distance is the spacing between points, and weak echoes from far surfaces.
@@ 76
<strong>"All points of a scan share one time stamp."</strong> They are spread over the sweep period. Ignoring that is harmless for a parked sensor and a large error for a fast one.
