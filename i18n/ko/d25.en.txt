1. One pose per measurement does not scale
@@ 13
Until now every measurement was attached to a pose variable at its own time stamp. That works when a camera delivers a few frames per second. It breaks down in three situations the book lists. <span class="src">§2.2 · pp. 62–63</span>
@@ 14
<strong>High-rate sensors.</strong> Suppose an inertial measurement unit reports 200 times a second. One minute of driving would need 12,000 pose variables for that sensor alone.
@@ 15
<strong>Asynchronous sensors.</strong> A camera and a lidar rarely fire at the same instants, so each would need its own poses.
@@ 16
<strong>Motion-distorted sensors.</strong> A spinning lidar, a radar, or a rolling-shutter camera collects one "frame" over a stretch of time while the robot moves. Every point or pixel row has its own time stamp, and strictly each one was taken from a different pose.
@@ 17
A <strong>continuous-time trajectory</strong> is a function \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) that returns the pose at any time \(t\). It is defined by far fewer variables than there are measurements. It also lets you ask for the pose at times when nothing was measured: between measurements (interpolation) or, with caution, after the last one (extrapolation). <span class="src">§2.2 · p. 63</span>

2. A trajectory as a weighted sum of basis functions
@@ 27
The <strong>parametric</strong> way to build \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) is to fix \(K\) known functions of time, the <strong>temporal basis functions</strong> \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t)\), and add them up with unknown weights, the <strong>coefficients</strong> \(\mathbf{c}_k\):
@@ 28
The coefficients are the estimation variables. The basis functions are chosen once and never change. For now \(\mathbf{p}\) is an ordinary vector, such as a position. Poses on a Lie group come on <a href="#d29">Day 5</a>. <span class="src">§2.2.1 · p. 63</span> In the simplest case every component of \(\mathbf{p}\) uses the same scalar function \(\psi_k(t)\), so \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t) = \psi_k(t)\,\mathbf{I}\). The book makes that simplification itself in equation (2.53).
@@ 29
Here is the smallest useful example. Put knots at \(t = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) seconds and let \(\psi_k\) be a "hat" that is 1 at knot \(k\), falls linearly to 0 at the two neighbouring knots, and is 0 everywhere else. At \(t = 2.25\) only two hats are nonzero: \(\psi_2 = 0.75\) and \(\psi_3 = 0.25\). With \(c_2 = 4\) and \(c_3 = 8\),

3. B-splines and local support
@@ 32
A <strong>spline</strong> is a function made of polynomial pieces joined at <strong>knots</strong>. A basis function has <strong>local support</strong> if it is zero outside a short interval. <span class="src">§2.2.1 · p. 63</span> The standard family with both properties is the <strong>B-spline</strong>. The book names it but does not construct it, so here is the construction.

@@ 60
The sum looks like it involves all \(K\) coefficients. It does not: with local support, \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t_i)\) is zero for all but a few \(k\). Collect those active coefficients and the landmark in \(\mathbf{x}_i\), and the model is again \(\mathbf{z}_i = \mathbf{h}_i(\mathbf{x}_i) + \boldsymbol{\eta}_i\), equation (2.39), which is the form every factor has had since <a href="#d8">Week 2 · Day 2</a>. 5. Velocity and queries come for free
@@ 74
Because the coefficients do not depend on time, differentiating the trajectory only differentiates the basis functions: <span class="src">§2.2.1 · p. 64</span>
@@ 75
So a sensor that measures velocity, or acceleration, gives a factor on the same coefficients. For the cubic weights above, with knot spacing \(T\) seconds, \(\dot w_0 = -\tfrac{1}{2T}(1-u)^2\), \(\dot w_1 = \tfrac{1}{2T}(3u^2-4u)\), \(\dot w_2 = \tfrac{1}{2T}(-3u^2+2u+1)\), \(\dot w_3 = \tfrac{1}{2T}u^2\). In the worked example (\(T = 1\) s, \(u=0.5\)) these are \(-0.125, -0.625, 0.625, 0.125\), and the velocity is \(3\cdot(-0.625) + 6\cdot 0.625 + 3\cdot 0.125 = 2.25\) units per second.

Only the marginal over the few active coefficients is needed, not the whole covariance. <span class="src">§2.2.1 · p. 65</span> Example with the hats of section 2: at the midpoint the weights are \((0.5, 0.5)\). If the two coefficients each have variance 0.04 and covariance 0.01, the queried value has variance \(0.25\cdot0.04 + 0.25\cdot0.04 + 2\cdot0.25\cdot0.01 = 0.025\).
@@ 78
<strong>"The coefficients are poses on the trajectory."</strong> Only for the linear hat functions. A cubic B-spline does not pass through its coefficients: at a knot (\(u = 0\)) the weights are \(\tfrac16, \tfrac46, \tfrac16\), so the curve is a blend of three of them.
@@ 79
<strong>"Continuous time means infinitely many unknowns."</strong> The unknowns are the \(K\) coefficients. The function is continuous; the list of variables is finite and usually shorter than in discrete time.
@@ 80
<strong>"Local support is only a convenience."</strong> It is what keeps each factor small. With basis functions that are nonzero everywhere, such as sines, every measurement would touch every coefficient and the sparsity of <a href="#d13">Week 3 · Day 1</a> would be lost.

Q2. At a knot of a cubic B-spline (u = 0) the three active coefficients are 1, 4, and 7. What is the value of the curve there?
@@ 86
At u = 0 the weights are 1/6, 4/6, 1/6 (the fourth is 0). So p = (1 + 16 + 7)/6 = 4.
