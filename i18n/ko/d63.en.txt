@@ 10
A camera model is a function \(\pi\) that maps a 3D point in the camera's frame to a pixel, using a handful of calibrated numbers. Going forward is a division by depth. Going back is impossible for a point and possible only for a ray, because the division threw the depth away. Every visual measurement model in the rest of the chapter is built on \(\pi\).

1. Projection and unprojection
@@ 12
A camera model has a <strong>geometric</strong> part, saying where a 3D point lands in the image, and a <strong>photometric</strong> part, saying how bright the pixel gets. <span class="src">§7.3.1 · p. 197</span> The geometric part is the <strong>projection function</strong>
@@ 13
Here \(\mathbf{x}^c\) is the point in <strong>camera coordinates</strong> (origin at the camera, \(z\) pointing forward along the optical axis), \(\mathbf{z}\) is its pixel position in the image domain \(\Omega\), and \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) holds the <strong>intrinsic parameters</strong>, usually calibrated beforehand. The <strong>unprojection</strong> \(\mathbf{x}^c = \pi^{-1}(\mathbf{z}, \boldsymbol{\xi})\) returns a 3D ray: the depth is unknown, so the result is known only up to scale. <span class="src">§7.3.1.1 · p. 198</span>
@@ 14
Two notation warnings. In Part I, \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) was a pose perturbation (<a href="#d21">Week 4 · Day 3</a>); in Chapter 7 it is the vector of camera intrinsics. And the book uses \(z\) both for the depth coordinate and, in bold, for the measurement \(\mathbf{z}\).

2. The pinhole model
@@ 16
The <strong>pinhole</strong> (also rectilinear or perspective) model suits narrow-angle lenses without distortion. <span class="src">§7.3.1.1 · p. 198</span>
@@ 17
\(f_u, f_v\) are the <strong>focal lengths</strong> in pixels, horizontally and vertically; with square pixels \(f_u \approx f_v\). \((u_0, v_0)\) is the <strong>principal point</strong>, the pixel where the optical axis meets the image.

Filling the gap: where the formula comes from, and the matrix form
@@ 26
<strong>Similar triangles.</strong> In the figure, the ray from the point through the camera centre crosses a plane at distance \(f\). The small triangle (legs \(f\) and the image height) and the large one (legs \(z\) and \(x\)) have the same angles, so image height \(= f\,x/z\). The same holds for \(y\).
@@ 27
<strong>Pixels.</strong> A sensor counts pixels from a corner of the image, not from the optical axis, so the principal point is added. If the lens has focal length 4 mm and pixels are 5 µm wide, then \(f_u = 4\,\text{mm} / 0.005\,\text{mm} = 800\) pixels. This is why \(f\) is "in pixel units".
@@ 28
<strong>Example.</strong> \(f_u = f_v = 500\), \((u_0, v_0) = (320, 240)\), and \(\mathbf{x}^c = (0.4, -0.2, 2)\) m:
@@ 29
Unprojecting \((420, 190)\) gives \(\big(\tfrac{420-320}{500}, \tfrac{190-240}{500}, 1\big) = (0.2, -0.1, 1)\), which is \(\mathbf{x}^c/2\): the right direction, with the depth gone. The point \((4, -2, 20)\) lands on the same pixel.
@@ 30
<strong>Homogeneous coordinates.</strong> Represent the pixel \((u, v)\) by any triple \((\lambda u, \lambda v, \lambda)\) with \(\lambda \ne 0\), and write \(\sim\) for "equal up to a nonzero scale factor". Then the pinhole model is a matrix product:
@@ 31
Dividing by the last entry, \(z\), gives back \(\pi_p\). The matrix \(\mathbf{K}\) is the <strong>calibration matrix</strong>, and \(\pi_p^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) \sim \mathbf{K}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} u & v & 1 \end{bmatrix}\T\). The \(\sim\) in the book's unprojection formula is this same symbol. Homogeneous coordinates turn the awkward division into linear algebra, which Day 5 uses to relate two views.
@@ 32
<strong>Field of view.</strong> A pixel at the image edge, \(W/2\) from the principal point, sees a ray at angle \(\arctan\frac{W/2}{f_u}\) from the axis. An image 640 pixels wide with \(f_u = 500\) has a horizontal field of view of \(2\arctan(0.64) \approx 65^\circ\).

3. Lens distortion
@@ 34
Real lenses bend straight lines. The most common correction is the <strong>radial-tangential</strong> model, which adds four parameters, \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{RT} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\xi}_p\T & k_1 & k_2 & p_1 & p_2 \end{bmatrix}\T\), and inserts one step between the division and the scaling to pixels. <span class="src">§7.3.1.1 · pp. 198–199</span>
@@ 35
\((x', y')\) are <strong>normalized image coordinates</strong>: the pinhole image for \(f = 1\) and principal point at zero. The \(k\) terms are <strong>radial</strong>: they move a point along the line through the image center, by an amount that grows with the distance \(r\). The \(p\) terms are <strong>tangential</strong>, and model a lens that is slightly off-center or tilted.
@@ 36
Example: \(k_1 = -0.2\), all other coefficients zero, \(x' = 0.5\), \(y' = 0\). Then \(r^2 = 0.25\), the factor is \(1 - 0.05 = 0.95\), and \(x'' = 0.475\). With \(f_u = 500\) the pixel moves \(500 \times 0.025 = 12.5\) pixels toward the center.
@@ 37
Try it: slide \(k_1\) from negative to positive, then change the focal length.
@@ 38
Notice that negative \(k_1\) pulls the corners inward (barrel distortion) and positive \(k_1\) pushes them outward (pincushion). The center barely moves; the corner at \(r = 1\) moves 72 pixels at the default settings (\(0.18 \times 400\)). The last tile counts the iterations needed to undo the distortion for that corner.

Filling the gap: undoing distortion by iteration
@@ 40
To unproject a pixel you need \((x', y')\) from \((x'', y'')\). That means solving a polynomial of degree five in \(r\) (and worse with tangential terms), for which there is no formula. The book mentions Newton-Raphson. <span class="src">§7.3.1.1 · p. 199</span> An even simpler scheme works for moderate distortion. With radial terms only, the model says \(x' = x'' / (1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4)\), where \(r\) belongs to the unknown point. So guess, and repeat:

For the example above (\(x'' = 0.475\), \(k_1 = -0.2\)) the iterates are \(0.475 \to 0.49745 \to 0.49973 \to 0.49997\), heading to the true value 0.5. Systems that need this for every pixel compute it once and store a lookup table.

4. Wide-angle and fisheye lenses
@@ 43
A pinhole image point lies at distance \(f\tan\theta\) from the principal point, where \(\theta\) is the angle between the ray and the optical axis. As \(\theta \to 90^\circ\) this goes to infinity, so a pinhole-based model cannot represent rays from the side, let alone from behind. The book lists the usual choices (Figure 7.1 shows the lenses). <span class="src">§7.3.1.1 · pp. 198–199</span>

With all \(k_n = 0\), a ray at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) lands at radius \(f\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), a finite number: about 471 pixels for \(f = 300\). The DS model projects the point onto two unit spheres in turn and then applies a shifted pinhole projection; the book reports it to be around five times faster to compute than KB at comparable reprojection error. <span class="src">§7.3.1.1 · p. 199</span>

5. Brightness and time
@@ 66
The <strong>photometric model</strong> \(I = f(E, T)\) maps irradiance \(E\) (the light power arriving at a pixel) to the stored intensity \(I\), given camera settings \(T\): exposure time, gain, gamma correction, de-bayering, lens vignetting. It matters for methods that compare pixel brightness across images (the direct methods of Day 2) and for textured dense reconstruction. <span class="src">§7.3.1.2 · pp. 199–200</span>
@@ 67
Time matters too. A <strong>global shutter</strong> exposes all rows at once. A <strong>rolling shutter</strong>, found in most consumer cameras, reads the rows one after another, so each row is taken from a slightly different camera pose. Suppose read-out takes 30 ms while the camera turns at 60°/s: the last row is taken 1.8° later than the first, which at \(f = 500\) is a shift of \(500\tan 1.8^\circ \approx 16\) pixels. The remedies are a global-shutter camera, or a model with one pose per row, which becomes practical when an IMU measures the motion during the exposure. <span class="src">§7.3.1.3 · p. 200</span> The continuous-time trajectories of <a href="#d25">Week 5 · Day 1</a> were built for exactly this.
@@ 68
<strong>"Unprojection recovers the 3D point."</strong> It recovers a direction. Every point \(\lambda\,\pi^{-1}(\mathbf{z})\) with \(\lambda \gt 0\) projects to the same pixel.
@@ 69
<strong>"The focal length is a distance in meters."</strong> In \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_p\) it is in pixels: the physical focal length divided by the pixel size.
@@ 70
<strong>"Distortion is a small cosmetic effect."</strong> At the image corners it can be tens of pixels, far more than the sub-pixel noise of a good feature detector.

Q1. A pinhole camera has \(f_u = f_v = 400\), \((u_0, v_0) = (320, 240)\). Where does the point \(\mathbf{x}^c = (1, 0.5, 4)\) project?
@@ 72
u = 400·(1/4) + 320 = 420 and v = 400·(0.5/4) + 240 = 290. (720, 440) forgets the division by depth; (100, 50) forgets the principal point.
@@ 73
Q2. With the same camera, which 3D point could have produced the pixel (520, 240)?
@@ 74
Unprojection gives the ray direction ((520−320)/400, 0, 1) = (0.5, 0, 1). The point (2, 0, 4) is 4 times that direction, so it lies on the ray.
@@ 75
Q3. An image is 640 pixels wide and \(f_u = 320\). What is the horizontal field of view of this pinhole camera?
@@ 76
Half the width is 320 pixels, so the half-angle is arctan(320/320) = 45°, and the full field of view is 90°.

@@ 84

@@ 85

Fisheye cameras always use rolling shutters
@@ 88
The normalized coordinate is tan θ, which diverges at 90° and has the wrong sign behind the camera. Models such as Kannala-Brandt and Double Sphere parametrize by the ray angle or by a sphere instead.
