Week 2 review: from factor graph to solved estimate
@@ 3
After today you can take a small SLAM problem from its factor graph to a least-squares cost to a solved linear system, and say what steepest descent, Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt, and dogleg each do with that system.
@@ 4
The posterior over all poses and landmarks is a product of factors, one per prior or measurement, each touching only a few unknowns; the factor graph draws that product. With Gaussian noise, the negative logarithm of the product is a sum of squared errors, each measured in standard deviations, so the most probable state is the minimizer of a nonlinear least-squares cost. Linearizing every measurement function at the current estimate and whitening gives \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}-\mathbf{b}}^2\), which is solved exactly by factoring \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) (Cholesky) or \(\mathbf{A}\) (QR) into a triangle. Nonlinear solvers repeat that solve. Steepest descent uses only the gradient, Gauss-Newton takes the full linear solution, and Levenberg-Marquardt and Powell's dogleg limit the step to a region where the linear model can be trusted.

A scalar \(x\) has a Gaussian prior with mean 1 and \(\sigma=2\). One direct measurement gives \(z=4\) with \(\sigma=1\). Find the MAP estimate by whitening and solving the normal equation, with linearization point \(x^0=0\).
@@ 60
Both factors have Jacobian \(H=1\). Dividing each row by its \(\sigma\): the prior row is \(A_1=1/2=0.5\), \(b_1=(1-0)/2=0.5\). The measurement row is \(A_2=1\), \(b_2=4\).
@@ 61
\(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=0.25+1=1.25\) and \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}=0.25+4=4.25\), so \(\delta^*=4.25/1.25=3.4\) and \(x^{\text{MAP}}=x^0+\delta^*=3.4\).
@@ 62
Check with weights \(1/\sigma^2\): \((0.25\cdot1+1\cdot4)/(0.25+1)=3.4\). The estimate sits between prior and measurement, four times closer to the measurement.

A robot on a line has two unknown positions \(x_1,x_2\). Three factors, all with \(\sigma=1\): a prior \(x_1=0\), odometry \(x_2-x_1=2\), and an absolute measurement \(x_2=3\). Build \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), then solve by Cholesky factorization and the two substitutions. What cost is left?
@@ 64
Cholesky: \(r_{11}=\sqrt2=1.414\), \(r_{12}=-1/1.414=-0.707\), \(r_{22}=\sqrt{2-0.707^2}=\sqrt{1.5}=1.225\).
@@ 65
Forward, \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\): \(y_1=-2/1.414=-1.414\), \(y_2=(5-(-0.707)(-1.414))/1.225=4/1.225=3.266\).
@@ 66
Back, \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\delta}^*=\mathbf{y}\): \(\delta_2=3.266/1.225=2.667\), \(\delta_1=(-1.414+0.707\cdot2.667)/1.414=0.333\).
@@ 67
So \(x_1=1/3\) and \(x_2=8/3\). The three residuals are \(1/3\), \((8/3-1/3)-2=1/3\), and \(8/3-3=-1/3\): the disagreement of 1 between "0 + 2" and "3" is shared equally by the three equally trusted factors. The cost left is \(3\cdot(1/3)^2=1/3\).

A robot is on the \(x\)-axis at unknown position \(x\). A beacon at \((0,3)\) reports range \(z=5\) with \(\sigma=1\), so \(h(x)=\sqrt{x^2+9}\) and the true answer is \(x=4\). Start at \(x^0=1\). (a) Compute \(A\), \(b\), and \(J\). (b) Take one Gauss-Newton step and evaluate \(J\). (c) Take instead one Levenberg step (1.37) with \(\lambda=0.1\). (d) Compute the gain ratio of the step in (c).
@@ 69
(a) \(dh/dx=x/\sqrt{x^2+9}\). At \(x=1\): \(A=1/\sqrt{10}=0.316\), \(b=5-\sqrt{10}=1.838\), \(J=b^2=3.377\).
@@ 70
(b) \(\delta^{\text{gn}}=b/A=5.811\), so \(x=6.811\). There \(h=\sqrt{46.39+9}=7.443\) and \(J=(5-7.443)^2=5.97\). The cost went up: the slope of \(h\) at \(x=1\) is only 0.316, but it grows toward 1 as \(x\) grows, so the straight-line model demanded far too long a step.
@@ 71
(c) \(A^2=0.1\), so \(\delta^{\text{lm}}=Ab/(A^2+\lambda)=0.581/0.2=2.906\), and \(x=3.906\). There \(h=\sqrt{15.25+9}=4.925\) and \(J=(5-4.925)^2=0.006\). Lower than 3.377: accept, and reduce \(\lambda\).
@@ 72
(d) Predicted: \(L(0)-L(\delta)=b^2-(A\delta-b)^2=3.377-(0.919-1.838)^2=3.377-0.844=2.533\). Actual: \(3.377-0.006=3.371\). So \(\rho=3.371/2.533=1.33\), above 0.75: the model was pessimistic, and a trust-region method would enlarge its region.
