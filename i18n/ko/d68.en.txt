2. Lucas-Kanade: the one-dimensional case
@@ 37
To see how such a loss is minimized, strip the problem down. Take one image row, \(I_1(x)\), and a second row that shows the same content moved by an unknown shift \(d\): \(I_2(x) = I_1(x - d)\). The warp is \(x \mapsto x + s\), and we look for the shift \(s\) that minimizes
@@ 38
At \(s = d\) every residual is zero. This is the smallest example of <strong>direct image alignment</strong>, and the classic algorithm for it is named after Lucas and Kanade.

@@ 42
That is the Gauss-Newton step of <a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a> with a \(1 \times 1\) "Hessian" \(\sum g_x^2\). Two things follow at once.
@@ 43
<strong>No gradient, no information.</strong> Pixels in flat regions have \(g_x = 0\) and contribute nothing. In two dimensions the scalar \(\sum g^2\) becomes the \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(\sum \nabla I\, \nabla I\T\), which must be invertible. It is the second-moment matrix that the Harris and Shi-Tomasi detectors of Week 11 examine: a corner is a patch that Lucas-Kanade can track.
@@ 44
<strong>The step is only as good as the linearization.</strong> A pure stripe pattern \(I_1(x) = \sin(2\pi x / \lambda)\) makes this exact. Averaged over many periods, the cost per pixel and the step are
@@ 45
The cost has a minimum at \(s = d\) and another at every \(s = d + k\lambda\). The step always moves towards the nearest one. So the iteration reaches the true shift exactly when it starts within half a period: \(|s_0 - d| \lt \lambda/2\). With \(\lambda = 16\) px and \(d = 3\) px, starting from \(s = 0\): the first step is \(\tfrac{16}{2\pi}\sin(2\pi \cdot 3/16) = 2.35\), leaving an error of 0.65 px; the second leaves 0.007 px. With \(d = 9\) px the same iteration walks to \(s = -7\), the wrong minimum.

3. The basin of convergence, and the pyramid
@@ 47
The set of starting values from which the iteration reaches the right answer is the <strong>basin of convergence</strong>. For direct alignment it is about half the size of the finest dominant structure in the image, often only a few pixels. A camera that moves quickly between two frames shifts the image by much more.
@@ 48
The remedy is an <strong>image pyramid</strong>: blur the image and keep every second pixel, repeatedly. At level \(\ell\) the image is \(2^\ell\) times smaller, fine detail has been blurred away, and a shift of \(d\) original pixels is only \(d / 2^\ell\) pixels. <strong>Coarse-to-fine</strong> alignment solves at the coarsest level first, multiplies the result by 2, and uses it as the initial guess one level down. Each level only has to correct what the level above could not resolve. §7.7 of the book uses exactly this "coarse-to-fine Gauss-Newton optimization" for RGB-D tracking. <span class="src">§7.7 · p. 219</span>
@@ 49
Try it: the true shift is 20 px. Press "Run to convergence". Then switch the pyramid on and run again, one step at a time if you want to see the levels.
@@ 50
Notice: at full resolution the cost has a narrow valley at the true shift and shallow dips elsewhere. The estimate stops in a dip near the start, at about −2.6 px, far from 20 px. The strip under the curve shows that only starting values within roughly 13 px of the truth succeed. With the pyramid, the first steps run on a 32-pixel image whose cost has one broad valley; they land close to 20 px, and the finer levels only polish the result. The basin is now several times wider.
@@ 51
Lining up two detailed pictures by sliding one over the other only works if they are almost lined up already; otherwise every small detail finds some wrong partner. Squint first. With the detail blurred away only the big shapes are left, and those have one obvious best position. Then open your eyes a little at a time and fix what remains.

5. Two direct systems, and three levels of consistency
@@ 68
<strong>LSD-SLAM</strong> alternates: it tracks the camera against the current depth maps, then updates the depth maps given the camera, and runs a pose-graph optimization for global consistency. <strong>DSO</strong> optimizes structure and motion together in one <strong>photometric bundle adjustment</strong>. Its robust loss is a weighted sum of squared differences over a small patch around each point and can adapt to an unknown exposure time. To run in real time on a CPU it keeps only a sliding window of keyframes and marginalizes older ones (marginalization is the Schur complement of <a href="#d15">Week 3 · Day 3</a>, and of tomorrow). <span class="src">§7.4.2 · pp. 210–211</span>
@@ 69
The book lists three steps by which real-time direct methods reach global consistency: <span class="src">§7.4.2 · p. 211</span>
@@ 70
<strong>Sliding-window photometric BA</strong> over the last \(k\) keyframes, as in DSO. Consistent inside the window.
@@ 71
<strong>Pose-graph optimization</strong> on top: find the trajectory that agrees best with all pairwise image alignments (Figure 7.9).
@@ 72
<strong>Pose graph bundle adjustment (PGBA)</strong>: a variant of pose-graph optimization that also carries the full photometric uncertainty of BA, at the same cost, because still only poses are updated.
@@ 73
<strong>"Direct methods need no correspondences at all."</strong> They need no <em>matching step</em>. A correspondence is still used for every residual; it is computed from the current depth and pose by the warp, and it is wrong until those are right.
@@ 74
<strong>"A pyramid makes the result less accurate because coarse images are blurry."</strong> The coarse levels only supply the starting point. The final answer comes from the full-resolution level.
@@ 75
<strong>"More texture always helps."</strong> Fine, repetitive texture creates many local minima close together. It sharpens the final estimate and shrinks the basin.
