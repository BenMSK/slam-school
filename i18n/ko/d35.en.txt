IRLS on a non-convex loss slides into whichever valley its starting point belongs to. <strong>Graduated non-convexity (GNC)</strong> first replaces the loss by a nearly convex version with a single broad valley, solves that, and then reshapes the loss step by step back into the real one, following the minimum as it moves. Each step is still an ordinary weighted least-squares solve.

1. IRLS is alternating minimization
@@ 12
Yesterday ended with the joint problem (3.16): minimize \(\sum_i [\,w_i\,r_i^2(\mathbf{x}) + \Phi_\rho(w_i)\,]\) over the state \(\mathbf{x}\) and the weights \(w_i\in[0,1]\). Optimizing both at once is hard. Optimizing either one with the other held fixed is easy. <span class="src">§3.3.3 · p. 92</span>
@@ 13
<strong>Variable update.</strong> With the weights fixed, \(\Phi_\rho(w_i)\) is a constant and what remains is weighted least squares:
\[ \mathbf{x}^{(t)} \in \argmin_{\mathbf{x}} \sum_i w_i^{(t)}\,r_i^2(\mathbf{x}). \tag{3.22} \]
@@ 14
<strong>Weight update.</strong> With \(\mathbf{x}\) fixed, each residual is a known number and the sum splits into \(N\) separate scalar problems:
\[ w_i^{(t+1)} \in \argmin_{w_i\in[0,1]}\ \Phi_\rho(w_i) + w_i\,r_i^2(\mathbf{x}^{(t)}), \qquad i = 1,\dots,N. \tag{3.23} \]

Filling the gap: why the two weight rules agree
@@ 23
Write \(z = r^2\) and \(\phi(z) = \rho(\sqrt{z})\), as in Theorem 3.4. B-R duality says \(\phi(z) = \min_{w}\,[\,w z + \Phi_\rho(w)\,]\). Let \(w^\star(z)\) be the minimizer. If it lies inside \([0,1]\), the derivative with respect to \(w\) vanishes there: \(z + \Phi_\rho'(w^\star) = 0\). Now differentiate \(\phi(z) = w^\star(z)\,z + \Phi_\rho(w^\star(z))\) with respect to \(z\):
@@ 24
So the optimal weight is the slope of \(\phi\): \(w^\star = \phi'(r^2)\). On the other hand \(\rho(r) = \phi(r^2)\), so by the chain rule \(\psi(r) = \rho'(r) = 2r\,\phi'(r^2)\), which gives \(\phi'(r^2) = \psi(r)/(2r)\). That is rule (3.12). The two derivations of IRLS produce the same weights.
@@ 25
This also explains the three conditions in Theorem 3.4. \(\phi'(z)\to1\) as \(z\to0\) says small residuals get weight 1. \(\phi'(z)\to0\) as \(z\to\infty\) says huge residuals get weight 0. \(\phi''(z)\lt0\) says the weight decreases as the residual grows, so it stays between 0 and 1.
@@ 26
<strong>A useful consequence.</strong> Each step minimizes the joint cost (3.16) over one block of unknowns with the other fixed, so neither step can increase it. The cost goes down at every iteration and the iteration settles. Nothing says it settles at the global minimum.

2. Why plain IRLS gets stuck
@@ 28
The first variable update uses all weights equal to 1, so it is least squares, and Day 1 showed how bad that estimate is. The first weight update then judges every measurement by its residual <em>with respect to that bad estimate</em>. With the truncated quadratic the judgment is final: measurements that happen to lie near the wrong estimate get weight 1, the rest get 0, and the next solve sees only the first group. Yesterday's widget stopped after 2 steps for this reason. The book reports that IRLS with the truncated quadratic or Geman-McClure loss can fail with as little as 10% outliers. <span class="src">§3.3.4 · p. 93</span>
@@ 29
Picture the robust cost as a landscape with many pits. IRLS only walks downhill. GNC first floods the landscape so that one wide basin remains, lets the walker reach its bottom, then drains the water slowly. As the pits reappear the walker is already standing in the deep one.

Filling the gap: checking the ends of the dial, and deriving the weights
@@ 45
<strong>Geman-McClure.</strong> Divide top and bottom of (3.25) by \(\mu\beta^2\): \(\rho_\mu(r) = r^2/(1 + r^2/(\mu\beta^2))\). As \(\mu\to\infty\) this tends to \(r^2\), the convex quadratic. At \(\mu = 1\) it is (3.17). In between, differentiating twice with \(c = \mu\beta^2\) gives \(\rho_\mu''(r) = 2c^2(c - 3r^2)/(c+r^2)^3\), which is positive while \(r^2 \lt \mu\beta^2/3\). So \(\mu\) sets the width of the convex zone, and a large \(\mu\) puts most residuals inside it. The surrogate is (3.17) with \(\beta^2\) replaced by \(\mu\beta^2\), so the weight is (3.20) with the same replacement.
@@ 46
<strong>Truncated quadratic: the pieces join.</strong> At the lower edge \(r^2 = \tfrac{\mu}{\mu+1}\beta^2\), so \(|r| = \beta\sqrt{\mu/(\mu+1)}\), and the middle expression is \(2\mu\beta^2 - \mu\beta^2 - \tfrac{\mu^2}{\mu+1}\beta^2 = \tfrac{\mu}{\mu+1}\beta^2 = r^2\). At the upper edge it is \(2(\mu+1)\beta^2 - \mu\beta^2 - (\mu+1)\beta^2 = \beta^2\). As \(\mu\to\infty\) both edges close in on \(\beta^2\) and (3.24) becomes \(\min\{r^2, \beta^2\}\). As \(\mu\to0\) the band stretches over all residuals and \(\rho_\mu(r)\approx 2\beta\sqrt{\mu}\,|r|\): a shallow V, which is convex.
@@ 47

@@ 48
Setting \(g'(w) = 0\) gives \((\mu+w)^2 = \mu(\mu+1)\beta^2/r^2\), hence
@@ 49
This is at least 1 when \(r^2 \le \tfrac{\mu}{\mu+1}\beta^2\) and at most 0 when \(r^2 \ge \tfrac{\mu+1}{\mu}\beta^2\), so outside the band the weight is clipped to 1 or 0. Those are exactly the three cases of (3.24). Example: \(\beta = 1,\ \mu = 1,\ r = 1.2\). The band is \(r^2\in[0.5, 2]\) and \(r^2 = 1.44\) is inside, so \(w^\star = \sqrt{2}/1.2 - 1 = 0.18\). The plain truncated quadratic would say 0.

4. The GNC algorithm
@@ 51
Each iteration has three steps. <span class="src">§3.3.4 · p. 95</span>
@@ 52
Steps 1 and 2 are IRLS on the current surrogate. Step 3 is the only addition, and it is why the method is cheap to add to an existing solver.
@@ 53
The book does not say where to start \(\mu\). The widget below uses a common-sense choice: after the first least-squares solve, take the largest residual \(r_{\max}\) and pick \(\mu\) so that no measurement starts with weight zero. For the truncated quadratic that means the upper edge of the band lies beyond \(r_{\max}^2\), and the widget sets \(\mu_0 = \beta^2/(2r_{\max}^2-\beta^2)\), which puts the edge at \(2r_{\max}^2\). For Geman-McClure it sets \(\mu_0 = 2r_{\max}^2/\beta^2\), so every weight starts above \((2/3)^2 = 0.44\).

5. What GNC can and cannot do
@@ 57
On the three datasets of Day 1 (Figure 3.9), GNC with either loss recovers fairly accurate trajectories where gradient descent and IRLS sometimes failed, and in real problems it has tolerated around 80–90% wrong loop closures. <span class="src">§3.3.4 · p. 95</span> Two limits:
@@ 58
<strong>No guarantee.</strong> GNC is a heuristic. The 50% experiment in the widget is a small example. Its performance depends on the problem: excellent for pose-graph optimization, much worse on some other perception problems. <span class="src">§3.3.4 · p. 95</span>
@@ 59
<strong>It leans on the odometry.</strong> All the experiments so far assumed an <strong>odometry backbone</strong>: a chain of trusted measurements that also supplies a decent initial guess. Figure 3.10 corrupts just two odometry measurements. GNC then starts from a broken initial guess and ends in a local minimum where it labels all measurements, inliers included, as outliers. <span class="src">§3.3.4 · pp. 96–98</span>
@@ 60
The fix the book tries is to combine the two halves of the chapter. PCM does not use an initial guess, so it can prune gross outliers first. PCM followed by GNC gives the best results on SubT and Victoria Park. On M3500 all three variants (GNC, PCM, PCM + GNC) fail in this harder setting. <span class="src">§3.3.4 · p. 98</span> Robust SLAM without a trusted backbone is an open problem.

Consensus maximization

RANSAC variants that refine by local optimization, score better than by counting (MLESAC), or bias the sampling (PROSAC). Exact solvers by branch and bound: globally optimal, exponential in the worst case.
@@ 66
Consistency graphs
@@ 67
Group-\(k\) consistency, where a test involves \(k\) measurements and the graph becomes a hypergraph. Soft versions with continuous edge weights.
@@ 68
Robust back-ends
@@ 69
Formulations with latent on/off variables per measurement, max-mixture noise models, expectation-maximization, a single parametrized family of robust losses, and convergence guarantees for GNC-like schemes.
@@ 70
Certifiable algorithms
@@ 71
Relax the robust problem to a convex semidefinite program that can be solved in polynomial time and can certify afterwards that a solution is globally optimal. Slower than heuristics. Chapter 6 (Weeks 9 and 10) develops the idea.
