Two efforts pull in opposite directions. One connects language models <em>to</em> maps, so that their knowledge is tied to a real place. A system that passes a VLM-built map as text to an LLM is an instance of what the book calls a <strong>Socratic model</strong>: pretrained models composed as modules. With a map API, map calls can be interleaved with LLM calls and robot commands. This is the pattern of <strong>tool calling</strong>, in which an LLM is free to call external functions and use their outputs while it generates an answer. The book's example, NLMap, has an LLM break a user request into proposed objects and check that each exists, and where, by querying an open-vocabulary map. <span class="src">§17.4.1 · p. 515</span>

3. A foundation model for robots needs robot data
@@ 43
Text and images had the internet. The book's example of the recipe working in a new domain is DUSt3R for 3D vision (<a href="#d113">Week 19 · Day 5</a>). A <strong>robotics foundation model</strong> would be a base generalist policy: one model performing many skills on many robot bodies, representing the world only implicitly. Its training data are trajectories of sensor–action pairs \(\mathcal{D} = \{\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{u}_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\), which must be collected on robots or in simulation. <span class="src">§17.4.3 · pp. 516–517</span>

4. Policies that act without an explicit map
@@ 55
The simplest learner is <strong>behaviour cloning</strong>: supervised learning of the mapping from observation to action. The book calls it brittle, because the demonstrations contain little sub-optimal data and small deviations take the model out of distribution. <span class="src">§17.4.3 · p. 517</span>
@@ 56
Filling the gap: why small errors compound
@@ 57
Suppose the cloned policy errs with probability \(\epsilon\) per step while it is in states like those in the demonstrations. An expert never shows how to recover from a mistake, so in the worst case one error puts the robot in unfamiliar states where it keeps erring for the rest of a \(T\)-step episode. A first error at step \(t\) then costs \(T - t + 1\) bad steps, and the expected number of bad steps is at most
@@ 58
If errors did not compound it would be \(\epsilon T\). With \(\epsilon = 0.01\) and \(T = 100\): 1 bad step against a worst case of about 50. The bound grows with the <em>square</em> of the horizon, which is the same horizon the book pointed to in section 2. An explicit map and planner behave differently: after a slip the robot relocalizes and replans.

5. Where the chapter lands
@@ 74
The book does not pick a winner. Scaling robot datasets and models, combined with internet-scale VLMs and LLMs, could conceivably make explicit maps obsolete. Against that, it cites evidence that transformers struggle with compositional or chain-of-thought reasoning, which is why neural memory mechanisms with a notion of state (it names structured state space models) are re-emerging, with limits still unclear. It judges it equally likely, or more so, that generalization to compositional tasks in large, complex environments will come from explicit structure learned through a process such as SLAM. And it suggests the two are complementary: perceiving, representing, and understanding the world comes before acting, and an explicit world model should only help. <span class="src">§17.4.4 · pp. 519–520</span>
@@ 75
<strong>"The book says VLMs have made maps unnecessary."</strong> It reports one benchmark where frames beat a scene-graph prompt and one robot where the map-free variant failed, and concludes that it depends on the task's horizons.
@@ 76
<strong>"A VLA model contains no representation of the world."</strong> It has no <em>explicit</em> one that you can inspect or query. Whatever it knows is implicit in its weights and context.
@@ 77
<strong>"These model lists are the state of the art."</strong> The book itself says the section is not exhaustive and that new models appear very frequently.
