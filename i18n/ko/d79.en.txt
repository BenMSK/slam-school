@@ 10
An FMCW radar does not time an echo with a stopwatch. It transmits a tone whose frequency climbs steadily and compares the echo with what it is transmitting at that moment. The echo left earlier, so it is lower in frequency, and the size of that gap is proportional to the range. Velocity comes from a second comparison: how much the phase of the gap signal has turned between one chirp and the next.

1. Two kinds of radar
@@ 12
<strong>Radar</strong> (radio detection and ranging) sends radio waves and listens for reflections. It works in smoke, dust, rain and darkness, reaches far, and can measure velocity directly, which is why its use in SLAM is growing. <span class="src">Ch. 9 intro · p. 250</span> Robotics uses <strong>millimeter-wave (mmWave)</strong> radar: wavelengths of 1–10 mm, frequencies of 30–300 GHz, and in practice mostly the 76–81 GHz band reserved for cars. <span class="src">§9.1.2 · p. 253</span> Wavelength and frequency are tied by \(\lambda = c/f\), so a 77 GHz radar has \(\lambda = 3\times10^{8}/77\times10^{9} \approx 3.9\) mm.

How strongly an object answers is its <strong>radar cross section (RCS)</strong>: the size of an imaginary sphere that would reflect the same energy. It depends on material, size and shape. A truck or a concrete wall has a high RCS, a pedestrian a low one. The echo strength, called <strong>radar intensity</strong>, depends on the transmitted power and the RCS. <span class="src">§9.1.2.1 · p. 253</span> You will meet intensity again on Day 4, where it replaces the height that LiDAR descriptors use.

2. The chirp and the beat frequency
@@ 33
<strong>FMCW</strong> stands for frequency-modulated continuous wave. The transmit antenna (TX) emits a <strong>chirp</strong>: a signal whose frequency rises linearly from \(f_m\) to \(f_M\) during a time \(T\). The <strong>bandwidth</strong> is \(B = f_M - f_m\) and the <strong>slope</strong> is \(S = B/T\). Repeating up chirps one after the other is called <strong>sawtooth modulation</strong>. <span class="src">§9.1.2.2 · pp. 253–255</span>

The receiver mixes the echo with the chirp it is transmitting at that instant. The output is the <strong>intermediate frequency (IF) signal</strong>, also called the <strong>beat signal</strong>: a sine wave of constant frequency \(f_0\).

<strong>Worked example.</strong> Take \(B = 300\) MHz, \(T = 40\ \mu\text{s}\), and a target at \(d = 12\) m. The delay is \(t_0 = 24/(3\times10^{8}) = 80\) ns. The slope is \(S = 3\times10^{8}/(40\times10^{-6}) = 7.5\times10^{12}\) Hz/s. The beat frequency is \(f_0 = 7.5\times10^{12}\times 80\times10^{-9} = 600\) kHz. A delay of 80 billionths of a second, which is hard to time directly, has become a 600 kHz tone, which is easy to measure.

3. From samples to a range
@@ 49
The radar samples the IF signal with an analog-to-digital converter and takes a Fourier transform (FFT) of the samples of one chirp. Each target is a tone, so each target is a peak in the spectrum, and equation (9.2) converts the frequency of the peak into a range. <span class="src">§9.1.2.2 · p. 255</span> With several targets the IF signal is a sum of tones and the spectrum has several peaks. That spectrum, for one antenna direction, is one row of a radargram.
@@ 50

@@ 51
The book does not say how close two targets may be before their peaks merge. A tone observed for a time \(T\) can be separated from another only if their frequencies differ by about \(1/T\), which is also the spacing of the FFT bins. Put \(\Delta f = 1/T\) into equation (9.2):
@@ 52
<strong>Range resolution depends only on the bandwidth.</strong> For \(B = 300\) MHz it is 0.5 m, for 1 GHz it is 15 cm.
@@ 53
The spectrum also has an upper end. If the radar takes \(N\) complex samples per chirp, the sampling rate is \(N/T\), which is the highest beat frequency it can represent. That frequency corresponds to the range
@@ 54
So \(N\) samples give \(N\) range bins of size \(c/(2B)\). For a fixed number of samples, more bandwidth means finer bins and a shorter reach.

4. Speed from the phase between two chirps
@@ 58
Equation (9.3) says the phase of the beat signal is \(\phi_0 = 4\pi d/\lambda\). It turns through a full circle every time the range changes by \(\lambda/2\), about 1.95 mm at 77 GHz. That makes it useless for absolute range, because the radar cannot tell how many full turns happened. It makes it very good at measuring a small <em>change</em> in range. <span class="src">§9.1.2.3 · p. 255</span>

@@ 65
Imagine whistling a note that slides steadily upward while standing in front of a cliff. The echo you hear is the note from a moment ago, so it is a bit lower than the note you are whistling now. The farther the cliff, the older the echo and the bigger the gap in pitch. Range is the gap. To get speed, whistle the same slide twice and check whether the echo's wave has shifted by a fraction of one ripple in between.

Going deeper (optional): many chirps and the datacube
@@ 67
Two chirps give a noisy velocity, as the widget shows (about 5.2 m/s for a true 5.0). Real sensors send a frame of \(N_c\) chirps. For one range bin, the phase advances by the same \(\Delta\phi\) from chirp to chirp, so the \(N_c\) complex values form a tone with frequency \(\Delta\phi/(2\pi T) = 2v/\lambda\), and a second FFT across the chirps finds it. This is what the book means by a series of FFTs over several chirps. <span class="src">§9.1.2.2 · p. 255</span> The frame lasts \(N_c T\), so by the argument of section 3 the frequency resolution is \(1/(N_c T)\) and the <strong>velocity resolution</strong> is

@@ 69
<strong>"The radar measures the time of flight with a fast clock."</strong> FMCW measures a frequency difference. The chirp slope converts nanoseconds of delay into kilohertz of beat frequency.
@@ 70
<strong>"More bandwidth means more range."</strong> Bandwidth sets the size of a range bin, \(c/(2B)\). For a fixed number of samples, more bandwidth shortens the unambiguous range.
@@ 71
<strong>"The radar measures how fast the target moves."</strong> It measures only the radial part, the rate of change of range. A car crossing in front of the sensor at constant range has zero Doppler velocity.
@@ 72
<strong>"The phase gives a very precise range."</strong> It wraps every half wavelength. Only phase differences are used: between chirps for velocity, between antennas for angle.

An FMCW chirp rises in frequency with slope \(S = B/T\). The echo is delayed by \(t_0 = 2d/c\), so mixing gives a beat tone \(f_0 = S t_0\) and the range is \(d = c f_0/(2S)\).
@@ 92

@@ 93
