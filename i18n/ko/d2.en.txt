SLAM is a service for the rest of the robot, and its two customers have different deadlines: the controller needs odometry within milliseconds, the planner can wait seconds for a globally corrected map. The language the back-end uses to do its job is probability. A belief is a density, usually a Gaussian, and <strong>Bayes' rule</strong> says how a measurement turns a prior belief into a sharper one.

The same SLAM system answers at two rates. The slow process must never block the fast one.
@@ 33
This is why a real SLAM system is several processes running in parallel and not one function from sensor data to a map. <span class="src">§I.3 · p. 9</span> The processes also talk in both directions. The back-end periodically corrects the odometric trajectory that the controller uses, and it can tell the loop-closure detector which candidate matches are plausible. <span class="src">§I.3 · p. 10</span>
@@ 34
"Slow" does not mean "offline". The back-end must stay <strong>online</strong>: its runtime may not grow without bound as data keeps arriving, and it has to fit on the robot's own computer. That requirement is what historically separated SLAM from <strong>structure from motion (SfM)</strong>, the computer-vision problem of reconstructing a 3D scene from images. SfM typically takes an unordered pile of photos and may spend hours on a cluster. SLAM receives its data in order, as the robot moves, and must answer in seconds. The book adds that the border has blurred, and many people now use "visual SLAM" and "SfM" interchangeably. <span class="src">§I.3 · p. 10</span>

Q3. Do we need a map at all?
@@ 45
Open, but maps help
@@ 46
A network can map sensor data straight to actions, but its internal representation cannot be queried or inspected. Maps transfer across tasks, and in some applications the map is the product.
@@ 47
Q2 has a tempting middle road. A <strong>topological map</strong> is a graph whose nodes are places and whose edges mean "you can get from here to there". It stores no distances or angles, so it needs no optimization: add an edge when the robot drives between two places or when place recognition says two views show the same place. The weakness is <strong>perceptual aliasing</strong>: two different places that look alike. If Office 1 and Office 3 look the same, a purely topological map merges them into one room (Figure I.5d). A metric map has geometry to check the claim against. <span class="src">§I.3.1 · p. 12</span> Section 4 below puts a number on how misleading a look-alike can be.

3. Random variables and Gaussians
@@ 49
The rest of today is background that Chapter 1 assumes. A <strong>random variable</strong> is a quantity whose value we are unsure of. Its <strong>probability density</strong> \(p(x)\) says how plausible each value is and integrates to 1. The book writes \(p(\mathbf{a})\) for a density and \(p(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{b})\) for the density of \(\mathbf{a}\) once \(\mathbf{b}\) is known. <span class="src">Notation · p. xiv</span> Two numbers summarize a density: the <strong>mean</strong> \(\mu = \E[x]\) and the <strong>variance</strong> \(\sigma^2 = \E[(x-\mu)^2]\). The square root \(\sigma\) is the <strong>standard deviation</strong>, in the same units as \(x\).
@@ 50
The <strong>Gaussian</strong> (normal) density with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) is
@@ 51
About 68% of its probability lies within \(1\sigma\) of the mean, 95% within \(2\sigma\), and 99.7% within \(3\sigma\). A range sensor with \(\sigma = 0.05\) m reads within 0.10 m of the true range about 95% of the time.
@@ 52
For a vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\R^n\) the mean becomes a vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and the variance becomes the <strong>covariance matrix</strong> \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \E[(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\T]\), an \(n\times n\) symmetric matrix with the variances on its diagonal. You will meet the multivariate Gaussian in the book as equation (1.9):

4. Bayes' rule
@@ 57
Write the joint density of a state \(x\) and a measurement \(z\) in the two possible orders, \(p(x,z) = p(x\mid z)\,p(z) = p(z\mid x)\,p(x)\), and divide by \(p(z)\):
@@ 58
The <strong>prior</strong> is what you believed before the measurement. The <strong>likelihood</strong> is the measurement model read backwards: for the \(z\) you actually observed, how well does each candidate \(x\) explain it? The <strong>posterior</strong> is what you believe afterwards. The denominator \(p(z)\) does not depend on \(x\); it only rescales the result so that it integrates to 1. The book marks prior quantities with a check, \(\check{x}\), and posterior ones with a hat, \(\hat{x}\). <span class="src">Notation · p. xiv</span>
@@ 59
A worked example with perceptual aliasing. From odometry, the robot gives a 10% chance to being back at a place it has seen before. Its place-recognition module reports a match. The module fires 90% of the time when the place really is the same, and 20% of the time when it is a different place that looks similar. How likely is a true revisit?
@@ 60
The match is wrong two times out of three, although the detector "works 90% of the time". Revisits are rare and look-alikes are common, and the prior carries that fact. This is why a back-end cannot accept every loop closure at face value; the book returns to it in Chapter 3. <span class="src">§I.3.1 · p. 12</span>

5. Two estimators: MLE and MAP
@@ 62
A density is a belief; a robot needs one number to act on. Two standard choices:
@@ 63
The <strong>maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)</strong> picks the state that best explains the measurements and ignores the prior. The <strong>maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate</strong> picks the peak of the posterior. They coincide when the prior is flat. Tomorrow's pages name these two as the estimation framework behind modern SLAM, <span class="src">§I.4.1 · p. 15</span> and Chapter 1 writes SLAM as a MAP problem in equation (1.8). <span class="src">§1.2.1 · p. 27</span>
@@ 64
Try it: a robot in a corridor believes from odometry that it is at 2.0 m, give or take 1.0 m. A range sensor then says 4.0 m, give or take 0.5 m. Move the four sliders.
@@ 65
Notice: at the default settings the MLE is the measurement itself, 4.00 m, and the MAP is 3.60 m. The prior pulls the estimate back, but only a fifth of the way, because the measurement is the more precise of the two. The posterior is narrower than both inputs. Raise the prior σ to 3.0 and the MAP moves to 3.95 m, almost onto the MLE.

Q2. The prior chance of a revisit is 0.2. A place recognizer fires with probability 0.9 on a true revisit and 0.1 otherwise. It fires. What is the probability of a true revisit?
@@ 82
About 0.69
@@ 83
Bayes' rule: 0.9 × 0.2 / (0.9 × 0.2 + 0.1 × 0.8) = 0.18 / 0.26 ≈ 0.69.
@@ 84
Q3. Prior: position 10 m with σ = 2 m. Measurement: 13 m with σ = 1 m. What is the MAP estimate?
@@ 85
Weights are 1/σ²: 1/4 for the prior and 1 for the measurement. (10 × 0.25 + 13 × 1) / 1.25 = 15.5 / 1.25 = 12.4 m. Equivalently K = 4/(4+1) = 0.8 and 10 + 0.8 × 3 = 12.4.

Q4. A 2-D position has covariance with rows (9, −3) and (−3, 4). What is the correlation between its two coordinates?
@@ 87
About −0.08
@@ 88
The standard deviations are √9 = 3 and √4 = 2. Correlation = covariance / (σ₁σ₂) = −3 / 6 = −0.5.

Q5. With a Gaussian prior and a Gaussian measurement of the same scalar, when does the MAP estimate equal the MLE?
@@ 90
When prior and measurement have the same σ
@@ 91
In the limit of a very wide (flat) prior
@@ 92
When the measurement σ is very large
@@ 93
Always
@@ 94
The weight on the measurement is K = σ̌² / (σ̌² + σ_z²). It tends to 1 as the prior σ grows, and then the MAP estimate equals z, which is the MLE. With equal σ the MAP is the midpoint.
