@@ 11
A splat map can be both the thing you render and the thing you localize against. Hold the Gaussians fixed and descend on the camera pose: that is tracking. Hold the poses fixed and descend on the Gaussians: that is mapping. The map renders fast and its primitives can be added, moved, and removed, which suits SLAM. But optimizing it is still slow, and tracking by photometric error alone is not yet as accurate as classical sparse methods.

SuGAR adds a geometric regularizer: it defines a surface normal for each Gaussian and encourages neighbouring Gaussians to be flat, so a mesh can be extracted. 2DGS replaces the 3D Gaussians with 2D discs, which have a normal by construction, and adds normal and depth consistency losses.

@@ 22

2. One loss for tracking and mapping
@@ 25
As with neural fields (<a href="#d117">Day 3</a>), the appeal for SLAM is end-to-end optimization through the renderer: localization and mapping share one objective, there is no chain of hand-designed stages, and the map can represent complex light, including transparent objects. On top of that the book names two advantages specific to 3DGS. <span class="src">§14.3.3 · pp. 411–412</span>
@@ 26
<strong>Fast rendering.</strong> Rasterization is much faster than ray marching, which matters for anything that must interact with the map in real time, such as robot navigation.
@@ 27
<strong>Flexible primitives.</strong> Discrete, point-like Gaussians can be allocated and updated freely. That fits SLAM, where the scene's boundary grows and loop closures move parts of the map. This is the "dynamic allocation" row of Table 14.1.
@@ 28
How does a renderer estimate a pose? Camera poses are among the parameters in Figure 14.2. The pose enters equation (14.5) through \(\mathbf{W}\) and through the camera-frame mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c\), so the rendered image is a differentiable function of the pose, and the loss has a gradient with respect to it.

Filling the gap: the gradient of a pixel with respect to the camera position
@@ 30

@@ 31
Moving the camera to the right slides every footprint to the left, near ones more than far ones. That is parallax. Ignoring the smaller change in footprint width, the opacity at pixel \(u\) is \(\alpha = o\,\exp\bigl(-(u - \mu')^2 / 2\sigma'^2\bigr)\), so by the chain rule
@@ 32
Numbers: \(o = 0.8\), \(\sigma' = 0.25\), \(z = 2\). At a pixel one \(\sigma'\) from the centre, \(u - \mu' = 0.25\): \(\alpha = 0.8\,e^{-0.5} = 0.485\) and \(\partial\alpha/\partial c_x = 0.485 \times 0.25 / 0.0625 \times (-0.5) = -0.97\). At a pixel four \(\sigma'\) away, \(u - \mu' = 1\): \(\alpha = 0.8\,e^{-8} = 0.0003\) and the derivative is \(-0.002\).
@@ 33
The second number is the catch. A pixel tells the camera where to move only if a footprint already reaches it. If the rendered image and the recorded image do not overlap, the gradient carries almost no information, and the estimate can settle somewhere wrong.

@@ 37 attr
Two designs. End-to-end: the Gaussian map and camera pose are rendered to an image, compared with the camera image in a photometric and depth loss, and the gradient updates both the map and the pose. Hybrid: a classical tracker using ORB features or ICP supplies the camera pose, the Gaussian map is rendered and compared, and the gradient updates the map.

Two ways to use a splat map in SLAM. End-to-end systems get the pose from the rendering loss. Hybrid systems leave tracking to a proven classical method and use the Gaussians as the map.
@@ 53
What the book says
@@ 54
<strong>RGB-D</strong> (several early systems)
@@ 55
A depth camera supplies dense depth that initializes the Gaussians' positions and regularizes them through a depth rendering loss. Tracking is accurate and rendering is fast, but extra hardware is needed.
@@ 56
<strong>Monocular</strong> (MonoGS)
@@ 57
Colour images only. The Gaussians start at random positions and are refined by multi-view optimization, with no depth supervision.
@@ 58
<strong>Loop closure</strong> (LoopSplat)
@@ 59
Discrete Gaussians act as features that can be registered across times and viewpoints, so segments of the scene can be matched and aligned.
@@ 60
<strong>Physical imaging</strong> (I²-SLAM)
@@ 61
Adds motion blur and tone mapping to the rendering model, and reconstructs a high-dynamic-range map while tracking the camera.
@@ 62
<strong>Hybrid with keypoints or ICP</strong>
@@ 63
Photo-SLAM pairs ORB-SLAM's sparse tracking with a 3DGS map. GS-ICP SLAM and RTG-SLAM track with ICP, and RTG-SLAM adds a back-end optimization based on ORB keypoints.
@@ 64
<strong>LiDAR + 3DGS</strong>
@@ 65
Hybrid frameworks use the Gaussians as one map shared by several sensor types.

<span class="src">§14.3.3 · pp. 412–414 · Figs. 14.17–14.20</span> The hybrids work because a set of Gaussians with its extra attributes left out is a point cloud, so established point-based algorithms apply directly: ICP from <a href="#d74">Week 13 · Day 2</a>, the ORB-SLAM pipeline from <a href="#d71">Week 12 · Day 5</a>. <span class="src">p. 414</span>

4. What is still hard
@@ 68
The chapter ends with four limits of NeRF- and 3DGS-based SLAM. <span class="src">§14.4 · pp. 414–416</span>
@@ 69
Challenge
@@ 70
The problem

Directions the book proposes
@@ 72
<strong>Real-time performance</strong>
@@ 73
End-to-end systems are very slow. The book contrasts ElasticFusion, which tracked, mapped, and rendered in real time on a 2015 GPU, with most NeRF and 3DGS SLAM methods, which struggle to exceed 5 frames per second on modern GPUs. Rendering is fast; optimizing is the bottleneck.
@@ 74
Fewer Gaussians (compact representations). A higher-order optimizer in place of first-order gradient descent. A network that predicts poses and Gaussians in one forward pass.
@@ 75
<strong>Tracking accuracy</strong>
@@ 76
Often behind classical sparse SLAM. These methods assume photometric consistency across frames, which holds on synthetic data such as Replica and breaks in real scenes through unmodelled effects such as sensor noise and rolling shutter.
@@ 77
Model the light field and the imaging process more precisely. Match in a domain other than colour. Use a robust feed-forward prediction model.
@@ 78
<strong>4D scenes</strong>
@@ 79
Most methods assume a static world, and commonly mask out possibly moving objects.
@@ 80
Build on the success of 3DGS in non-rigid and 4D reconstruction and point tracking, toward a full 4D SLAM.
@@ 81
<strong>Large and outdoor scenes</strong>
@@ 82
Most methods are limited to room-scale sequences. Outdoors, illumination changes with weather and time of day.
@@ 83
Efficient map updates such as adaptive resolution, and drift correction over long distances.
@@ 84
On the feed-forward direction the book is specific. PixelSplat and MVSplat predict Gaussian parameters from posed images without any optimization at test time. DUSt3R (<a href="#d113">Week 19 · Day 5</a>) showed that a network can predict pointmaps without pose input, and NoPoSplat extends that to predicting Gaussians from two unposed views. Using such predictors in an incremental, multi-view SLAM setting is left as future work. <span class="src">§14.4 · pp. 415–416 · Fig. 14.21</span>
@@ 85
"Higher-order optimizer" is a pointer back to <a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a>. Gradient descent knows only the slope, so on a loss that is steep in some directions and flat in others it takes many small steps, as you saw the widget do from a distant start. Gauss-Newton also uses curvature and can cover the same ground in a few steps. The price is building and solving a linear system over a very large number of Gaussian parameters.
@@ 86
<strong>"3DGS renders in real time, so 3DGS SLAM runs in real time."</strong> Rendering one image is fast. SLAM must run many optimization steps per frame, and that is the slow part. <span class="src">p. 415</span>
@@ 87
<strong>"A photorealistic map means accurate geometry."</strong> 3DGS optimizes appearance. Accurate surfaces need extra regularization, as in SuGAR or 2DGS. <span class="src">p. 411</span>
@@ 88
<strong>"Rendering-based tracking has replaced feature-based tracking."</strong> The book says its accuracy often falls behind classical sparse SLAM, and several practical systems still track with ORB features or ICP. <span class="src">pp. 414–415</span>
