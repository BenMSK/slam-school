A Gaussian needs a mean and a way to add noise to it. For a pose the mean is a group member \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\), the noise is an ordinary Gaussian vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) in the tangent space, and "add" means \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\). Because Exp bends straight lines into arcs, this family can describe the curved clouds that real robot motion produces, which no Gaussian in \((x, y)\) can.

To draw a sample: draw \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) as for any Gaussian vector, then form \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\). The simplest case is a heading. Because \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) multiplies on the right, it is expressed in the frame attached to the mean pose. For a robot pose, \(\rho_1\) is error along the robot's own forward direction, whichever way the robot faces in the world.

Filling the gap: what "surjective-only" means, and when it matters
@@ 30
The book calls the exponential a "surjective-only mapping". <strong>Surjective</strong> means every group member is reached. "Only" means it is not one-to-one: angles \(\delta\) and \(\delta + 2\pi\) give the same rotation (Day 2). A Gaussian has tails that run to infinity, so some of its probability lands on \(|\delta| \gt \pi\) and wraps around onto rotations that smaller angles also reach.
@@ 31
How much? With a standard deviation of 10°, the wrap point 180° is 18 standard deviations away, and the wrapped probability is far too small to matter. With a standard deviation of 90° it would matter, and this way of describing uncertainty stops being a good model. The representation is meant for <em>concentrated</em> uncertainty, which is the usual case for a tracked robot.
@@ 32
3. The banana
@@ 33
Let a robot drive straight ahead in \(K\) steps of one unit. Each step is a pose increment whose tangent vector is the command \(\mathbf{u} = (1, 0, 0)\) plus a little Gaussian noise, mostly in heading: \(\mathbf{T}_{k+1} = \mathbf{T}_k\,\Exp(\mathbf{u} + \boldsymbol{\delta}_k)\). Where can the robot be after \(K\) steps? Simulate it 500 times.
@@ 34
Notice that the cloud is curved, and that the pink ellipse, the best Gaussian in (x, y), covers empty ground ahead of the cloud and misses its curled-back ends, while the green contour bends with the cloud. With the default settings (15 steps, 6° of heading noise per step) the mean of (x, y) is at about (14.4, −0.1), 0.6 behind the noise-free end point (15, 0), and the tangent-space mean maps to (15.0, −0.1). The x-coordinate has skewness about −2.2, and a Gaussian has none.
@@ 35
The green contour is computed as the book's definition suggests. Take \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) to be the noise-free end pose, convert every sample to a tangent vector \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_j = \Log(\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}\mathbf{T}_j)\), fit an ordinary mean and covariance to those vectors, and push the two-sigma ellipse of that fit back through \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\cdot)\). (The fit is three-dimensional, and the widget draws the ellipse spanned by its two largest principal directions.) An ellipse in the tangent space becomes a banana on the ground.
@@ 36
If you walk twenty paces with your eyes closed, you know quite well <em>how far</em> you walked and rather badly <em>in which direction</em>. The places you might be form a piece of a ring around the starting point, not a blob. A description that says "about twenty paces, direction uncertain" fits that ring. A description in east and north coordinates does not.

4. Why a banana, and why the tangent space straightens it
@@ 38
Filling the gap: a one-step model you can solve by hand
@@ 39
Strip the simulation to its core. The robot first turns by an uncertain angle \(\delta_\theta\sim\N(0,\sigma^2)\) and then drives a known distance \(L\). It ends at
@@ 40
<strong>In (x, y).</strong> The sideways coordinate is linear in the noise, so it is close to Gaussian with standard deviation \(L\sigma\). The forward coordinate is <em>quadratic</em> in the noise: whichever way the robot turns, it falls short of \(L\), never beyond. That one-sided shortfall is the curl of the banana. Its average is \(\E[x] \approx L(1-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\). For \(L = 10\) and \(\sigma = 0.3\) rad (17°) the mean is at \(x \approx 9.55\), a point inside the bend where the robot almost never is. A Gaussian puts its highest density at its mean.
@@ 41
<strong>In the tangent space.</strong> Take the mean pose \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) at \((L, 0)\) with heading 0 and try the tangent vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta} = \delta_\theta\,(0, L, 1)\). With \(\boldsymbol{\rho} = (0, L\delta_\theta)\) and \(\theta = \delta_\theta\), the matrix \(\mathbf{V}\) of Day 2 gives
@@ 42
\(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) only shifts this by \((L, 0)\), so \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\) is at \((L\cos\delta_\theta,\ L\sin\delta_\theta)\) with heading \(\delta_\theta\). That is the true end pose, <em>exactly</em>, with no approximation. The tangent vector is a fixed direction \((0, L, 1)\) times the Gaussian number \(\delta_\theta\), so it is exactly Gaussian, with covariance
@@ 43
The whole arc of possible end poses is the image under Exp of a straight line in the tangent space. The off-diagonal entry \(L\) says that sideways error and heading error go together: a robot that ended up to the left is also facing left.
@@ 44
The simulation in the widget is messier than this model, since noise enters at every step, and its tangent-space cloud is only approximately Gaussian. The picture still holds: the curvature comes from the group, and a description that respects the group absorbs it. The book points to Long et al. and to Barfoot and Furgale for the full treatment. <span class="src">§2.1.4 · p. 60</span>

5. Where these covariances appear
@@ 46
<strong>Noise models of factors.</strong> An odometry or loop-closure measurement that is itself a pose carries a covariance of this kind, and its residual is formed in the Lie algebra, as in equation (2.20) from yesterday.
@@ 47
<strong>The uncertainty of an estimate.</strong> Yesterday's Gauss-Newton solved for \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\). The inverse of the final information matrix \(\sum_i\mathbf{H}_i\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i^{-1}\mathbf{H}_i\) is the approximate covariance of that \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\). So the covariance a solver reports for a pose is a tangent-space covariance about the estimate, in the sense of (2.23).
@@ 48
<strong>Left or right.</strong> The book's noise multiplies on the right. Putting it on the left, \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}\), is equally valid but gives a different \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) for the same cloud. Tomorrow's adjoint converts one into the other.
@@ 49
The book closes with a caveat: (2.22) <em>induces</em> a distribution on rotations by pushing a tangent-space Gaussian through Exp. One can also define distributions directly on the group, and Chapter 6 does so because it brings computational advantages there. <span class="src">§2.1.4 · p. 60</span>
@@ 50
<strong>"The covariance of a pose is the covariance of its matrix entries."</strong> It is the covariance of the tangent vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\): 6 × 6 for a 3D pose, not 16 × 16.
@@ 51
<strong>"The first block of Σ is the position uncertainty in world coordinates."</strong> With right-multiplied noise it is expressed in the frame of the mean pose, and \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\) is not the position error once rotation error is present.
@@ 52
<strong>"The mean pose is the average of the sampled matrices."</strong> Averaging matrices leaves the group (Day 1). The mean is the \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) about which the tangent vectors average to zero.
@@ 53
<strong>"A Gaussian in the tangent space is always a good model."</strong> Only while the spread is well inside a half turn. For very uncertain headings the tails wrap.

Q2. One-step model: a robot turns by \(\delta_\theta\sim\N(0,\sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma = 0.2\) rad, then drives \(L = 10\) m. Roughly where is the mean of its x-coordinate?
@@ 57
\(\E[x]\approx L(1-\tfrac12\sigma^2) = 10\,(1 - 0.02) = 9.8\) m. Every turn, left or right, shortens the forward progress, so the mean falls behind the noise-free end point.

The noise in each step is Gaussian. But the position is a sine and cosine of the heading error, and a nonlinear function of a Gaussian is not Gaussian. In tangent coordinates at the mean pose the relation is close to linear, so the Gaussian shape is nearly kept.
@@ 66
A Gaussian on a Lie group is \(\mathbf{T} = \bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\) with \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\sim\N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\): the mean is on the group, the covariance in the tangent space.
@@ 67
\(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is 3 × 3 for \(\SO(3)\) and \(\SE(2)\), and 6 × 6 for \(\SE(3)\). With right-multiplied noise it is expressed in the frame of the mean.
@@ 68
