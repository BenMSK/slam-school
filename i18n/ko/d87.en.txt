@@ 10
A single event is four numbers and may be noise, so a front-end must combine thousands of them. It makes three design choices: how to hold the events (the representation), whether to reduce them to features or use them all (indirect or direct), and whether the processing is designed or learned. Every choice trades the sensor's speed and sparsity against the convenience of mature image-based tools.

1. What the front-end does
@@ 12
In this chapter the <strong>front-end</strong> takes the raw events (and calibration) and outputs camera poses and a scene map. The <strong>back-end</strong> then refines both, runs more slowly, and can feed its result back to reduce drift. <span class="src">§10.4 · p. 289</span> In <a href="#d1">Week 1 · Day 1</a> the front-end handed the back-end pseudo-measurements; here it hands over a first full estimate. The split is the same idea: fast and rough first, slow and careful second.

2. Event representations
@@ 26
In an image, all pixels share one timestamp and therefore one camera pose. That shared pose is the assumption on which classical multi-view geometry rests. Events break it: each has its own microsecond timestamp, so each belongs to a slightly different pose. <span class="src">§10.4.1 · p. 290</span>
@@ 27
Many methods nevertheless convert events into an array first, to reuse mature image tools or to feed a neural network. The book warns of two prices: <strong>quantization</strong> (events with similar timestamps are lumped together) and <strong>loss of sparsity</strong> (the array is mostly zeros where nothing happened). It names event images, time surfaces, and voxel grids, and sends you to a survey for the definitions. <span class="src">§10.4.1 · p. 290</span>

One system can mix them: EVO maps with raw events and tracks the camera with edge-like event images. The book's ideal is a representation that keeps the speed and sparsity of the sensor, and it says plainly that this is still open research. <span class="src">§10.4.1 · p. 290</span>
@@ 62
A number shows why quantization matters. Suppose the camera turns at 300° per second (5.24 rad/s) and its focal length is 200 pixels. Near the image centre the picture then slides at about \(200 \times 5.24 \approx 1050\) pixels per second. An event frame over 10 ms smears every edge across 10 pixels, exactly the motion blur the sensor was built to avoid.
@@ 63
3. Indirect methods
@@ 64
<strong>Indirect methods</strong> work in two steps. They first extract and track features in the events (points, lines, or other primitives), then hand those to classical geometric SLAM. Features compress the stream into a few informative primitives, which saves computation. <span class="src">§10.4.2 · p. 291</span>
@@ 65
The hard part is data association. One event is too little, and too dependent on the motion, to be matched by appearance. The sensor offers a way out: events arrive almost continuously, so a feature has barely moved when its next events arrive. Association can be made by closeness in pixel position and time, and it becomes natural to <strong>track</strong> a feature from moment to moment instead of <strong>matching</strong> it between two distant views.
@@ 66
The rest is the toolbox of Weeks 11 and 12. Camera tracking aligns features with a known map by minimizing the reprojection error (<a href="#d67">Week 12 · Day 1</a>), and the 3D structure comes from triangulating corresponding features from known poses (<a href="#d65">Week 11 · Day 5</a>). The weak link is the feature step: event features are less mature than image features because of noise, sparsity, asynchrony, and motion dependence. That limits accuracy, and some systems add grayscale images or an IMU to compensate. <span class="src">§10.4.2 · p. 291</span>
@@ 67
4. Direct methods
@@ 68
<strong>Direct methods</strong> use all events, not only those that fit the definition of a feature. They align the events with a map, with an image, or with other events, in one step: the motion or scene parameters are found by optimizing an objective such as a photometric error. If the event rate exceeds what the computer can handle, events are denoised or subsampled first. <span class="src">§10.4.3 · p. 291</span>
@@ 69
Direct methods lean on the near-continuity of the data. Since there is no blind time, the pose changes only a little from one update to the next, so each update can start from the previous pose and compute a small increment. <span class="src">§10.4.3 · p. 292</span>
@@ 70
The most accurate subclass, according to the book, estimates motion by <strong>event alignment</strong>. The idea is to estimate the motion by undoing it. Guess the motion; move every event back along that motion to where it would have been at one reference time; add up the moved events per pixel. The result is the <strong>image of warped events (IWE)</strong>. If the guess is right, events from the same edge pile onto the same pixels and the IWE is sharp. If it is wrong, the IWE is blurred. Searching for the sharpest IWE is called <strong>contrast maximization (CMax)</strong>, and sharpness can be scored by variance, gradient magnitude, or other measures. Tomorrow you will run it. <span class="src">§10.4.3 · p. 292</span>
@@ 71
Data association in direct methods is implicit and soft: events are associated because they land near each other on the pixel grid. <span class="src">§10.4.3 · p. 292</span>
@@ 72
5. Designed or learned
@@ 73
Most event-based SLAM so far is <strong>model-based</strong>: hand-designed from the sensor's operating principle and the geometry of the problem. <strong>Learning-based</strong> methods use artificial neural networks instead, either on image-like representations or directly on events with <strong>spiking neural networks</strong>. <span class="src">§10.4.4 · p. 292</span>

A network can replace one part of the pipeline, such as feature extraction and tracking, or the whole system end to end. Learned methods cope with complicated representations and with noise without a hand-written model. In return they need large training sets, and they can fail on scenes unlike the training data, which is called <strong>domain shift</strong>. <span class="src">§10.4.4 · p. 292</span>
@@ 83
<strong>"Converting events to frames costs nothing."</strong> It quantizes time and fills the array with zeros, giving up two of the sensor's advantages.
@@ 84
<strong>"A time surface is a brightness image."</strong> It stores how recently each pixel fired. A bright scene region with no motion has value zero.
@@ 85
<strong>"Direct methods have no data association."</strong> They have it implicitly: events are associated by where they land on the pixel grid.

@@ 98
Because events carry rich descriptors that make matching unnecessary
@@ 99

Because event cameras cannot observe the same point twice

Because tracking does not require any data association

Single events have no stable appearance to match, but the high temporal resolution keeps successive events of a feature close together in pixel space, so association by vicinity works.
