@@ 10
Every static point in a radar scan reports how fast the sensor is closing in on it. That is one linear equation in the sensor's velocity, and one scan holds dozens of them, so the velocity comes out of a small least-squares problem with no matching between scans at all. Rotation is invisible to this trick and position still has to be integrated, so radar also uses the scan-matching methods you know from LiDAR, adapted to sparser and noisier data.

1. One scan, one velocity
@@ 12
<strong>Radar odometry</strong> estimates the sensor's own motion (its <strong>egomotion</strong>) from a sequence of radar readings. Like every odometry it drifts: methods for spinning radar accumulate on the order of 1–2% of translational drift per 100 m. <span class="src">§9.2 · p. 261</span>

2. Moving objects are outliers
@@ 24
The model assumes every target is static. A point on a moving car breaks it: its reading contains the car's velocity too, and it falls off the cosine. Least squares has no defence against such points (<a href="#d31">Week 6 · Day 1</a>). The book names two remedies. One is RANSAC (<a href="#d32">Week 6 · Day 2</a>) followed by least squares on the inliers; in 3D a sample is three points, because there are three unknowns and each point gives one equation. <span class="src">§9.2.1 · p. 262</span> The other is a robust loss on the Doppler residual, the Cauchy loss in the book's example (<a href="#d34">Week 6 · Day 4</a>). <span class="src">§9.2.1 · p. 265</span>
@@ 25
Try it: the right panel plots each point's closing speed against its azimuth. Press "Reject outliers", then narrow the field of view to 60°.
@@ 26
Notice: at the start the true velocity is (9.40, 3.42) m/s and plain least squares returns about (10.79, 7.36), more than 4 m/s off, because a quarter of the points sit on two moving cars. After outlier rejection 45 of the 60 points are kept and the estimate is within 0.1 m/s. With the field of view at 60°, the one-sigma uncertainty of the leftward component roughly doubles (0.029 to 0.056 m/s) while the forward one barely moves, as in the worked example.
@@ 27
The points RANSAC throws away are useful in their own right: they mark the moving objects. Several pipelines use the Doppler fit as a filter and remove those points before registration or place recognition. <span class="src">§9.2.4 · p. 268</span>

3. What Doppler cannot see, and the factor-graph version
@@ 29
Spin a radar on the spot. No range to any static point changes, so every Doppler reading is zero. <strong>Rotational velocity is not observable</strong> from the per-point velocities of a single radar, unless something is assumed about how the vehicle moves. <span class="src">§9.2.1 · pp. 262–263</span> The usual fix is a gyroscope. A registration-free combination of Doppler and IMU gave the lowest error in a comparison on 3+1D radar, with drift as low as 0.3% over 4.5 km. <span class="src">§9.2.1 · p. 263</span>

4. Matching scans: three families
@@ 62
Velocity must be integrated to get position, and rotation needs help. The other families estimate the relative pose between nearby scans by finding what the scans have in common. <span class="src">§9.2 · pp. 261–262</span>
@@ 63
How the relative pose is found
@@ 64
Watch out for
@@ 65
Direct, §9.2.2
@@ 66
The raw radargram
@@ 67
Correlation. A rotation of the sensor is a shift of the polar image along its azimuth axis, so phase correlation gives the rotation; correlating the Cartesian images then gives the translation. A learned variant masks out the parts of the radargram that are not stable
@@ 68
Assumes the power returned from a place stays the same over time. Moving objects and noise artifacts break this
@@ 69
Feature-based, §9.2.3
@@ 70
Keypoints with descriptors, as in vision (SIFT, SURF, ORB, or radar-specific ones)
@@ 71
Match descriptors, reject outliers with RANSAC, then align the matched points in closed form with the SVD (<a href="#d74">Week 13 · Day 2</a>)
@@ 72
Tolerates a poor initial guess, but descriptors are harder to make repeatable in radar. Learned features need training data with ground-truth poses
@@ 73
Registration-based, §9.2.4
@@ 74
A point cloud from CFAR, BFAR or k-strongest filtering
@@ 75
ICP- or NDT-style registration, usually against several previous keyframes or a submap
@@ 76
Too few points lose information and too many bring in noise. Association by proximity needs a good initial guess
@@ 77
Why register against several keyframes and not just the last scan? Radar clouds are sparse and noisy, so a single pair is fragile. More keyframes give more correspondences, which adds constraints in feature-poor places, and they give redundancy in time, so a sudden occlusion or a moving object has less effect. <span class="src">§9.2.4 · p. 267</span> CFEAR is the book's main example. It keeps the k strongest returns per azimuth, registers each new cloud jointly against the \(s\) most recent keyframes with a point-to-point, point-to-line or point-to-distribution error, and weights each match by the agreement of the normal vectors, the planarity, and the number of points nearby. <span class="src">§9.2.4 · p. 267</span>
@@ 78
For 3+1D radar the two worlds combine. The Doppler least-squares velocity of section 1 is the initial estimate and registration refines it. One method weights points by a covariance that grows with distance from the sensor, because azimuth and elevation are coarse (Day 2). Another matches each point to a weighted set of nearby distributions to cope with sparse clouds. <span class="src">§9.2.4 · p. 268</span>

5. Motion compensation
@@ 80
Day 2 showed that a 4 Hz scan is smeared by the vehicle's own motion. Correcting it with a constant-velocity model is reported to reduce the absolute trajectory error by 29% for such a sensor. <span class="src">§9.2.5 · p. 268</span> The recipe: take the relative pose between the last two scans and their time stamps, divide to get a velocity, and move every point according to its own time stamp.
@@ 81

@@ 85
<strong>"Doppler odometry needs matching between scans."</strong> It needs none. The velocity comes from a single scan, which is why the book calls it correspondence-free.
@@ 86
<strong>"More points always fix a bad fit."</strong> Not if they are moving objects. Sixty points with fifteen outliers gave an error above 4 m/s in the widget. Reject first, then fit.
@@ 87
<strong>"A radar gives the full 6-DoF motion from Doppler."</strong> Doppler gives linear velocity. Rotation needs a gyroscope, a kinematic assumption with a known mounting offset, or scan matching.
@@ 88
<strong>"CFEAR and CFAR are the same thing."</strong> CFAR is a detection filter (Day 2). CFEAR is an odometry method.

Q1. A sensor drives straight ahead at 12 m/s. What closing speed does it measure for a static point at azimuth 60°?
@@ 90
The reading is the projection on the direction to the point: 12 × cos 60° = 6 m/s.

Q5. A radar rotates at 4 Hz on a vehicle driving at 20 m/s. Without motion compensation, how far apart are the positions from which the first and last azimuth of one scan were taken?
@@ 110
One rotation takes 1/4 s. In that time the vehicle moves 20 × 0.25 = 5 m.
@@ 111
A static point's Doppler reading is the sensor velocity projected on the direction to the point. Across azimuth the readings form a cosine.
@@ 112
Stacking the directions gives a linear least-squares problem for the velocity. Its covariance \(\sigma^2(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A})^{-1}\) shows which components the field of view supports.
@@ 113
Moving objects are outliers. RANSAC or a robust loss removes them, and the rejected points identify the movers.
@@ 114
Rotation is not observable from one radar's Doppler alone. Add a gyroscope, a kinematic model, or scan matching.
