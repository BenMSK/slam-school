A measurement becomes a factor once it has a residual and a covariance. For leg odometry the covariance has two sources: encoder noise, pushed through the kinematics with the Jacobian, and slip, modeled as noise on the contact point's velocity. With those in hand, leg odometry sits in the factor graph next to the IMU factor of Week 16, and the graph, not the legs alone, decides what to believe when the ground is soft or slippery.

2. Filter or factor graph
@@ 24
A walking controller needs the state at a high rate, so legged robots have mostly used Kalman filters (EKF, UKF, invariant EKF) that fuse IMU and kinematics. A Kalman filter needs a process model; when there is none, a constant-velocity model or IMU propagation stands in for it. A factor graph treats process and measurement models alike, as relations between states and measurements, which makes it the more general tool. With only two consecutive states in the graph it behaves like a filter. With a longer window it can also correct past states. Keeping the number of states small over a long window calls for <strong>preintegration</strong>, exactly as for the IMU in <a href="#d94">Week 16 · Day 4</a>. So far, graph-based estimators on legged robots have mostly served mapping at lower rates rather than control. <span class="src">§12.4, §12.4.2 · pp. 348–350</span>

Filling the gap: why the covariance is multiplied by \(\Delta t_{ij}\)
@@ 38
The book models slip as zero-mean Gaussian noise on the <em>velocity</em> of the contact point. Position is the integral of velocity, and the integral of white noise is a random walk: its variance grows in proportion to elapsed time (the same law as the drift in <a href="#d1">Week 1 · Day 1</a> and the integrated IMU noise of Week 16). So \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_v\) is a rate, in m²/s, and the position variance after a stance of length \(\Delta t_{ij}\) is \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_v\,\Delta t_{ij}\). Example: \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_v = (0.02)^2\mathbf{I}\) m²/s and a stance of 0.25 s give a standard deviation of \(0.02\sqrt{0.25} = 0.01\) m. The factor lets the contact point creep by about 1 cm before it objects. A longer stance is allowed more creep.
@@ 39
One limit: this factor is valid only for the same stance leg, within one stance phase. Handling the switch from one leg to the other needed follow-up work. <span class="src">§12.4.2.1 · p. 351</span>

Now the right side needs only \(\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\), the rotation since time \(i\), which is the preintegrated gyroscope rotation of Week 16. @@ 47
For the covariance, each term of the sum adds \(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_v\mathbf{A}\T\) with \(\mathbf{A} = \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\Delta t\), which is how the book builds \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{V},ij}\) in (12.38). If \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_v = \sigma_v^2\mathbf{I}\), the rotations cancel and \(n\) samples give \(n\,\Delta t^2\sigma_v^2\,\mathbf{I}\). Example: \(\sigma_v = 0.05\) m/s per sample, \(\Delta t = 2.5\) ms, \(n = 100\) samples (0.25 s): \(\sqrt{100} \times 0.0025 \times 0.05 = 1.25\) mm.

5. Fusing with other sensors, and what still breaks
@@ 50
In a SLAM system, leg odometry's job is to supply low-drift edges between consecutive nodes, so that loop closures have less to correct. Cameras and LiDARs are added as more measurements (in a filter) or more factors (in a graph). The difficulty is that the sources differ in rate, noise, and failure behaviour. A <strong>loosely coupled</strong> system runs separate estimators (visual-inertial, legged-inertial, LiDAR-inertial) in parallel and picks the best output. A <strong>tightly coupled</strong> system puts all factors in one graph; then the triage happens per factor: a failed modality's factor is simply not added, and robust cost functions (<a href="#d34">Week 6 · Day 4</a>) absorb noise that is not zero-mean Gaussian. <span class="src">§12.4.3 · p. 353</span>

Filling the gap: why sinking looks like climbing
@@ 68
Take the upright planar robot, hip at height 0.65 m, and let contact be declared when the foot first touches: the contact point is fixed at height \(c_z = 0\). The ground then gives way by 3 cm. The leg extends to keep pushing, so the foot's height in the base frame goes from \(-0.65\) to \(-0.68\) m. Leg odometry computes the body height as \(t_z = c_z - f_{p,z} = 0 - (-0.68) = 0.68\) m. The body did not move, yet it is reported 3 cm higher. At the next touchdown the new contact point is placed 3 cm too high as well, and the error repeats: after 10 such steps the robot believes it has climbed 30 cm on level ground.
@@ 69
Recent trends: learned contact detectors that replace the force threshold; locomotion controllers trained by reinforcement learning, which need only body velocity and the direction of gravity (both observable from inertial data), although skills that must reach a goal position, such as parkour, still need odometry; and humanoids, whose hands, arms, and trunk also make intermittent contact and whose compliance breaks the rigid-contact assumption. <span class="src">§12.6 · pp. 355–356</span>

<strong>"Encoder noise is the main error in leg odometry."</strong> It is the easiest to model. Slip, soft ground, and bending legs are biases, and they are usually larger.
@@ 71
<strong>"The contact factor says the foot is fixed."</strong> It says the foot is fixed up to a random walk whose variance grows with the stance time.
