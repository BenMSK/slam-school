2. The cost, and why it is a pose graph
@@ 23
When a loop closure arrives, all transformations are re-estimated by minimizing <span class="src">§16.3.3 · p. 481</span>
@@ 24
The first sum is ordinary pose-graph optimization over the relative-pose measurements \(\mathbf{Z}_{ij}\). The second keeps the mesh locally rigid: if the patch around node \(k\) moved as a rigid body, neighbour \(l\) would be at \(\mathbf{R}^M_k(\mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_k) + \mathbf{t}^M_k\), and the term penalizes its distance from where \(l\) actually is. The third does the same between a pose and the mesh nodes it saw, with \(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{il} = \tilde{\mathbf{R}}_i\T(\mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_i)\) the original position of node \(l\) in the frame of pose \(i\). Before deformation every rigidity term is zero.

The book defines \(\mathbf{G}_{kl} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_3 & \mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_k\\ \mathbf{0} & 1\end{bmatrix}\) and claims the second sum equals \(\sum \norm{\mathbf{M}_k\mathbf{G}_{kl} - \mathbf{M}_l}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\). Multiply out, writing \(\mathbf{M}_k\) as a \(4\times4\) matrix:
@@ 27
The last column is exactly the rigidity residual. The weighted Frobenius norm of (16.28), \(\norm{\mathbf{A}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} = \tr(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{A}\T)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \diag(\omega_R\mathbf{I}_3, \omega_t)\), weights the squared entries of the first three columns by \(\omega_R\) and those of the last column by \(\omega_t\). Setting \(\omega_R = 0\) on mesh edges leaves \(\omega_t\) times the squared rigidity residual, and the third sum works the same way. So every term has the form \(\norm{\mathbf{T}_i\mathbf{E}_{ij} - \mathbf{T}_j}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{ij}}\), equation (16.31): one pose graph over \(n + m\) nodes, for the solvers of <a href="#d77">Week 13 · Day 5</a>.

5. Hierarchical graphs and 3D scene graphs
@@ 37
A graph is <strong>hierarchical</strong> if its nodes split into layers \(1, \dots, l\) such that (1) a node has at most one parent in the layer above, (2) edges between layers join consecutive layers only, and (3) children of different parents are never connected. Then the descendants of two nodes of the same layer never mix, and the treewidth of the whole graph is bounded by <span class="src">Definition 16.2, §16.4.1 · p. 485</span>
@@ 38
where \(\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{C}(v)]\) is the subgraph formed by the children of \(v\).

Filling the gap: what treewidth buys
@@ 40
<strong>Treewidth</strong> measures how far a graph is from a tree: a tree has treewidth 1, a fully connected graph on \(n\) nodes has \(n - 1\). With the best elimination order (<a href="#d15">Week 3 · Day 3</a>), the largest clique created has treewidth \(+\,1\) variables, and exact inference over discrete variables costs exponentially in that number but only linearly in the number of nodes. So (16.37) says the cost is set by the most tangled group of siblings, such as the objects of one cluttered room, and not by the size of the building.
@@ 41
A <strong>3D scene graph</strong> is a graph \(\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})\) whose nodes carry features that always include a position, and whose edges carry features too. Its layers are typically objects, places (a sparse model of free space), rooms, and buildings. Edges between layers mean containment. Edges inside a layer mean relations such as "on top of" or "nearby", some of them directed. Relations such as "left of", common in 2D scene graphs, depend on the viewpoint and mean little in 3D. <span class="src">§16.4.2 · pp. 486–487</span>
