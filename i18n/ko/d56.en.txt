Shor's relaxation lifts a vector \(\mathbf{x}\) to the matrix \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T\), which holds every product of two unknowns, so it can express any quadratic. If the cost has degree 3 or 4, lift a longer vector that also contains the products themselves. This is the <strong>moment relaxation</strong>. It is again an SDP and again gives a certificate, but the SDP is larger and degenerate, so the fast solver of Week 9 no longer applies.

1. Anisotropic noise makes the cost quartic
@@ 12
Real sensors are rarely equally uncertain in all directions. A stereo camera, for example, locates a landmark much less precisely along the viewing direction than across it. The landmark model becomes \(\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}=\mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{m}_k-\mathbf{t}_i)+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{ik}\) with \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{ik}\sim\N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{W}_{ik})\), where the covariance \(\mathbf{W}_{ik}\) is <strong>anisotropic</strong>: not a multiple of the identity. Filling the gap: why (6.36) is the truncated quadratic
@@ 23
Write \(a=\mu_{ik}\norm{\cdot}^2\ge 0\). For fixed poses the term \(w\,a+(1-w)\beta^2=\beta^2+w\,(a-\beta^2)\) is a straight line in \(w\), so its minimum over \([0,1]\) is at an end point: \(w=1\) (cost \(a\)) if \(a\lt\beta^2\), and \(w=0\) (cost \(\beta^2\)) otherwise. The minimum is \(\min(a,\beta^2)\), the truncated quadratic. So \(w=1\) labels the measurement an inlier and \(w=0\) an outlier. With numbers: \(\beta^2=4\) and \(a=1.5\) give \(w=1\), cost 1.5; \(a=9\) gives \(w=0\), cost 4.
@@ 24

3. Polynomial optimization and the moment matrix
@@ 36
A <strong>polynomial optimization problem (POP)</strong> minimizes a polynomial \(p(\mathbf{x})\) subject to polynomial equalities \(h_i(\mathbf{x})=0\) and inequalities \(g_i(\mathbf{x})\le 0\). QCQPs are the special case of degree 2. POPs are hard in general; the reason to write SLAM this way is that a standard recipe, the <strong>moment relaxation</strong> (also called Lasserre's relaxation), turns any POP into an SDP. <span class="src">§6.1.4.2 · p. 171</span>
@@ 37
The recipe, for two unknowns \(\mathbf{x}=[x_1;x_2]\) and degree up to 4: collect every monomial of degree up to \(r=2\) in a vector, and take its outer product. <span class="src">§6.1.4.2 · p. 172</span>
@@ 38
\(\mathbf{X}_4\) is the <strong>moment matrix</strong>. Its entries are all monomials of degree up to 4, so any polynomial of degree up to 4 is a <em>linear</em> function \(\tr(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}_4)\) of it. The POP becomes "minimize \(\tr(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X})\) subject to \(\tr(\mathbf{H}_i\mathbf{X})=0\) and \(\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{x}]_2[\mathbf{x}]_2\T\)" (6.40). Exactly as in Shor's relaxation (<a href="#d50">Week 9 · Day 2</a>), the last condition is the same as \(\mathbf{X}\succeq 0\) with \(\rank(\mathbf{X})=1\); drop the rank condition and an SDP is left. The number \(r\) is the <strong>order</strong> of the relaxation.
@@ 39
One thing is new. Some monomials sit in more than one cell: \(x_1x_2\) is both entry \((1,5)\), as \(1\cdot x_1x_2\), and entry \((2,3)\), as \(x_1\cdot x_2\). For a true outer product these cells are equal automatically. Once the rank condition is dropped they are not, so the relaxation must state \(X_{15}=X_{23}\) explicitly. Such equalities are <strong>redundant constraints</strong>: they add nothing to the original problem but tighten the relaxation. A second family comes from the constraints: if \(h_i(\mathbf{x})=0\) then also \(x_1h_i(\mathbf{x})=0\) and \(x_2h_i(\mathbf{x})=0\). <span class="src">§6.1.4.2 · p. 172</span>
@@ 40
Filling the gap: how many redundant equalities?
@@ 41
There are \(\binom{n+r}{r}\) monomials of degree up to \(r\) in \(n\) variables: \(\binom{4}{2}=6\) here, which is the size of \([\mathbf{x}]_2\). A symmetric \(6\times 6\) matrix has \(6\cdot 7/2=21\) free entries, but only \(\binom{2+4}{4}=15\) distinct monomials of degree up to 4 exist. So \(21-15=6\) equalities are needed: one each for \(x_1^2\), \(x_1x_2\), \(x_2^2\), \(x_1^2x_2\), \(x_1x_2^2\), \(x_1^2x_2^2\). For example \(x_1^2x_2^2\) is entry \((4,6)\) and entry \((5,5)\).
@@ 42
The matrix grows fast. Shor's relaxation of a problem with \(n=1000\) unknowns uses a matrix of side 1001. The order-2 moment matrix has side \(\binom{1002}{2}=501{,}501\). This is why the book mentions sparse monomial bases, which keep only some of the monomials.

Filling the gap: a lower bound you can verify
@@ 47
Suppose you find numbers \(\gamma,\lambda\) such that this matrix is positive semidefinite:
@@ 48
Multiply out \([1\ \ x\ \ x^2]\,\mathbf{Q}\,[1\ \ x\ \ x^2]\T=-\gamma+bx+(2\lambda-a-2\lambda)x^2+x^4=p(x)-\gamma\). A positive semidefinite \(\mathbf{Q}\) makes the left side non-negative, so \(p(x)\ge\gamma\) for every \(x\): \(\gamma\) is a certified lower bound. The same computation with \(\mathbf{Y}\) in place of the outer product gives \(\tr(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Y})=y_4-ay_2+by_1-\gamma\ge 0\), so \(\gamma\le d^*\) too. The free number \(\lambda\) exists only because \(x^2\) sits in two cells; it is the Lagrange multiplier of the redundant constraint.
@@ 49
By hand for \(a=2\), \(b=0\): choose \(\lambda=1\), \(\gamma=-1\). Then \(\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}\T\) with \(\mathbf{v}=[1;0;-1]\), which is positive semidefinite, and indeed \(x^4-2x^2+1=(x^2-1)^2\ge 0\). So \(p(x)\ge -1\), and \(p(\pm 1)=-1\) shows the bound is attained.

Repeating the construction with monomials up to degree \(r=3,4,\dots\) gives a <strong>hierarchy</strong> of larger and tighter SDPs. Under mild assumptions the relaxation becomes exact at some finite order. For SLAM with anisotropic noise or outliers, experiments find it exact already at low order, even with a sparse monomial basis. <span class="src">§6.1.4.2 · p. 172</span>
@@ 73
The catch is the solver. An SDP with redundant constraints is <strong>degenerate</strong>, and that breaks the Riemannian Staircase in two ways: the constraint qualification conditions its convergence proof needs typically fail, and the dual problem has infinitely many solutions. Solvers built for moment relaxations exist but are still slow next to local solvers. <span class="src">§6.1.4.2 · pp. 172–173</span>

Intuition: why redundancy hurts a solver
@@ 75
A <strong>constraint qualification</strong> is a regularity condition on the constraints; the most common one asks that their gradients at the solution be linearly independent. Redundant constraints are implied by the others, which is the kind of dependence that violates such conditions. And when constraints are dependent, the Lagrange multipliers that certify optimality are not unique: weight can be moved from one constraint to an equivalent one. Algorithms that track "the" multiplier then have no single target.
@@ 76
<strong>"Redundant constraints can be dropped."</strong> They are redundant for the original problem only. In the relaxation they are what ties the cells of the moment matrix together; without them the bound gets worse.
@@ 77
<strong>"A higher order is always the answer."</strong> It is tighter, but the matrix side grows like \(\binom{n+r}{r}\). Practical work lowers the size instead (sparse bases).
@@ 78
<strong>"The weights w must be 0 or 1 by constraint."</strong> (6.36) only asks \(w\in[0,1]\). The optimum lands on 0 or 1 by itself because the cost is linear in \(w\).
