In bundle adjustment every measurement involves exactly one camera and one point. That makes the point part of the Hessian block diagonal, so the points can be removed from the linear system almost for free. What remains is a small system in the cameras alone, the <strong>reduced camera system</strong>. Solve it, then recover every point by a tiny separate solve.

3. Why this is cheap, and what fills in
@@ 39
Two facts make the trick pay.
@@ 40
<strong>\(\mathbf{H}_{pp}^{-1}\) is nearly free.</strong> A block-diagonal matrix is inverted block by block: one \(3 \times 3\) inverse per point, never a large factorization. The same holds for the back-substitution. In the book's words, both can be done "point by point". <span class="src">§7.4.3 · p. 213</span>
@@ 41
<strong>The remaining system is small.</strong> With 100 keyframes and 20,000 points the full system has \(600 + 60{,}000 = 60{,}600\) unknowns. The reduced camera system has 600.
@@ 42
The price is <strong>fill-in</strong> (<a href="#d15">Week 3 · Day 3</a>): \(\mathbf{H}_{cc}\) was block diagonal, and \(\mathbf{H}^{red}_{cc}\) is not. Write out one block of the product, using that \(\mathbf{H}_{pp}\) is block diagonal:
@@ 43
The term for point \(j\) is non-zero only if camera \(i\) <em>and</em> camera \(k\) both observe it. So block \((i, k)\) of the reduced camera system is non-zero exactly when the two cameras share at least one point. Its sparsity pattern is the covisibility graph of <a href="#d67">Day 1</a> with threshold one. That explains Figure 7.10: a block between cameras 1 and 2, none between cameras 1 and 3. <span class="src">§7.4.3 · p. 213</span>
@@ 44
A point seen by two cameras is a rigid link between them: move one camera and the point must move, which pulls on the other. Eliminating the point does not remove the link. It replaces "camera – point – camera" by a direct "camera – camera" constraint of the same strength. This is the marginalization of Week 3: the reduced camera system is the information the measurements carry about the cameras alone, whatever the points turn out to be.

5. Dense or sparse: local BA and full BA
@@ 50
The reduced camera system still has to be solved, and its structure decides how. In <strong>local BA</strong> the keyframes were chosen because they see the same points, so almost every pair shares some: the reduced system is nearly full and a dense solver is the right tool. <strong>Full BA</strong> has many more keyframes, but most pairs are far apart and share nothing: the reduced system is large and sparse, and a sparse solver (the sparse Cholesky of Week 3) pays off. <span class="src">§7.4.3 · p. 213</span>

<strong>"The Schur complement approximates the solution."</strong> It is exact. It is Gaussian elimination done in a clever order; the widget shows the difference from a direct solve at rounding level.
@@ 66
<strong>"\(\mathbf{H}_{pp}\) is diagonal."</strong> It is <em>block</em> diagonal: the three coordinates of one point are coupled with each other, not with other points.
@@ 67
<strong>"Eliminating the points means the points are not optimized."</strong> They are updated in step 3 of every iteration. Elimination only changes the order in which the unknowns are solved.
@@ 68
<strong>"Eliminate the cameras instead; there are fewer of them."</strong> The cost is set by what remains. Eliminating cameras would leave a huge, filled-in system in the points.
