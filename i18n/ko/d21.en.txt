Keep the pose as a matrix, but let the unknown of each Gauss-Newton iteration be a small vector \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) in the Lie algebra. Linearize in \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) around \(\boldsymbol{\xi}=\mathbf{0}\), solve an ordinary unconstrained least-squares problem, then push the answer back onto the group with \(\mathbf{T} \leftarrow \mathbf{T}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})\). The estimate never leaves \(\SE(3)\).

1. The running example: where is the camera?
@@ 12
A camera sees landmarks whose world positions \(\boldsymbol{\ell}^w_i\) are known. The camera model \(\mathbf{h}_i\) takes a homogeneous point in the <em>camera</em> frame and returns its pixel coordinates \(\mathbf{z}_i\in\R^2\). The point first has to be brought into the camera frame, so the measurement model is <span class="src">§2.1.3 · p. 57, eq. (2.8)</span>
@@ 13
Read the indices as on Day 1: \(\mathbf{T}^c_w\) converts world coordinates into camera coordinates. Finding the pose that best explains all pixel measurements is the <strong>perspective-n-point (PnP)</strong> problem: <span class="src">§2.1.3 · p. 57, eq. (2.9)</span>
@@ 14
This is the nonlinear least-squares problem of <a href="#d8">Week 2 · Day 2</a> with one difference: the unknown is a member of \(\SE(3)\), not a vector. The Gauss-Newton recipe of <a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a>, "linearize, solve, add the step", cannot add a step to \(\mathbf{T}\). To keep the formulas short, write \(\mathbf{T}^0\) for the current guess (the book writes \(\mathbf{T}^{c^0}_w\)) and \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_i\) for \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^w_i\).

2. Perturb the pose through its Lie algebra
@@ 16
Describe every pose near the guess as the guess times a small motion: <span class="src">§2.1.3 · p. 57, eqs. (2.10)–(2.11)</span>
@@ 17
The symbol \(\oplus\) is shorthand for "multiply on the right by Exp of". Two things make this the right substitute for \(\mathbf{x}^0 + \boldsymbol{\Delta}\). First, closure: \(\mathbf{T}^0\) and \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) are both in \(\SE(3)\), so their product is too, for <em>any</em> \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\). Second, \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) has exactly six entries, one per degree of freedom, so there is nothing to constrain.
@@ 18
For a small \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\), keep only the first two terms of the series \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi}) = \mathbf{I} + \boldsymbol{\xi}^\wedge + \tfrac12(\boldsymbol{\xi}^\wedge)^2 + \dots\): <span class="src">§2.1.3 · p. 57, eq. (2.12)</span>
@@ 19
How good is that? In the plane with \(\theta = 0.1\) rad, \(\Exp(\theta)\) has entries \(\cos 0.1 = 0.995\) and \(\sin 0.1 = 0.0998\), while \(\mathbf{I} + \theta^\wedge\) has \(1\) and \(0.1\). The error is about \(\theta^2/2 = 0.005\): second order, which is what a linearization is allowed to drop.

@@ 31
The third step swaps a cross product: \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\wedge\boldsymbol{\ell} = \boldsymbol{\theta}\times\boldsymbol{\ell} = -\boldsymbol{\ell}\times\boldsymbol{\theta} = -\boldsymbol{\ell}^\wedge\boldsymbol{\theta}\). The result is a 4 × 6 matrix times \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\). In words: a small motion \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) moves the point by \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\) (the translation) plus \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\times\boldsymbol{\ell}\) (the rotation swings it about the origin).
@@ 32
The pose is now linear in \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\), but the camera function is still nonlinear. A first-order Taylor expansion of \(\mathbf{h}_i\) around the point \(\mathbf{T}^0\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_i\) finishes the job: <span class="src">§2.1.3 · p. 58, eq. (2.16)</span>
@@ 33
\(\mathbf{H}_i\) is a chain rule: (how pixels move when the point moves in the camera frame) × (how the point moves in the camera frame when the pose is perturbed). The sizes are 2 × 4, then 4 × 4 times 4 × 6, giving a 2 × 6 Jacobian: two pixel coordinates against six degrees of freedom. Equation (2.17) splits the same Jacobian into four smaller factors, each a reusable building block. That is how libraries and automatic differentiation assemble it.

5. Names and variations
@@ 45
The book steps back and names what just happened. <span class="src">§2.1.3 · p. 59</span> Working in the Lie algebra constrains each step to be <strong>tangent</strong> to the manifold. Mapping the step back with (2.19) is a <strong>retraction</strong>. The whole scheme is an instance of <strong>Riemannian optimization</strong>, which also works on manifolds that are not Lie groups and with retractions other than the exponential.

@@ 56
<strong>"ξ is the pose."</strong> \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) is only the correction of the current iteration. It starts at zero every time, and \(\mathbf{T}^0\) carries the estimate.
@@ 57
<strong>"The update can use \(\mathbf{T}^0(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\xi}^\wedge)\), since ξ is small."</strong> That matrix is not in the group. The first-order form is for deriving \(\mathbf{H}_i\). The update uses Exp.
@@ 58
<strong>"H is the derivative with respect to the entries of T."</strong> It is the derivative with respect to the six entries of \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\), evaluated at \(\boldsymbol{\xi}=\mathbf{0}\).
@@ 59
<strong>"Left or right does not matter."</strong> Both work, but a Jacobian derived for one side with an update on the other gives wrong steps.

Q1. In the 3D camera example, what is the size of the Jacobian \(\mathbf{H}_i\) of one pixel measurement?
@@ 61
Two pixel coordinates, six entries in \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\). The product is (2 × 4)(4 × 4)(4 × 6) = 2 × 6.

Q4. Planar example, camera \(u = p_2/p_1\), current guess \(\mathbf{T}^0 = \mathbf{I}\), landmark \(\boldsymbol{\ell} = (2,0)\). What is \(\mathbf{H}\)?
@@ 67
\(\partial h/\partial\mathbf{p} = [-p_2/p_1^2,\ 1/p_1] = [0,\ 0.5]\) and \(\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\ell} = (0, 2)\), so \(\mathbf{H} = [0,\ 0.5]\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 2\end{bmatrix} = [0,\ 0.5,\ 1]\). Moving the point forward does not change \(u\), moving it sideways changes \(u\) by half as much, and turning changes \(u\) one for one.
