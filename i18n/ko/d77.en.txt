@@ 12
A LiDAR SLAM system is odometry plus memory. Odometry gives a locally consistent trajectory that drifts. Place recognition notices a revisit, registration measures it, and <strong>pose-graph optimization</strong> bends the whole past trajectory so that odometry and loop closures agree as well as they can. Then the map, which was drawn with the old poses, has to be redrawn or deformed to match.

1. Four modules at two rates
@@ 14
Most current systems are built from the four modules of Figure 8.7: odometry, loop closure detection, pose-graph optimization, and map update. Odometry runs at the sensor rate, for example 10 Hz, and the others more slowly, for example 1 Hz. <span class="src">§8.4.1 · p. 242</span>
@@ 15 attr
Two lanes. In the fast lane, run for every scan at about 10 hertz, a scan enters odometry, which registers it to the local map. In the slow lane, at about 1 hertz, loop closure detection passes loop edges to pose-graph optimization, which also receives odometry edges, and sends corrected poses to the map update. The corrected map and pose flow back to odometry.

2. From a candidate to a constraint
@@ 36
Place recognition only says that two scans look alike. A loop closure needs a number: the relative transformation between the two poses. That takes three more steps. <span class="src">§8.4.1 · p. 242</span>
@@ 37
<strong>Initial guess.</strong> In a small pose graph the current estimates of the two poses give a geometric prior. Otherwise the guess comes from the place-recognition method itself (yesterday's awareness), or from a global registration method that needs no guess and tolerates low overlap.
@@ 38
<strong>Fine registration.</strong> ICP between the two scans, started from that guess, yields the relative pose.
@@ 39
<strong>Verification.</strong> Heuristics reject doubtful candidates: the travel distance or time between the two visits, or the confidence of a RANSAC-based alignment (<a href="#d32">Week 6 · Day 2</a>).
@@ 40
A false loop closure is worse than a missed one. It ties together two places that are not the same and bends the whole map to fit. For the ones that slip through, robust pose-graph optimization can down-weight or disable individual constraints (<a href="#d31">Week 6</a>). <span class="src">§8.4.2 · p. 244</span>
@@ 41
3. Pose-graph optimization
@@ 42
The back-end is a factor graph whose variables are sensor poses and whose factors are relative-pose constraints, hence <strong>pose graph</strong>. Figure 8.8 shows the factor types: a prior that fixes the origin, odometry factors between consecutive poses, loop-closure factors between distant ones, and optional attitude factors that constrain roll and pitch when an inertial sensor is available. <span class="src">§8.4.2 · p. 243</span> A relative-pose measurement \(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{ij}\) between poses \(\mathbf{T}_i\) and \(\mathbf{T}_j\) contributes the residual \(\mathbf{e}_{ij} = \Log\big(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{ij}^{-1}\,\mathbf{T}_i^{-1}\mathbf{T}_j\big)\), and <strong>pose-graph optimization (PGO)</strong> minimizes \(\sum \mathbf{e}_{ij}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}^{-1}\mathbf{e}_{ij}\) over all poses, by the methods of Weeks 2 to 4.

Each step absorbs a share of the disagreement in proportion to its variance. <strong>Numbers:</strong> three steps \(u = (10, 10, 10)\) m with variances \((1, 1, 4)\), and an accurate loop closure, \(z = 29.4\) m with \(\sigma_L^2 \approx 0\). Then \(e = -0.6\) and \(\delta = -0.6 \times (\tfrac16, \tfrac16, \tfrac46) = (-0.1, -0.1, -0.4)\). The corrected positions are \(0,\ 9.9,\ 19.8,\ 29.4\). Every pose moved, not only the last, and the least trusted step moved most.
@@ 48
This is why the book cares about the covariances on the edges: giving a high covariance to stretches where drift is likely to be high makes the optimizer put the correction there. <span class="src">§8.4.2 · p. 244</span> It is also why §8.5 lists uncertainty as unsolved. Estimated odometry uncertainty is often unreliable, so many systems fall back on fixed covariances, which spreads the error evenly whether or not that is where it arose. <span class="src">§8.5 · p. 249</span>
@@ 49
<strong>Keeping it real-time.</strong> The graph grows for as long as the robot runs, but it is sparse, with few edges per node, and sparse factorization with good ordering and incremental relinearization (iSAM2, <a href="#d17">Week 3</a>) updates graphs of over 1000 nodes in a fraction of a second. <span class="src">§8.4.2 · p. 243</span> Two habits keep the node count down. <span class="src">§8.4.2 · pp. 243–244</span>
@@ 50
Add a node every few metres travelled, not for every scan.
@@ 51
Group the trajectory into <strong>submaps</strong> of fixed size, say 30 m of travel, with one node each, and treat the odometry inside a submap as exact.
@@ 52
Count it for a robot that walks at 1 m/s for one hour, 3.6 km. One node per scan at 10 Hz is 36,000 nodes. One node every 2 m is 1,800. One submap per 30 m is 120. This is how pose-graph SLAM reaches city scale.

5. Several sessions, several robots, and what is still open
@@ 71
Taking the finished clouds of two separate runs and aligning them rigidly does not work well: each run keeps its own small internal errors, about a metre per kilometre, so walls double up wherever those errors differ (Figure 8.10). The sessions have to be optimized jointly. <span class="src">§8.4.3 · p. 245</span>
@@ 72
Filling the gap: what an anchor node does
@@ 73
Let each session \(s\) keep its poses \(\mathbf{T}^s_i\) in its own coordinate frame, and add one extra variable per session, the <strong>anchor</strong> \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_s \in \SE(3)\), which places that frame in a common global frame: the global pose is \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_s\mathbf{T}^s_i\). Factors inside a session do not see the anchor, because \((\boldsymbol{\Delta}_s\mathbf{T}^s_i)^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_s\mathbf{T}^s_j) = \mathbf{T}^{s\,-1}_i\mathbf{T}^s_j\). A loop closure between pose \(i\) of session \(a\) and pose \(j\) of session \(b\) measures
@@ 74
and is the only kind of factor that involves the anchors. Until the first such factor exists, \(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_b\) is completely free: the sessions float relative to each other. Since there is no geometric prior between disconnected sessions, that first link can only come from place recognition. <span class="src">§8.4.3 · pp. 245–246</span>
@@ 75
<strong>Multi-robot SLAM</strong> does the same with data arriving live from several robots, so that a team can share one map and plan together. A <strong>centralized</strong> system sends measurements to a base station that computes the combined map; a <strong>decentralized</strong> (distributed) one builds a map on every robot and merges the robot maps at a base station. <span class="src">§8.4.3.1 · p. 246</span> Two competitions mark the progress. At MAGIC in 2010, the winning team ran 14 wheeled robots with 2D LiDAR in a 500 m × 500 m area; each sent pose-graph constraints to a base station that built one 2D map. At the DARPA Subterranean Challenge in 2021, robots with 3D LiDAR and further odometry sources ran pose-graph SLAM on board in tunnels with stairs and ramps, and a base station merged the maps; one system sent compressed surfel submaps, 21.5 MB per robot for a complete map. <span class="src">§8.4.3.1 · pp. 247–248</span>
@@ 76
The hardest version is fully distributed SLAM, where every robot maintains the joint map under communication limits, and consistency is the central issue. <span class="src">§8.4.3.1 · p. 248</span> The chapter closes with four open areas. <span class="src">§8.5 · pp. 248–249</span>
@@ 77
<strong>Robustness.</strong> Cluttered scenes, featureless corridors and mines, snow, fog, and dust still break systems, and performance depends on hand tuning. Day 2's singular 6 × 6 matrix is the corridor case.
@@ 78
<strong>Multi-sensor fusion.</strong> Radar in rain or smoke, cameras in tunnels. Open questions are redundancy against computation, early (tightly coupled) against late fusion, and imperfect calibration and synchronization.
@@ 79
<strong>Uncertainty.</strong> Fusing estimates needs calibrated covariances, and those from ICP-based odometry are often unreliable.
@@ 80
<strong>Deployment.</strong> Size, weight, power, and latency matter: the most accurate system is not always the right one for a car that must react quickly.
@@ 81
<strong>"After a loop closure, snap the current pose to the old one."</strong> That fixes one pose and leaves a tear in the trajectory. The correction is distributed over all poses of the loop, in proportion to their uncertainty.
@@ 82
<strong>"Once the poses are optimized the map is correct."</strong> The map was built with the old poses. Without a map update the walls stay doubled.

@@ 92
δ₃ = e σ₃²/(σ₁² + σ₂² + σ₃²) = 0.8 × 2/4 = 0.4 m. The first two steps get 0.2 m each.
