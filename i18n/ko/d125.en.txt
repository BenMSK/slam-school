If every point may move freely in every frame, one camera does not provide enough equations to locate the points, let alone the camera. Deformable SLAM becomes solvable by assuming how surfaces deform: smoothly in time, like their neighbours, and without stretching. The rigid part of what the camera sees is then assigned to the camera's own motion and the rest to the scene.

1. The problem, and why it is under-constrained
@@ 11
If only part of the scene deforms, the robot localizes against the static background as on Day 2, and what remains is reconstruction of a deforming object with known sensor poses. <strong>Deformable SLAM</strong> is the general case with no visible rigid part. The motivating application is minimally invasive surgery, where a monocular camera sees only deforming organs. The related vision problem is <strong>non-rigid structure from motion (NRSfM)</strong>: a point has a different 3D position in every frame, so the map stores a 3D <em>trajectory</em> per point. <span class="src">§15.4 · p. 446</span>

2. From NRSfM to deformable SLAM
@@ 25
Classical NRSfM assumes a fixed camera and a moving object with no static background: a hand, a waving flag, a jumping person. Most frames show the whole object. Each point's motion mixes a rigid motion of the object with a local deformation; the rigid part is never computed separately, but it supplies parallax, which makes the estimation well conditioned. <span class="src">§15.4.1.1 · pp. 446–447</span>
@@ 26
<strong>Shape from template (SfT)</strong> adds a strong prior: the textured 3D shape of the object at rest, the <strong>template</strong>, is known, and only the deformation is recovered. One model is <strong>isometric</strong> deformation: the geodesic distance between surface points, the shortest path within the surface, is preserved. Paper bends this way; rubber does not. It is well-posed and led to stable real-time solutions; the alternative jointly minimizes a deformation energy and the reprojection error. Without a template, early NRSfM assumed an orthographic camera, valid only for distant objects, and a low-dimensional basis for the trajectories. Close-ups need a perspective camera, where isometry has also worked well. <span class="src">§15.4.1.1 · pp. 447–448</span>

3. With depth: a canonical map and a deformation graph
@@ 42
RGB-D and stereo cameras measure depth. DynamicFusion, which the book calls the seminal work on deformable 3D SLAM from RGB-D data, builds a <strong>canonical map</strong>, the scene's shape at rest, and deforms it to explain the current depth image, combining a form of <strong>embedded deformation (ED) graph</strong> with an <strong>as-rigid-as-possible</strong> regularizer. An ED model discretizes the space of deformations as a graph, which speeds up dense reconstruction. <span class="src">§15.4.2 · p. 449</span>
@@ 43
Filling the gap: what a deformation graph and "as rigid as possible" compute
@@ 44
A <strong>deformation graph</strong> is a sparse set of nodes spread over the surface. Node \(i\) has a rest position \(\mathbf{q}_i\), a current position \(\mathbf{p}_i\), and a rotation \(\mathbf{R}_i\); edges join nearby nodes. Each dense surface point follows a blend of the transforms of the nodes near it, so a few hundred node transforms stand in for millions of points.

Rotating the rest edges by \(36.9^\circ\) gives exactly the current edges, so this node contributes 0. The simpler prior "neighbours move alike", \(\sum \norm{(\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j) - (\mathbf{q}_i - \mathbf{q}_j)}^2\), gives \(\norm{(-0.2, 0.6)}^2 + \norm{(-0.6, -0.2)}^2 = 0.8\): it punishes a pure rotation that deforms nothing.
@@ 49
Minimizing \(E_{\text{ARAP}}\) alternates two easy steps. With positions fixed, each \(\mathbf{R}_i\) has the closed form above (in 3D, an SVD as in <a href="#d74">Week 13 · Day 2</a>). With rotations fixed, the energy is quadratic in the positions, and its gradient vanishes when \(\sum_{j}(\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j) = \sum_{j} \tfrac12(\mathbf{R}_i + \mathbf{R}_j)(\mathbf{q}_i - \mathbf{q}_j)\) at every free node, a linear system.

4. One camera: the tracking and mapping threads
@@ 54
A monocular deformable SLAM system keeps the two threads of visual SLAM (<a href="#d67">Week 12 · Day 1</a>): tracking at frame rate, mapping at keyframe rate. <strong>Tracking</strong> resembles shape from template: a shape at rest is assumed available for the area being explored, and each frame estimates the camera pose and the deformation of that local template. <strong>Mapping</strong> takes point tracks and recomputes the templates. <span class="src">§15.4.3–15.4.4 · pp. 450–451</span>

5. Where the field stands
@@ 74
§15.5 closes with open problems. <span class="src">§15.5 · pp. 451–453</span> Tracking objects helps state estimation under large occlusions and many moving objects, at higher cost. NeRF and 3D Gaussian splatting (<a href="#d115">Week 20</a>) show promise for dynamic scenes, with real-time cost and map management unsolved. Data association is still hard, and the trend is toward learned priors. Lifelong operation needs memory management: what to keep, how to summarize or marginalize, when to forget. For deformable SLAM the basic questions are open: under what conditions can camera trajectory and deformation both be estimated, can the object's rigid motion be told from the camera's, are there local minima? The stated goal is spatio-temporal AI, a world model that gives a consistent history and predicts what comes next.
@@ 75
<strong>"As rigid as possible means the object stays rigid."</strong> Each small neighbourhood should move rigidly, each with its own rotation. The whole can bend a great deal.
