@@ 25
\(p(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{x})\) is the likelihood, the derivatives are taken at \(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}}\), and the average is over all measurements the sensors could have produced. The second form reads: the FIM is the average <em>curvature</em> of the negative log-likelihood at the truth. A sharply curved log-likelihood pins the parameter down; a flat one cannot, whatever estimator you use. <span class="src">§6.2.1 · p. 174</span>
@@ 26
What the matrix inequality gives you in practice: taking \(\mathbf{v}\T(\cdot)\mathbf{v}\) with \(\mathbf{v}\) a unit coordinate vector, \(\operatorname{var}(\hat x_i)\ge[\mathcal{I}^{-1}]_{ii}\). Note the order of operations: invert first, then read the diagonal. For \(\mathcal{I}=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathcal{I}^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), so the bound on \(\operatorname{var}(\hat x_1)\) is \(0.667\), not \(1/2\). The value \(1/2\) would be right only if \(x_2\) were known.

Filling the gap: a proof sketch for one parameter
@@ 28
Define the <strong>score</strong> \(s(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log p(\mathbf{z};x)\), so that \(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}=p\,s\). Assume derivatives and integrals may be swapped (the book's "regularity conditions").
@@ 29
<em>Step 1: the score has mean zero.</em> \(\int p\,d\mathbf{z}=1\) for every \(x\). Differentiate: \(\int p\,s\,d\mathbf{z}=\E[s]=0\). Hence \(\operatorname{var}(s)=\E[s^2]=\mathcal{I}\), which is (6.42).
@@ 30
<em>Step 2: unbiasedness ties the estimator to the score.</em> Unbiased means \(\int\hat x(\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z};x)\,d\mathbf{z}=x\) for every \(x\). Differentiate both sides: \(\int\hat x\,p\,s\,d\mathbf{z}=\E[\hat x\,s]=1\). Subtract \(x\,\E[s]=0\): \(\E[(\hat x-x)\,s]=1\).

@@ 39
<strong>Example 1: averaging.</strong> \(N\) measurements \(z_k=x+\epsilon_k\) with variance \(\sigma^2\). Then \(\mathbf{J}\) is a column of \(N\) ones, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^2\mathbf{I}\), and \(\mathcal{I}=N/\sigma^2\). No unbiased estimator has variance below \(\sigma^2/N\). The sample mean has exactly that variance, so it cannot be improved.
@@ 40
<strong>Example 2: a range.</strong> A robot at position \(x\) on a line measures its distance to a beacon 3 m off the line: \(h(x)=\sqrt{x^2+9}\), with \(\sigma=0.1\) m. The Jacobian is \(J=x/\sqrt{x^2+9}\). At \(x=4\): \(J=0.8\), \(\mathcal{I}=0.8^2/0.1^2=64\), so the standard deviation of any unbiased estimate of \(x\) is at least \(1/\sqrt{64}=0.125\) m. At \(x=0\), directly beside the beacon, \(J=0\) and \(\mathcal{I}=0\): a small step along the line does not change the range to first order, so one range says nothing about \(x\) there.

Notice: with N = 10 and σ = 1 the bound is 0.1. The sample mean's variance is about 1.0 times the bound, and its histogram matches the black curve. @@ 43
<strong>"Nothing can have variance below the CRLB."</strong> Nothing <em>unbiased</em> can. An estimator that always answers "7" has variance zero.
@@ 44
<strong>"The bound on one variable is one over its diagonal entry of the FIM."</strong> It is the diagonal entry of the inverse, which is larger whenever the variable is coupled to others.
@@ 45
<strong>"A solver that reaches the global optimum beats the bound."</strong> The bound is about the data, not the solver. A certified optimum (Days 1–2) is the best use of the measurements; the CRLB says how good that best can be.

4. Maximum likelihood, and one number for a matrix
@@ 47
Under regularity conditions, as the number of measurements grows, the maximum likelihood estimator \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{mle}}\) becomes distributed as \(\N\big(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}},\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}})^{-1}\big)\): asymptotically it is unbiased and attains the bound. Since \(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}}\) is unknown, practice evaluates the FIM at the estimate and reports \(\mathcal{I}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{mle}})^{-1}\) as the covariance of the solution. <span class="src">§6.2.1 · pp. 174–175</span> This is the covariance SLAM libraries return.
@@ 48
To compare two sensor layouts or two candidate paths you need one number, not a matrix. The field of <strong>optimal experimental design</strong> supplies three standard choices, all functions of the eigenvalues of the FIM. <span class="src">§6.2.1 · p. 175</span>

Going deeper (optional): two caveats
@@ 69
<strong>Biased estimators.</strong> If the bias is \(b(x)\), the scalar bound becomes \(\operatorname{var}(\hat x)\ge(1+b'(x))^2/\mathcal{I}\). For "0.8 × sample mean", \(b(x)=-0.2x\), so the bound is \(0.64\,\sigma^2/N\), which is what the widget measures.
@@ 70
<strong>Rotations and poses.</strong> The classical bound assumes parameters in a Euclidean space. For rotations and poses there is a version with an extra term that depends on the curvature of the space; the book notes that it is often negligible when the noise is small compared with the signal (footnote 8, p. 174).
