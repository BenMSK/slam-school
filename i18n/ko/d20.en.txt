@@ 10
Every rotation or pose can be reached from the identity by moving at a constant velocity for one unit of time. The velocity lives in a flat vector space, the <strong>Lie algebra</strong>, and the matrix exponential carries out the motion. So an ordinary vector with as many entries as there are degrees of freedom always produces a valid group member, with no constraints to enforce.
@@ 11
1. What makes these sets groups
@@ 12
The book singles out one property: <strong>closure</strong>. The product of two members is again a member, \(\mathbf{R}^c_a = \mathbf{R}^c_b\mathbf{R}^b_a \in \SO(d)\). <span class="src">§2.1.2 · p. 55</span> You can check it with yesterday's constraints: \((\mathbf{R}_1\mathbf{R}_2)\T(\mathbf{R}_1\mathbf{R}_2) = \mathbf{R}_2\T\mathbf{R}_1\T\mathbf{R}_1\mathbf{R}_2 = \mathbf{I}\) and \(\det(\mathbf{R}_1\mathbf{R}_2) = 1\cdot 1\). A <strong>group</strong> also needs an identity (\(\mathbf{I}\)), an inverse for every member (\(\mathbf{R}\T\), or \(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\) from Day 1), and associativity (true for all matrix products). A <strong>matrix Lie group</strong> is a group of matrices that is also a smooth manifold.
@@ 13
One thing a group does <em>not</em> promise is that order is irrelevant. Planar rotations commute, but 3D rotations and poses do not: \(\mathbf{T}_1\mathbf{T}_2 \ne \mathbf{T}_2\mathbf{T}_1\) in general. Drive one meter then turn left, or turn left then drive one meter: you end in different places. This will matter in section 5.

2. The Lie algebra and the wedge operator
@@ 15
The <strong>Lie algebra</strong> of a Lie group is its tangent space at the identity. It is a vector space whose dimension equals the group's degrees of freedom. <span class="src">§2.1.2 · p. 55</span> For rotations it is written \(\so(d)\), for poses \(\se(d)\).
@@ 16
Filling the gap: why the tangent space of SO(d) is the skew-symmetric matrices
@@ 17

@@ 18
So every velocity through the identity is a matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\T = -\boldsymbol{\Omega}\): a <strong>skew-symmetric</strong> matrix. Its diagonal is zero and the lower triangle is minus the upper, which leaves one free entry in a 2 × 2 matrix and three in a 3 × 3. Those counts are the degrees of freedom of \(\SO(2)\) and \(\SO(3)\).
@@ 19
The <strong>wedge</strong> operator \((\cdot)^\wedge\) fills a skew-symmetric matrix from a plain vector, and <strong>vee</strong> \((\cdot)^\vee\) reads the vector back out: <span class="src">§2.1.2 · pp. 55–56, eqs. (2.5), (2.6b)</span>
@@ 20
In 3D the wedge matrix carries out a cross product: \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\wedge\mathbf{v} = \boldsymbol{\theta}\times\mathbf{v}\). <span class="src">Notation · p. xv</span> For example, \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = (0,0,1)\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (1,0,0)\) give \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\wedge\mathbf{v} = (0,1,0)\): the velocity of the tip of the x-axis when the body spins about z.

3. The exponential map, worked out for SO(2)
@@ 39
The map from the Lie algebra to the group is the <strong>matrix exponential</strong>, defined by the same series as the scalar one: <span class="src">§2.1.2 · p. 55, eq. (2.4)</span>
@@ 40
Why an exponential? A body spinning at constant angular velocity obeys \(\dot{\mathbf{R}}(t) = \boldsymbol{\omega}^\wedge\mathbf{R}(t)\) (the book writes this later as equation (2.32)). This is the matrix version of \(\dot{x} = a x\), whose solution is \(x(t) = e^{at}x(0)\). Starting at \(\mathbf{R}(0)=\mathbf{I}\), the solution is \(\mathbf{R}(t) = \exp(\boldsymbol{\omega}^\wedge t)\). \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) is where you are after one unit of time at angular velocity \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\).

5. Poses: Exp on SE(d)
@@ 53
For poses the tangent vector stacks a translational part \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\) on top of a rotational part \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\): <span class="src">§2.1.2 · p. 56, eq. (2.7)</span>
@@ 54
In the plane \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\R^2\), \(\theta\in\R\), and \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\R^3\). In 3D both parts are in \(\R^3\) and \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\R^6\). The book's order is translation first. Some other texts put rotation first, so check before you copy a formula.

Example: \(\boldsymbol{\xi} = (\rho_1,\rho_2,\theta) = (\tfrac{\pi}{2}, 0, \tfrac{\pi}{2})\). Both coefficients equal \(2/\pi\), so \(\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\rho} = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,(1,0) + \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,(0,1) = (1,1)\). The pose is at \((1,1)\), turned by 90°.
@@ 59
The meaning: \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\) is a velocity in the robot's <em>own</em> frame, and the frame turns while the robot moves. Driving forward at speed \(\pi/2\) while turning at rate \(\pi/2\) for one unit of time traces a quarter circle of radius 1, which ends at \((1,1)\). The path length is \(\pi/2\), but the end point is only \(\sqrt{2}\) away.

@@ 64
A tangent vector is a steering command: "this much forward, this much sideways, this much turn, held steady for one second". Exp is what happens when you carry out the command. Log looks at where you ended up and tells you which steady command would have taken you there.
@@ 65
<strong>"The first entries of ξ are the position."</strong> They are \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\). The position is \(\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\rho}\), and the two agree only when there is no rotation.
@@ 66
<strong>"exp of a matrix means exponentiating each entry."</strong> It is the power series in the whole matrix.
@@ 67
<strong>"Exp(a) Exp(b) = Exp(a + b)."</strong> Only when the two wedge matrices commute, for example for two planar rotations, or two 3D rotations about the same axis.
@@ 68
<strong>"Log is unique."</strong> Angles that differ by a full turn give the same group member. Log returns one representative.

The scalar proof of \(e^ae^b = e^{a+b}\) reorders products, which is allowed only for commuting matrices. All of \(\so(2)\) is multiples of one matrix \(\mathbf{S}\), so planar rotation angles simply add. In \(\SO(3)\) and \(\SE(d)\) the rule holds only in special cases, such as a shared rotation axis.
@@ 85
\(\SO(d)\) and \(\SE(d)\) are matrix Lie groups: closed under products and inverses, and smooth manifolds.
@@ 86
