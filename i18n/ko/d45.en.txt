2. Points and surfels
@@ 24
The simplest dense map is every point ever measured. Each scan \(\mathsf{P}_t\) lives in its own sensor frame, so it is first moved into the map frame with the pose \(\mathbf{T}^1_t\) that the back-end estimated, and then added to the map cloud: <span class="src">§5.3.2.1 · p. 127</span>
@@ 25
This grows without bound, since every revisit of a place adds the same wall again. The usual cure is to keep only a few points per small cube of space, or only scans chosen as keyframes. A second weakness is that a bare point says nothing about the surface it came from: once the viewpoint is forgotten, you cannot tell which side is inside. <span class="src">§5.3.2.1 · p. 127</span>
@@ 26
A <strong>surfel</strong> (surface element) repairs that. It is a small disc: a position \(\mathbf{p} \in \R^3\), a normal \(\mathbf{n} \in \R^3\), and a radius \(r\). Many nearby measurements are merged into one disc, which loses fine detail but saves memory: in the book's Figure 5.7 the accumulated point cloud takes 2.95 GB and the surfel map of the same drive 160 MB. <span class="src">§5.3.2.2 · pp. 128–129</span>
@@ 27
The <strong>normal distributions transform (NDT)</strong> is a close relative. Space is cut into voxels, and each voxel stores the mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) of the points that fell in it. If the eigenvalues of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) are \(\lambda_1 \lt \lambda_2 \lt \lambda_3\) and \(\lambda_1 \ll \lambda_2\), the points form a flat patch whose normal is the eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_1\). <span class="src">§5.3.2.2 · p. 129</span>
@@ 28
Filling the gap: why the smallest eigenvector is the normal
@@ 29
Take a unit vector \(\mathbf{u}\). The spread of the points along \(\mathbf{u}\) is the variance of their projections,
@@ 30
Points on a plane have almost no spread along the plane's normal, so the normal is the \(\mathbf{u}\) that minimizes \(\mathbf{u}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}\). Write \(\mathbf{u} = \sum_j c_j \mathbf{v}_j\) in the orthonormal eigenvectors of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), with \(\sum_j c_j^2 = 1\). Then \(\mathbf{u}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{u} = \sum_j \lambda_j c_j^2 \ge \lambda_1\), with equality when \(\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}_1\). The smallest eigenvalue is the variance across the patch, and its eigenvector is the normal.

@@ 32
Solving \((\boldsymbol{\Sigma} - \lambda_1\mathbf{I})\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{0}\) gives \(\mathbf{v}_1 \propto (0.02, 1)\): the normal points along y, as it should, and the thickness of the patch is \(\sqrt{0.0045} = 0.067\) m.

3. Meshes and voxels
@@ 34
Points and surfels do not say which piece of surface connects to which. A <strong>mesh</strong> does: it is a set of <strong>vertices</strong> joined into polygon <strong>faces</strong>, usually triangles, since any polygon can be cut into triangles. Connectivity makes a surface watertight and lets you walk along it or interpolate on it. The element count follows the complexity of the shape, not its size: a flat wall of any extent is two triangles and four vertices. <span class="src">§5.3.2.3 · p. 129</span>
@@ 35
Occupancy and signed distance are defined at every point of space, not only on surfaces. The direct way to store them is a regular grid of cells, called <strong>voxels</strong> in 3D. A voxel is only a container, and what its number means depends on the method. <span class="src">§5.3.2.4 · p. 130</span>

This is <strong>bilinear interpolation</strong>. Each corner's weight is the product of two factors, the four weights sum to 1, and at a corner the formula returns that corner's value. Example: \(f_{00} = 0.10\), \(f_{10} = -0.06\), \(f_{01} = 0.14\), \(f_{11} = -0.02\), and \((a, b) = (0.5, 0.25)\). The rows give \(0.02\) and \(0.06\), and \(f = 0.75 \times 0.02 + 0.25 \times 0.06 = 0.03\). In 3D the same idea with eight corners and three factors is <strong>trilinear interpolation</strong>.

4. Continuous functions
@@ 49
The last abstraction drops the grid. Mapping becomes regression: fit a function to the measurements and evaluate it wherever you like. Functions with few parameters, such as lines in 2D or planes in 3D, are compact and fast but assume the world is made of them. Control-point functions such as B-splines and non-parametric ones such as Gaussian processes assume less. Their advantages are that the resolution is not fixed in advance, that they fill gaps between measurements, and that a function which is at least once differentiable provides gradients, and so surface normals. <span class="src">§5.3.2.5 · pp. 130–131</span> Day 5 builds one.

@@ 82
<strong>"A voxel is a little solid cube."</strong> It is a storage slot. In an occupancy grid its value does stand for the whole cell; in a distance field it is a sample at one point, meant to be interpolated.
@@ 83
<strong>"A point cloud is a surface."</strong> It is samples of one, with no normals, no connectivity, and no inside or outside. Turning it into a mesh or a distance field needs extra information such as the viewpoint, or assumptions.
@@ 84
<strong>"Marching Cubes can recover detail the grid missed."</strong> It only reads signs and values at the samples. If no sample falls inside an object, there is no sign change and the object is absent from the mesh.

Representations differ in what they describe (surface or volume) and how (explicit elements or an implicit function), and real systems mix them.
@@ 106
Points are raw samples. Surfels add a normal and a radius. An NDT voxel stores a mean and covariance, whose smallest eigenvector is the surface normal.
@@ 107
Meshes add connectivity. Voxels are containers: an occupancy value covers the cell, a distance value is a sample to interpolate (bilinear in 2D, trilinear in 3D).
@@ 108
Continuous functions have no fixed resolution and can provide gradients.
@@ 109
Marching Cubes converts a distance grid to a mesh: corner signs select the pattern, linear interpolation \(t = f_a/(f_a - f_b)\) places the vertices.
