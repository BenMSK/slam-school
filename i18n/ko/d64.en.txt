Feature-based SLAM replaces each image by a few hundred points that it can find again in other images. A <strong>detector</strong> chooses locations where the image changes in every direction, so the location is well defined. A <strong>descriptor</strong> summarizes the neighbourhood as a vector, so that the same point can be recognized later. Thirty years of methods trade speed against invariance to scale, rotation, and lighting.

1. What a keypoint must do
@@ 12
<strong>Keypoints</strong> (or <strong>features</strong>) are salient image regions that are distinctive and can be re-detected from other viewpoints, in other runs, and under other conditions. A <strong>keypoint detector</strong> finds them. A <strong>keypoint descriptor</strong> encodes the local appearance around each one in a compact vector. <span class="src">§7.3.2 · p. 200</span> The desirable properties are: <span class="src">§7.3.2 · p. 201</span>
@@ 13
<strong>Scale and rotation invariance</strong>: the same point is found, and described the same way, when the camera moves closer or rolls.
@@ 14
<strong>Repeatability and distinctiveness</strong>: it is found again, and it is not confused with other points.
@@ 15
<strong>Efficiency</strong>: all of this fits in the time between two frames.
@@ 16
Matching quality is measured with the precision and recall of <a href="#d61">Day 1</a>. If two images share 100 true correspondences and a matcher returns 80 pairs of which 60 are right, precision is \(60/80 = 0.75\) and recall is \(60/100 = 0.6\). The wrong 20 are the outliers that <a href="#d32">Week 6 · Day 2</a> (RANSAC) and the robust kernels of <a href="#d34">Week 6 · Day 4</a> exist for. Strong lighting changes, textureless surfaces, and moving objects all make detection and matching worse. <span class="src">§7.3.2 · p. 201</span>

2. Corners and the second-moment matrix
@@ 18
The Harris detector classifies a patch as a keypoint when both eigenvalues of its second-moment matrix are large, meaning the intensity varies strongly in two orthogonal directions. Shi-Tomasi uses the smaller eigenvalue directly; Harris uses a "cornerness" function that avoids computing eigenvalues. <span class="src">§7.3.2.1 · p. 201</span> The book does not define the matrix, so here it is.

<strong>Numbers.</strong> For \(\mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & 2 \\ 2 & 8 \end{bmatrix}\): the eigenvalues of a symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix \(\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & d \end{bmatrix}\) are \(\tfrac{a+d}{2} \pm \sqrt{\big(\tfrac{a-d}{2}\big)^2 + b^2}\), here \(9 \pm \sqrt{5}\), so \(\lambda_1 = 11.24\), \(\lambda_2 = 6.76\). Negative: an edge.

Look at a long straight fence through a small hole. If the fence slides sideways along its own direction you cannot tell. That is an edge, and the effect is called the aperture problem. Now put a fence post corner in the hole: any motion is visible. A good keypoint is a place where the hole pins down both directions.

@@ 67
The <strong>image pyramid</strong> is how scale invariance is usually obtained: detect on the image and on copies shrunk by a constant factor, so a corner that is too big for the window at full resolution fits it a few levels up. SIFT's 128 numbers come from a \(4\times4\) grid of cells around the keypoint with an 8-bin histogram of gradient directions in each (\(4\cdot4\cdot8 = 128\)).

4. Binary descriptors and matching
@@ 69
A BRIEF-style descriptor is a bit string: bit \(i\) is 1 if pixel \(a_i\) of the patch is brighter than pixel \(b_i\), for a fixed list of pixel pairs. Two descriptors are compared by the <strong>Hamming distance</strong>, the number of bit positions in which they differ. <span class="src">§7.3.2.1 · p. 202</span>
@@ 70
Filling the gap: computing and using the Hamming distance
@@ 71
Take the bitwise exclusive-or (XOR) of the two strings and count the ones:
@@ 72
Processors do both steps in single instructions on 64 bits at a time, which is why binary descriptors match so fast. A SIFT comparison instead needs a 128-term sum of squared differences.
@@ 73
<strong>How small is "close"?</strong> ORB descriptors have 256 bits. As a rough model, treat the bits of two unrelated patches as independent coin flips. Each bit then differs with probability \(\tfrac12\), so the distance is binomial with mean \(256\cdot\tfrac12 = 128\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{256\cdot\tfrac14} = 8\). A pair at distance 40 is eleven standard deviations below what chance produces. Real bits are correlated, so the separation is less clean, but this is why a threshold well below 128 works.
@@ 74
<strong>Matching.</strong> For each descriptor in image 1, find its nearest neighbour in image 2. A common safeguard is the <strong>ratio test</strong>: accept the match only if the nearest distance is clearly smaller than the second-nearest, for example below 0.8 times it. Query 11001010 against candidates 11001110 (distance 1), 01101010 (distance 2), and 00110101 (distance 8): the best is at 1, the runner-up at 2, the ratio is 0.5, so the match is accepted. If the runner-up were also at 1 the point would be ambiguous, typical of repeated texture, and dropped.

@@ 76

@@ 87
<strong>"A strong edge is a good keypoint."</strong> Its position along the edge is undetermined. One large eigenvalue is not enough; that is why Shi-Tomasi looks at the smaller one.
@@ 88
<strong>"Detector and descriptor are the same thing."</strong> FAST only detects, BRIEF only describes, and ORB is the combination. They can be mixed.
@@ 89
<strong>"Matched keypoints are correct correspondences."</strong> Matching by appearance always produces some wrong pairs. Geometry (Day 5) and robust estimation (Week 6) remove them.

A detector picks repeatable locations; a descriptor makes them recognizable. Wanted: invariance, repeatability, distinctiveness, efficiency.
@@ 113
The second-moment matrix \(\mathbf{M}\) measures how a patch changes under a small shift. Corner: both eigenvalues large. Shi-Tomasi: \(\lambda_2\). Harris: \(\det\mathbf{M} - k(\tr\mathbf{M})^2\).
@@ 114
