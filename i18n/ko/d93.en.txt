Turn the measurement model around and you can compute motion from the readings: rotate each accelerometer sample into the world, add gravity back, and integrate twice. This is dead reckoning. It needs the starting orientation and velocity, it drifts fast because every error is integrated two or three times, and its result depends on the starting state in a way that forces a full re-integration whenever that state is revised.

1. Why not one state per IMU sample?
@@ 12
The models of Day 1 relate each IMU sample to the robot state at that instant, so in principle each sample could be a factor in the MAP problem of <a href="#d8">Week 2 · Day 2</a>. The trouble is the rate. An IMU delivers 200 to 1000 samples per second, and each sample would need its own state in the graph. <span class="src">§11.2 · p. 308</span> One minute at 200 Hz is 12,000 states, each with a rotation, a position, a velocity, and two biases: 15 unknowns apiece, 180,000 in all, for a sensor that only tells you how the robot moved. A continuous-time trajectory (<a href="#d25">Week 5 · Day 1</a>) avoids the extra states but still needs one factor per sample. <span class="src">§11.2 · p. 308</span>

4. Dead reckoning drifts, and gravity makes it worse
@@ 34
Try it: the widget runs the loop above on a simulated planar IMU (one gyroscope, two accelerometer axes) carried over two hills. Start with the default 0.1°/s gyroscope bias. Then set it to zero and try an accelerometer bias of 0.05 m/s².
@@ 35
Notice that a gyroscope bias of only 0.1°/s puts the estimate about 22 m off after 20 s, on a 40 m drive, and that the error follows the cubic model curve. The tilt error at the end is just 2°, but 2° of tilt turns 0.34 m/s² of gravity into false horizontal acceleration. An accelerometer bias of 0.05 m/s² alone gives roughly 10 m, following the quadratic curve. With every slider at zero about 6 cm of error remains: the rectangle rule of (11.12) on a changing acceleration.

Filling the gap: how fast does each error grow?
@@ 37
The book says inertial odometry drifts but does not give rates. They follow from counting integrations.
@@ 38
<strong>Accelerometer bias</strong> \(b_a\). A constant acceleration error, integrated twice: velocity error \(b_a t\), position error \(\tfrac12 b_a t^2\).
@@ 39
<strong>Gyroscope bias</strong> \(b_g\). The orientation error grows as \(\delta\theta = b_g t\). The integrator rotates the measured specific force, which is mostly the 9.81 m/s² support force, with the wrong \(\mathbf{R}\). A vertical vector of length \(g\) tilted by a small angle \(\delta\theta\) acquires a horizontal component \(g\sin\delta\theta \approx g\,\delta\theta\). After adding \(\mathbf{g}\) back, the vertical parts still nearly cancel, but the horizontal part is left over as a false acceleration \(g\,b_g\,t\). Integrate twice:
@@ 40
With \(b_g = 0.1\)°/s \(= 1.745\times10^{-3}\) rad/s and \(t = 20\) s: \(\tfrac16 \times 9.81 \times 1.745\times10^{-3} \times 8000 = 22.8\) m, the number in the widget.
@@ 41
<strong>White noise.</strong> Accelerometer noise of density \(\sigma_a\) is a stream of independent kicks. A kick at time \(s\) changes the velocity for good, so by time \(t\) it has moved the position by an amount proportional to \(t-s\). Adding the variances of all kicks, \(\operatorname{var}(\delta p) = \sigma_a^2\int_0^t (t-s)^2 ds = \sigma_a^2 t^3/3\). The same argument with one more integration gives the gyroscope-noise entry in the table.

@@ 56
The integrator has to subtract gravity, a known vector of length 9.81, from a reading of about the same length. If it holds the reading at a slightly wrong angle, the subtraction leaves a sideways remainder. An IMU navigates well for exactly as long as it knows which way is down.
@@ 57
One more experiment: set the gyroscope bias to +0.10°/s and the accelerometer bias to about +0.11 m/s². The final error collapses to a few metres, because on this path the two errors push in opposite directions. A tilt error and an accelerometer bias look alike to the integrator. Next week's observability analysis is about telling such effects apart.

5. Keyframes, and the flaw of plain integration
@@ 59
Dead reckoning alone is hopeless beyond a few seconds, so the IMU is combined with a camera or LiDAR in a factor graph whose states sit at selected instants. The book calls them <strong>keyframe states</strong>, whatever sensor triggers them. <span class="src">§11.2.1 · p. 310</span> Between consecutive keyframes at times \(t_i\) and \(t_j\) there are many IMU samples, \(k = i, i+1, \dots, j-1\). Chaining (11.12) over all of them gives
@@ 60
with \(\Delta t_{ij} = \sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\Delta t\) the time between the keyframes. <span class="src">§11.2.1 · p. 310</span> This already is a relative-motion constraint between states \(i\) and \(j\). Its flaw is in how the unknowns enter. Every \(\mathbf{R}_k\) inside the sums is \(\mathbf{R}_i\) times a product of gyroscope terms, and every \(\mathbf{v}_k\) contains \(\mathbf{v}_i\). A nonlinear solver such as Gauss-Newton (<a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a>) changes its estimate of \(\mathbf{R}_i\) and \(\mathbf{v}_i\) at every iteration, and each change invalidates every term of the sums. <span class="src">§11.2.1 · p. 310</span>
@@ 61
Count the cost: 100 keyframe intervals, 200 samples each, 10 solver iterations is 200,000 sample integrations per solve, repeated every time the graph is re-optimized. Tomorrow's rearrangement brings it down to 20,000, done once.
@@ 62
<strong>"Integrate the accelerometer twice to get position."</strong> First rotate the reading into the world with the current \(\mathbf{R}\), then add \(\mathbf{g}\), then integrate. Skip the rotation and the reading is added along the wrong axes; skip gravity and a robot at rest accelerates upward at 9.81 m/s².
@@ 63
<strong>"Position drift from a gyroscope is small because gyroscopes are accurate."</strong> Tilt error is multiplied by \(g\) and integrated twice more. It grows as \(t^3\) and usually dominates.
@@ 64
<strong>"Equation (11.13) cannot be used in a factor graph."</strong> It can. It is just wasteful, because it must be re-evaluated from the raw samples whenever the state at \(i\) changes.
