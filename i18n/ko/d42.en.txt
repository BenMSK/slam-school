Week 7 review: gradients through an optimizer
@@ 3
After today you can set up a bilevel problem, compute its upper-level gradient by unrolling and by implicit differentiation on paper, differentiate simple expressions on Lie groups, and say what a differentiable optimizer library has to provide.
@@ 4
A learned front-end with parameters \(\mathbf{y}\) feeds a back-end that solves a nonlinear least squares problem for \(\mathbf{x}^*\). Training the front-end on the final estimate is a bilevel problem, and its gradient needs \(\partial\mathbf{x}^*/\partial\mathbf{y}\). Unrolled differentiation gets it by backpropagating through the solver's iterations: exact for the iterations that ran, with memory that grows with their number unless the backward pass is truncated. Implicit differentiation gets it from the optimality condition: one linear solve with the Hessian at the solution, no stored iterates, valid only at convergence. Both rely on AutoDiff, which on SLAM variables needs Jacobians defined with ⊕ and ⊖ on Lie groups, numerically safe \(\Exp\) and \(\Log\), and second-order robust optimizers built from differentiable parts.

Q3. An unrolled scalar solver has constant A = 0.5 and B = 0.3, and the initial guess does not depend on y. What is the sensitivity S₂ after two steps?
@@ 43
S₁ = 0.5·0 + 0.3 = 0.3 and S₂ = 0.5·0.3 + 0.3 = 0.45. The limit B/(1 − A) = 0.6 is the converged value.

Q4. Near the solution an inner solver multiplies the adjoint by A = 0.9 per backward step. About how many backward steps M keep the truncation loss below 1%?
@@ 45
The missing fraction is 0.9^M. 0.9^M ≤ 0.01 needs M ≥ ln(0.01)/ln(0.9) = 43.7. A slowly converging solver needs a long backward pass.

A pose-graph factor between poses \(\boldsymbol{\chi}_a\) and \(\boldsymbol{\chi}_b\) with measurement \(\mathbf{Z}\) has the error element \(\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{Z}^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\chi}_a^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\chi}_b\). Using the blocks of Day 4 and the chain rule, find the right Jacobians of \(\mathbf{E}\) with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\chi}_b\) and \(\boldsymbol{\chi}_a\). Verify the second one directly from the definition, and check both on SO(2).
@@ 75
With respect to \(\boldsymbol{\chi}_b\): it is the last factor of the product, so the Jacobian is \(\mathbf{I}\) (4.34).
@@ 76
With respect to \(\boldsymbol{\chi}_a\), in three stages. Inversion: \(-\Ad_{\boldsymbol{\chi}_a}\) (4.32). Then \(\boldsymbol{\chi}_a^{-1}\circ\boldsymbol{\chi}_b\) with respect to its first factor: \(\Ad_{\boldsymbol{\chi}_b^{-1}}\) (4.33). Then composing with \(\mathbf{Z}^{-1}\) on the left, where our quantity is the second factor: \(\mathbf{I}\). Product:
@@ 77
Directly: \(\mathbf{Z}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\chi}_a\Exp(\boldsymbol{\tau}))^{-1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_b = \mathbf{Z}^{-1}\Exp(-\boldsymbol{\tau})\,\boldsymbol{\chi}_a^{-1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_b\). Write \(\mathbf{D} = \boldsymbol{\chi}_a^{-1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_b\). Then \(\Exp(-\boldsymbol{\tau})\,\mathbf{D} = \mathbf{D}\,\big(\mathbf{D}^{-1}\Exp(-\boldsymbol{\tau})\mathbf{D}\big) = \mathbf{D}\,\Exp(-\Ad_{\mathbf{D}^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\tau})\), so the output is \(\mathbf{E}\circ\Exp(-\Ad_{\mathbf{D}^{-1}}\boldsymbol{\tau})\) and the Jacobian is \(-\Ad_{\mathbf{D}^{-1}} = -\Ad_{\boldsymbol{\chi}_b^{-1}\boldsymbol{\chi}_a}\), as before.
