A visual-inertial odometry system is the preintegration factor graph of Week 16 with vision factors added, solved over a short sliding window so that the answer arrives within milliseconds. States that leave the window are marginalized into a prior. That prior is frozen at old linearization points, and if newer factors on the same variables are linearized elsewhere, the estimator gains information about global yaw that no sensor supplied. The observability analysis of Day 3 is what detects this and what fixes it.
@@ 13
1. What a VIO has to deliver
@@ 14
A VIO is used as the odometry source inside control loops and in virtual-reality headsets, and both need the estimate with very low delay, typically 10–50 ms. The book's example is a headset that refreshes between 72 and 120 times per second: that is one frame every \(1/72 \approx 13.9\) ms to \(1/120 \approx 8.3\) ms. A late pose shows the user a world that lags behind the head, and in a controller a long delay can destabilize tracking. <span class="src">§11.4.1 · pp. 326–327</span>
@@ 15
So the estimator cannot optimize the whole trajectory at every frame. It has to bound its work.

3. Fixed-lag smoothing and marginalization
@@ 42
Factor-graph VIO systems usually run a <strong>fixed-lag smoother</strong>, also called <strong>sliding-window optimization</strong>: only the states of a receding horizon, for example the last 5 to 10 seconds, are optimized. A longer horizon is more accurate and costs more. States and factors that fall out of the horizon are <strong>marginalized</strong>. <span class="src">§11.4.1 · p. 327</span>
@@ 43
Filling the gap: what marginalizing a state does to the information matrix
@@ 44
Split the unknowns into the part to remove (\(m\)) and the part to keep (\(r\)). The linearized factors that touch \(m\) give an information matrix in blocks. Marginalizing is the Schur complement of <a href="#d15">Week 3 · Day 3</a>:
@@ 45
<strong>Smallest example.</strong> Two scalar positions \(x_0, x_1\). A prior on \(x_0\) with variance 0.25 (information 4) and an odometry factor \(x_1 - x_0 = u\) with variance 1 (information 1) give
@@ 46
The variance of \(x_1\) is \(1/0.8 = 1.25 = 0.25 + 1\): the two variances add, as they should along a chain.
@@ 47
<strong>Marginalization keeps unobservable directions unobservable.</strong> Suppose \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\) with \(\mathbf{u} = (\mathbf{u}_m, \mathbf{u}_r)\). The first block row says \(\mathbf{u}_m = -\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{mm}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{mr}\mathbf{u}_r\). Insert that into the second block row, \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{rm}\mathbf{u}_m + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{rr}\mathbf{u}_r = \mathbf{0}\), and you get \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{u}_r = \mathbf{0}\). The prior has the same null direction, restricted to the variables that remain. Without the prior on \(x_0\) in the example, \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) has the null vector \((1, 1)\) and \(\Lambda_{\text{prior}} = 1 - 1 = 0\): nothing is known about \(x_1\) alone.
@@ 48
The alternative the book mentions is an incremental solver such as iSAM2 (<a href="#d17">Week 3 · Day 5</a>), which keeps the whole history and reuses earlier computation. It can be very accurate, but its run time per update is not bounded and can spike, which a latency-critical system cannot accept. <span class="src">§11.4.1 · p. 327</span>

4. Consistency and first-estimate Jacobians
@@ 50
The book introduces observability analysis partly as a tool "to improve estimation consistency", and its example system is named after the technique: First-Estimate Jacobian (FEJ) window bundle adjustment. <span class="src">§11.3 · p. 320; §11.4.1 · p. 328</span> It does not explain the mechanism. Here it is.
@@ 51
An estimator is <strong>consistent</strong> when the covariance it reports matches the errors it actually makes. An inconsistent estimator is usually overconfident: it reports a small covariance and then trusts new measurements too little.
@@ 52
Filling the gap: how a sliding window invents information
@@ 53
A marginalization prior is a quadratic built from Jacobians evaluated at the estimates of that moment. Call them estimate A. The original factors are gone, so the prior can never be relinearized. Later, new factors arrive that involve some of the same variables, and Gauss-Newton linearizes them at the current estimates. Call them estimate B.
@@ 54
Now look at the yaw null vector of Day 3. Its blocks are \(-\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{g}\) and \(-\mathbf{p}_f\times\mathbf{g}\): they contain the values of the variables themselves. The prior's information matrix has the null vector built from A. The new factors have the null vector built from B. If A and B differ, no single vector is in both nullspaces, and the sum of the two information matrices has no yaw null vector at all. Its rank has gone up by one. The estimator now holds a finite variance for global yaw, although no sensor measured it. That is information from nowhere, and it makes the estimator overconfident.
@@ 55
The three shift vectors contain only identity blocks. They do not depend on the linearization point, so they survive.
@@ 56
<strong>The fix.</strong> For every variable that takes part in the prior, keep evaluating Jacobians at the estimate that was used when the prior was built, its "first estimate", even though the variable's value keeps being updated. Then all factors share one null vector and yaw stays unobservable. The price is a slightly less accurate linearization.
@@ 57
Try it: a window of 2-D poses and landmarks after the first pose has been marginalized. Move the slider to zero and back, then switch on first-estimate Jacobians.
@@ 58
Notice: with the slider at zero, A and B coincide and three eigenvalues are zero (x, y, yaw). As soon as the estimate has moved, only two are zero and a third small eigenvalue appears: the estimator now "knows" its global yaw. With first-estimate Jacobians the third zero comes back for any slider value.

5. How good is it?
@@ 60
The book lists widely used systems (a visual-inertial ORB-SLAM, Direct Sparse Visual-Inertial Odometry, VINS-Mono, OpenVINS, Kimera, BASALT, DM-VIO) and gives a yardstick: a good VIO drifts by less than 1% of the distance travelled, and in some cases as little as 0.1%. <span class="src">§11.4.1 · pp. 327–328</span>
@@ 61
Its worked example is a sliding-window FEJ system on a 36-minute, 11.42 km drive through a city (KAIST urban dataset, sequence 38), with a camera at 10 Hz and an IMU at 100 Hz, so ten IMU samples are preintegrated per frame. With no loop closure, the final error is about 2.05° and 21.2 m, which the book states as 0.18% of the distance. <span class="src">§11.4.1 · pp. 328–329, Fig. 11.5</span>

@@ 63

@@ 74
"Ill-posed" is this week's subject again: the extrinsics are extra unknowns, and for some motions they are unobservable.
@@ 75
<strong>Temporal synchronization</strong> makes sure all sensors agree on when each measurement was taken. A time offset that is not accounted for corrupts the trajectory estimate and can bias benchmark results. <span class="src">§11.4.3 · pp. 329–330</span> The size of the effect is plain kinematics: if an image is stamped \(t_d\) too late, the estimator pairs it with a pose that is off by about \(\mathbf{v}\,t_d\) in position and \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\,t_d\) in angle. At 10 m/s and \(t_d = 5\) ms that is 5 cm; at a turn rate of 100°/s it is 0.5°.
@@ 76
The book lists the options: a hardware trigger that fires all sensors from one clock; a clock-synchronization protocol such as PTP over Ethernet; time-stamping at the sensor and aligning afterwards, which is less accurate and robust; or, when none of these is possible, estimating the time offset as one more state variable. <span class="src">§11.4.3 · p. 330</span>

<strong>"States that leave the window are thrown away."</strong> They are marginalized: what they implied about the remaining states stays, as a prior factor.
@@ 91
<strong>"First-estimate Jacobians freeze the estimate."</strong> The estimate keeps moving. Only the point at which the Jacobians are evaluated is held, and only for variables tied to the prior.
@@ 92
<strong>"A drift of 0.18% means the pose is accurate to 0.18%."</strong> It is the final position error divided by the path length, without loop closure. The error keeps growing with distance.

@@ 94

Q2. Scalar states \(x_0, x_1\). A prior on \(x_0\) has variance 1, the odometry factor between them has variance 1. After marginalizing \(x_0\), what is the information on \(x_1\)?
@@ 96
\(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(\Lambda_{\text{prior}} = 1 - (-1)\cdot\tfrac12\cdot(-1) = 0.5\). The variance is 2, the sum of the two variances.

@@ 104

@@ 105

A VIO must answer within about 10–50 ms, so it optimizes a sliding window of recent keyframes: 15 unknowns per keyframe plus 3 per landmark.
@@ 112
Its factors are preintegrated IMU, bias, projection, and prior factors.
@@ 113
