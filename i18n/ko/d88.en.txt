Bundle adjustment for events is young. Indirect back-ends reuse the reprojection error of classical visual SLAM. Direct back-ends optimize either how well the events align (contrast maximization) or a brightness error for every event. Contrast maximization deserves a close look, because it turns the vague goal "the picture should be sharp" into a function of the motion that an optimizer can climb.
@@ 11
1. Why back-ends are rare here
@@ 12
A back-end improves the agreement between all the unknowns and all the data, and stops errors from passing back and forth between tracking and mapping. In frame-based visual SLAM that job is done by bundle adjustment (BA), which you studied in <a href="#d69">Week 12 · Day 3</a>. <span class="src">§10.5 · p. 293</span>
@@ 13
Most event-based systems have no such step. They run <strong>parallel tracking and mapping</strong>: a tracking module estimates the pose assuming the map is right, a mapping module updates the map assuming the poses are right, and each uses the other's latest output. The book's judgement is that these systems chose simplicity and low latency over accuracy and robustness. A joint refinement is hard for events for three reasons: the data is noisy and depends on the motion, the unknowns are many and correlated so the search space is large, and the optimization is costly and has local minima. <span class="src">§10.5 · p. 293</span>

3. Contrast maximization, step by step
@@ 29
The book describes contrast maximization in one paragraph (p. 292) and names it as one of the two event-only back-end objectives (p. 294). It gives no formula. Here is the method in full for the simplest case.
@@ 30
Filling the gap: warp, accumulate, score
@@ 31
<strong>The unknown.</strong> A vector of motion parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\). In the simplest case the whole image slides with one constant velocity, and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is that velocity in pixels per millisecond.

@@ 36
<strong>The estimate</strong> is \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\star = \argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\).
@@ 37
<strong>Why the true motion wins.</strong> A scene edge that moves at velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) fires its events along a line in space-time: at time \(t\) it is at \(\mathbf{x}_0 + (t - t_\text{ref})\mathbf{v}\). Warp with \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{v}\) and every one of those events lands on \(\mathbf{x}_0\). Warp with anything else and they are spread over a segment of length \(|\mathbf{v} - \boldsymbol{\theta}|\) times the duration.
@@ 38
<strong>Why variance rewards that.</strong> Expand the square: \(f = \frac{1}{N_p}\sum_i H_i^2 - \mu_H^2\). With \(b_k = 1\), and as long as no event is warped off the image, the total \(\sum_i H_i = N_e\) is the same for every \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), so \(\mu_H\) is a constant. Maximizing the variance is then the same as maximizing \(\sum_i H_i^2\), and a sum of squares with a fixed total is largest when the total is concentrated in few places. Take 12 events on 4 pixels:

@@ 49
Take a long-exposure photo of traffic at night and you get streaks. If you knew exactly how each car moved, you could slide every bit of light back along its path and the streaks would shrink to sharp headlights. Contrast maximization runs this backwards: it tries motions until the streaks become points, and the motion that does it is the answer.

4. From one speed to SLAM
@@ 51
The recipe does not care what \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is, as long as it defines a warp. Three steps lead to a back-end.
@@ 52
<strong>Rotation.</strong> For a camera that only rotates, \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is the angular velocity (three numbers), and the warp rotates each event's viewing direction back to the reference time. Table 10.1 lists this as "CMax-ω", for rotational motion. <span class="src">Table 10.1 · p. 295</span>
@@ 53
<strong>A whole trajectory.</strong> In CMax-SLAM the unknowns are the camera's rotations over time. All events are warped onto one panorama, and the trajectory is refined until that panorama is as sharp as possible. This is bundle adjustment with contrast as the objective, and the sharp panorama is an edge map of the scene (Figure 10.3 shows one). <span class="src">§10.5 · p. 294; Table 10.1</span>
@@ 54
<strong>The other objective.</strong> EMBA and EPBA instead write one residual per event from equation (10.1): the brightness change that the current map and trajectory predict at that pixel, minus the \(p_k C\) the event reported. They refine the motion together with a gradient map (EMBA) or an intensity map (EPBA). A gradient map can be turned into an intensity image by Poisson integration (Figure 10.4). <span class="src">§10.5 · p. 294; Table 10.1; Figure 10.4 · p. 290</span>
@@ 55
All three are for rotational motion. The book states that efficient, direct, event-only BA for 6-DoF motion in natural scenes is still open. <span class="src">§10.5 · p. 294</span>
@@ 56
Going deeper (optional): when the sharpest image is wrong
@@ 57
The book warns that when events can be warped onto a few pixels or a line, these methods "can suffer from undesired global optima". <span class="src">§10.4.3 · p. 292</span> The reason is visible in the table above: variance is highest when all events sit on one pixel. Our 1-D warp only shifts events, so it cannot do that. A warp family that can shrink the image, for instance one with a zoom-like parameter, can send every event toward a single point and score a huge contrast that has nothing to do with the true motion. Practical systems restrict the warp or add a penalty to rule this out.

@@ 75
The mean is 10/5 = 2. The squared deviations are 4, 9, 4, 9, 4, which sum to 30, and 30/5 = 6. The flat image (2, 2, 2, 2, 2) with the same 10 events has variance 0.
