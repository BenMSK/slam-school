1. Is SLAM solved? What Part III is about
@@ 13
The Prelude opens with a question the field keeps asking. Its answer: yes, if you restrict yourself to small, static, indoor scenes with plenty of features. The uses people actually want are underwater, on other planets, in disaster zones, and in dense city traffic, and there the answer is no. <span class="src">Prelude III · p. 361</span>
@@ 14
The editors call the direction of travel <strong>Spatial AI</strong>. Compared with the SLAM of Parts I and II it differs in two ways. First, it uses deep learning for the parts of the problem that are hard to model by hand, which makes systems more adaptive and robust. Second, it changes what a map is: maps that can render views nobody captured (<strong>novel view synthesis</strong>), maps that carry meaning (<strong>semantic</strong> information), and maps that cope with a scene that changes. The Prelude adds two cautions: use foundation models, and do not forget that the result has to run on a real device. <span class="src">§III.1 · pp. 361–362</span>
@@ 15
Why not skip the map and ask a large image-and-text model about the scene? The Prelude's answer is that such models give a holistic description of an image and often struggle with spatial questions. Many tasks need a persistent 3D representation that is kept up to date, a spatio-temporal memory, and building one is what SLAM does. <span class="src">§III.2 · p. 362</span>

@@ 76
<strong>Replace one component.</strong> Train a network to detect features, describe them, match them, or reject outliers, then plug it back into the usual pipeline. Such networks can often beat their hand-designed counterparts. <span class="src">p. 366</span>
@@ 77
<strong>Predict 3D properties and add them as factors.</strong> A network estimates depth from one image, or relative pose from two, and the prediction enters the factor graph as an extra term. <span class="src">p. 366</span>
@@ 78
<strong>Train through the optimizer.</strong> The weakness of the modular approach is that errors made by one component feed the next and compound. The alternative is to make more of the pipeline one trainable module, with bundle adjustment as a layer of the network. This is the differentiable optimization of <a href="#d37">Week 7 · Day 1</a>, put to work. <span class="src">p. 366</span>
@@ 79
<strong>Replace the stack.</strong> DUSt3R predicts a <strong>pointmap</strong>, one 3D point for every pixel, directly from uncalibrated images. <span class="src">p. 366</span>
@@ 80
The chapter's own summary is that these methods combine learning with geometry: some feed network predictions into an optimization-based pipeline, some join learned components to factor graphs, and some estimate motion and structure with a network alone. <span class="src">pp. 366–368</span>

4. The network vocabulary you need this week
@@ 82
The chapter uses a handful of network terms without defining them. Here they are, at the depth this week needs.
@@ 83
Filling the gap: networks, losses, and training in five lines
@@ 84
A <strong>neural network</strong> is a function \(f_\theta\) built by composing simple differentiable steps called <strong>layers</strong>. The vector \(\theta\) holds all its adjustable numbers, the <strong>parameters</strong> or weights. A <strong>loss</strong> \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\) measures how far the outputs are from what we want on a set of training examples. <strong>Training</strong> lowers the loss by gradient descent:
@@ 85
with a small step size \(\eta\). This is the steepest descent of <a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a>, applied to the weights instead of the robot's state. Example with one parameter: \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = (\theta - 3)^2\), start at \(\theta = 0\), \(\eta = 0.25\). The gradient is \(2(\theta - 3) = -6\), so \(\theta\) becomes \(0 - 0.25 \cdot (-6) = 1.5\). The next gradient is \(-3\), giving \(\theta = 2.25\). Each step halves the distance to the minimum at 3.
@@ 86
The gradient is computed by reverse-mode automatic differentiation (<a href="#d37">Week 7 · Day 1</a>), which is why every layer must be differentiable. If the labels come from a sensor or a simulator (for example, true depth), the training is <strong>supervised</strong>. If the loss is built from the input data alone, it is <strong>self-supervised</strong>; you will meet one tomorrow.
@@ 87
Two kinds of layer appear all week.
@@ 88
A <strong>convolutional</strong> layer slides one small filter over the image and applies it at every position. With the 1-D filter \((-1, 0, 1)\) and the signal \((2, 2, 5, 9, 9)\), the three interior outputs are \(5 - 2 = 3\), \(9 - 2 = 7\), and \(9 - 5 = 4\): the filter responds where brightness changes. A <strong>convolutional neural network (CNN)</strong> stacks many such layers with learned filters, usually shrinking the image grid as it goes, so the output is a coarse grid of <strong>feature vectors</strong>. RAFT's encoders output features at 1/8 of the input resolution. <span class="src">§13.2.3 · p. 376</span>
@@ 89
An <strong>attention</strong> layer lets every feature vector look at all the others, however far apart they are in the image. A <strong>transformer</strong> is a network made mostly of attention layers, and a <strong>vision transformer (ViT)</strong> is a transformer whose inputs are small image patches turned into vectors called <strong>tokens</strong>. Figure 13.6 draws attention as three boxes: MatMul, SoftMax, MatMul. <span class="src">Fig. 13.6 · p. 375</span>
@@ 90
Filling the gap: what MatMul → SoftMax → MatMul computes
@@ 91
Take one <strong>query</strong> vector \(\mathbf{q}\), and a set of tokens that each offer a <strong>key</strong> \(\mathbf{k}_j\) and a <strong>value</strong> \(\mathbf{v}_j\).
@@ 92
The first matrix product computes all scores \(s_j\) at once. The <strong>softmax</strong> turns scores into positive weights that sum to 1. The second matrix product averages the values with those weights. (Transformers also divide the scores by \(\sqrt{d}\), the square root of the vector length, and learn the linear maps that produce queries, keys, and values.)
@@ 93
Example: scores \((2, 1, 0)\) give \(e^2 = 7.39\), \(e^1 = 2.72\), \(e^0 = 1\), which sum to 11.11. The weights are \(0.665, 0.245, 0.090\). If the values are the numbers \(10, 20, 30\), the output is \(6.65 + 4.90 + 2.70 = 14.25\).
@@ 94
In <strong>self-attention</strong> the queries and the keys come from the same image. In <strong>cross-attention</strong> the queries come from one image and the keys and values from the other, which is how a network compares two views.
@@ 95
Try it: turn the query, then raise the sharpness β.
@@ 96
Notice that at the start the query points almost at key k2, which gets weight 0.73, and the output 2.15 is pulled toward the value 2. At β = 0 every key gets 1/6 and the output is the plain mean 3.5. At β = 20 the layer picks k2 alone. Attention is a soft, differentiable way of saying "find the best match".
@@ 97
<strong>"Part III replaces Parts I and II."</strong> The systems in this chapter still solve least-squares problems on factor graphs. What changes is where the residuals and their weights come from.
@@ 98
<strong>"A big image-language model makes the map unnecessary."</strong> The Prelude says these models often struggle with spatial understanding and that many tasks need a persistent 3D representation. <span class="src">§III.2 · p. 362</span>
@@ 99
<strong>"Learned components always help."</strong> Plugging separately trained modules together lets their errors compound. That observation is the reason for end-to-end training. <span class="src">p. 366</span>

Q3. Attention scores for three keys are (1, 1, 0). What weight does the third key get? Use e = 2.72.
@@ 113
The exponentials are 2.72, 2.72, 1, which sum to 6.44. The third weight is 1/6.44 = 0.155, about 0.16. A softmax weight is never exactly zero.
@@ 114
Q4. With loss (θ − 3)², θ = 1, and step size η = 0.1, what is θ after one gradient-descent step?
@@ 115
The gradient is 2(1 − 3) = −4. The update is 1 − 0.1·(−4) = 1.4.
