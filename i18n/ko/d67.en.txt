Full bundle adjustment is the most accurate thing you can do with a set of images, and it is far too slow to repeat for every new frame. Real-time systems therefore split the problem by how fast each answer is needed: the camera pose for every frame (a 6-unknown problem against a frozen map), the nearby map for every keyframe (a small BA), and the global shape only when a loop closes.

<strong>Parallel tracking and mapping.</strong> Split the work into two threads that run side by side. The <strong>tracking thread</strong> handles every image, but estimates only that image's camera pose. The <strong>mapping thread</strong> runs BA, but only on a subset \(\mathsf{K} \subset \mathsf{C}\) of the images called <strong>keyframes</strong>. Only keyframe poses become part of the map, so BA needs to run at keyframe rate, typically 0.5 to 5 Hz. <span class="src">§7.4.1 · p. 208</span>
@@ 16
<strong>Locality.</strong> What the camera sees now says almost nothing about parts of the map that are far away, except when a loop closes. So the mapping thread optimizes only a window of nearby keyframes (<strong>local BA</strong>), and a third thread that runs rarely takes care of loops. <span class="src">§7.4.1 · pp. 208–209</span>

3. Try it in a flat world
@@ 42
The widgets of this week use a <strong>flatland camera</strong>: the world is a plane with coordinates \((x, z)\), a camera pose is \((c_x, c_z, \theta)\) (3 unknowns instead of 6), a point is \((p_x, p_z)\), and the image is a line on which the point appears at \(u = f\,x_c / z_c\), where \((x_c, z_c)\) is the point in the camera frame and \(f = 300\) px. Everything above carries over with smaller matrices: one residual per match, a \(1 \times 3\) Jacobian, a \(3 \times 3\) system.
@@ 43
Try it: press "Gauss-Newton step" four times. Then switch on "2 wrong matches", step until the pose stops moving, and finally switch on "Huber kernel" and keep stepping.
@@ 44
Notice three things. From the previous pose, three or four steps bring the camera to within about a centimetre of the truth, and the RMS reprojection error settles near the pixel noise. Two wrong matches out of twelve drag the least-squares pose off by tens of centimetres and several degrees. With the Huber kernel the pose returns to within a few centimetres after several more steps, while the two bad matches keep their large residuals: the kernel does not repair them, it stops listening to them.

4. Keyframes and the mapping thread
@@ 46
A frame taken 30 ms after the last one shows almost the same scene from almost the same place. It adds little information and, because the baseline is tiny, almost no ability to triangulate. Keyframe-based systems therefore use most frames once, to track the camera, and then drop them. Only keyframes and their observations enter the map. <span class="src">§7.3.4 · p. 206</span> A keyframe can be taken at a fixed interval, but the book recommends promoting a frame when it contains significantly new information. <span class="src">§7.4.1 · p. 208</span>

5. Local BA and the covisibility graph
@@ 65
Which keyframes are "nearby"? One answer is temporal: the last \(k\) frames or keyframes, the usual choice in visual odometry and visual-inertial systems. A better answer for SLAM is <strong>covisibility</strong>: two keyframes are neighbours if they observe more than \(\theta\) map points in common. The <strong>covisibility graph</strong> has keyframes as nodes and an edge for each such pair. <span class="src">§7.4.1 · p. 209</span> A camera that returns to an old place becomes a neighbour of old keyframes, which a temporal window would miss.
@@ 66
Local BA then uses three sets (Figure 7.6): \(\mathsf{K}_1\), the current keyframe \(k\) and its covisibility neighbours; \(\mathsf{P}_1\), the points seen by them; and \(\mathsf{K}_2\), all other keyframes that see some point of \(\mathsf{P}_1\).
