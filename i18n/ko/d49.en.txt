2. Local solvers can be confidently wrong
@@ 21
A point is a <strong>local minimum</strong> of a cost \(f\) if no nearby point has a lower cost, and a <strong>global minimum</strong> if no point at all has a lower cost. A local solver stops when the gradient is zero and the cost stops decreasing, which is true at both. Figure 6.1 shows what that means on real data: one parking-garage dataset, one cost function, and three trajectories that are all local minima. Only one is the global minimum; the other two came from random starting points. <span class="src">Figure 6.1 · p. 151</span>
@@ 22
The book adds that this is not bad luck. Several special cases of SLAM, among them angular synchronization, rotation averaging, and pose-graph optimization (PGO), are <strong>NP-hard</strong>: unless P = NP, no algorithm solves every instance efficiently. <span class="src">§6.1 · p. 150</span> In practice SLAM often works anyway: odometry gives a good initial guess, and several optimization methods reach near-optimal solutions even from poor ones. But off-the-shelf local methods can also produce badly wrong estimates on well-posed problems. <span class="src">§6.1 · p. 150</span>

This does not contradict NP-hardness. On a hard instance the method is allowed to return a bound with a gap and no proof of optimality. The book gives two reasons it is still worth having: for the noise levels of real SLAM problems the gap is nearly always zero, and when it is not, the failure is itself a warning the robot can act on. <span class="src">§6.1 · p. 151</span>
@@ 44
A local solver is a hiker in fog who stops when every direction goes up. A certifiable solver also carries an altimeter reading for the lowest point on the whole map. If the hiker's altitude matches it, the hiker is at the bottom. If not, the difference says how much lower the true bottom could be.

5. Relaxation: an easier problem that bounds the hard one
@@ 74
A <strong>relaxation</strong> of a minimization problem keeps the objective and enlarges the feasible set. Minimizing over more candidates can only lower the minimum, so the relaxed optimum \(d^\star\) is a lower bound on \(p^\star\). And if the relaxed minimizer happens to lie in the original feasible set, it is optimal for the original problem too, because nothing in the larger set beats it.
@@ 75
A one-line example. Minimize \((x-0.4)^2\) over the two points \(x\in\{0,1\}\): the best is \(x=0\) with \(p^\star=0.16\). Relax the set to the interval \([0,1]\), which is convex: the minimizer is \(x=0.4\) with \(d^\star=0\). The bound \(0\le0.16\) holds but has a gap, and \(0.4\) is not feasible. Now change the objective to \((x+0.3)^2\). The relaxed minimizer is \(x=0\), an original point, so \(p^\star=d^\star=0.09\) and the relaxation has solved the hard problem. When that happens the relaxation is called <strong>tight</strong> or <strong>exact</strong>.
@@ 76
Day 2 builds such a relaxation for a whole class of problems, Days 3 and 4 apply it to pose-graph optimization, and Day 5 solves it fast.
