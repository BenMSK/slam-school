@@ 11
3DGS keeps the blending sum of Day 2 and changes where the opacities come from. The scene is a list of semi-transparent Gaussian blobs. To render, flatten each blob onto the image, sort the blobs by depth, and blend the ones that cover a pixel from front to back. No samples are spent on empty space, and every step is differentiable, so the blobs can be fitted to photos by gradient descent.

1. What one Gaussian stores
@@ 13
Each primitive has a mean position \(\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \R^3\), a covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \R^{3\times 3}\) that sets its size, shape, and orientation, an opacity \(o\), and a colour \(\mathbf{c}\) that can vary with viewing direction, stored as spherical-harmonic (SH) coefficients. The input is a set of colour images with poses from structure from motion (SfM), together with the sparse point cloud SfM produced, which seeds the initial Gaussians. <span class="src">§14.3 · p. 409</span>

Filling the gap: why factor the covariance?
@@ 17
A covariance must be symmetric and positive semi-definite: \(\mathbf{v}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{v} \ge 0\) for every \(\mathbf{v}\). If gradient descent adjusted the six free entries of \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) directly, a step could leave that set and the "Gaussian" would stop being one. The factored form cannot fail: with \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\), \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{v} = \norm{\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{v}}^2 \ge 0\) for any \(\mathbf{S}\) and \(\mathbf{R}\).
@@ 18
A 2D example. Radii \(2\) and \(0.5\), rotated by 30°: \(\cos^2 = 0.75\), \(\sin^2 = 0.25\), \(\cos\sin = 0.433\), and \(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}\T = \diag(4, 0.25)\).
@@ 19
Check: the trace is \(4.25 = 4 + 0.25\) and the determinant is \(3.0625 \times 1.1875 - 1.6238^2 = 1.00 = 4 \times 0.25\), as rotation must preserve both.

Filling the gap: deriving equation (14.5)
@@ 31
<strong>Step 1: into the camera frame.</strong> A world point becomes \(\mathbf{x}_c = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{t}\) in camera coordinates, with \(\mathbf{W}\) the rotation part of the world-to-camera transform. A linear map of a Gaussian is again a Gaussian, with covariance \(\cov(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}\T\). So in the camera frame the mean is \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c = \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{t}\) and the covariance is \(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}\T\). This step is exact.
@@ 32
<strong>Step 2: onto the plane \(z = 1\).</strong> Perspective projection (<a href="#d63">Week 11 · Day 3</a>) is \(\pi(x, y, z) = (x/z,\; y/z)\). It divides by \(z\), so it is not linear and the true image of a Gaussian is not a Gaussian. Replace \(\pi\) by its first-order Taylor expansion around the mean, exactly as measurement functions were linearized in <a href="#d9">Week 2 · Day 3</a>:
@@ 33
with \((x, y, z) = \boldsymbol{\mu}_c\). The approximate map is linear, so the same covariance rule applies again: \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}\T)\mathbf{J}\T\), a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, centred at \(\boldsymbol{\mu}' = \pi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c)\). The book speaks of the upper-left \(2 \times 2\) block because the usual derivation carries a third row in \(\mathbf{J}\), which makes \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'\) a \(3 \times 3\) matrix whose image part is that block. To get pixel units, multiply \(\mathbf{J}\) by the focal length.

Whatever \(T\) remains at the end goes to the background. The same picture results from painting back to front with the "over" rule \(C \leftarrow \alpha_i\mathbf{c}_i + (1 - \alpha_i)\,C\): for two layers over a background \(\mathbf{b}\), both give \(\alpha_1\mathbf{c}_1 + (1 - \alpha_1)\alpha_2\mathbf{c}_2 + (1 - \alpha_1)(1 - \alpha_2)\mathbf{b}\). 4. Making it fast, and making it learn
@@ 47
<strong>Sorting and culling.</strong> Blending needs the Gaussians in depth order from the viewpoint. The image is divided into tiles and the sort is done per tile with a GPU radix sort. Gaussians outside the camera's view frustum are skipped (<strong>frustum culling</strong>). <span class="src">§14.3.1 · p. 410</span>
@@ 48
<strong>The loss.</strong> The parameters of all Gaussians are fitted by minimizing
@@ 49
between the rendered and the real image, with a weight \(\lambda\). <span class="src">§14.3.1 · p. 410</span> \(\mathcal{L}_1\) is the mean absolute difference of pixel values. SSIM, the structural similarity index, compares small patches of the two images by their local brightness, contrast, and pattern, so the second term rewards getting local structure right and not only each pixel on its own.
@@ 50
<strong>Changing the number of Gaussians.</strong> Gradient descent can move, reshape, and recolour Gaussians, but it cannot create one. During optimization, <strong>adaptive densification and pruning</strong> adjust the count based on the Gaussians' gradients and opacities. <span class="src">§14.3.1 · p. 410 · Fig. 14.14</span> In the original method, a large gradient signals a region the current Gaussians cannot explain, where more are added, and a Gaussian whose opacity has fallen to almost nothing is removed.

@@ 53
<strong>"The opacity o is the α in the blend."</strong> \(\alpha_i = o_i\,G_{2D}\) also includes the footprint's falloff. They are equal only at the footprint's centre.
@@ 54
<strong>"A sum does not care about order."</strong> Each term carries the product of \((1 - \alpha_j)\) over everything in front. Swap two Gaussians and their weights change.
@@ 55
<strong>"The projected footprint is exact."</strong> It comes from linearizing the projection at the mean. The error grows for Gaussians that are large compared with their distance from the camera.
@@ 56
<strong>"3DGS traces rays through the Gaussians."</strong> It loops over Gaussians and writes each to the pixels its footprint covers. That is rasterization.

Q1. In a 2D world, a Gaussian sits on the optical axis at (x, z) = (0, 4) with Σ = diag(0.16, 0.09). The camera is at the origin. What is the standard deviation σ′ of its footprint on the image line z = 1?
@@ 58
J = [1/z, −x/z²] = [0.25, 0]. σ′² = 0.25² × 0.16 = 0.01, so σ′ = 0.1. On the axis the depth variance 0.09 has no first-order effect.
@@ 59
Q2. At one pixel a red Gaussian in front has α = 0.6 and a blue one behind it has α = 0.5. What is the weight of the blue Gaussian?
@@ 60
Blue's weight is its α times the transmittance in front of it: 0.5 × (1 − 0.6) = 0.2. Red has 0.6, and 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2 reaches the background.
@@ 61
Q3. A Gaussian has opacity o = 0.8 and a 1D footprint with standard deviation σ′. What is its α at a pixel that lies 2σ′ from the footprint's centre?
@@ 62
G = exp(−½ · 2²) = e⁻² = 0.135, so α = 0.8 × 0.135 = 0.108.

Q4. Why is the covariance stored as R S Sᵀ Rᵀ and not as a free symmetric matrix?
@@ 64
It uses fewer numbers
@@ 65
It makes the projection to the image exact
@@ 66

It removes the need to sort by depth

Q5. Equations (14.2) and (14.6) are the same blending sum. What differs?
@@ 70
3DGS blends back to front, NeRF front to back
@@ 71
Where αᵢ comes from: samples of a density field along the ray in NeRF, projected primitives covering the pixel in 3DGS
@@ 72
NeRF's weights sum to 1 and 3DGS's do not
@@ 73
Only NeRF's sum is differentiable
@@ 74
Both are front-to-back alpha blending with transmittance ∏(1 − αⱼ). NeRF spends samples everywhere on the ray. 3DGS only visits the primitives that land on the pixel.
@@ 75
A 3D Gaussian stores a mean, a covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}\T\mathbf{R}\T\), an opacity, and a view-dependent colour.
@@ 76
Its footprint in the image has covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}' = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{W}\T\mathbf{J}\T\): rotate into the camera frame, then linearize the projection at the mean.
@@ 77
A pixel is \(\sum_i \mathbf{c}_i\alpha_i\prod_{j \lt i}(1 - \alpha_j)\) with \(\alpha_i = o_i G_{2D}\), over the depth-sorted Gaussians that cover it.
@@ 78
Tiles, a per-tile sort, and frustum culling make this fast. An L1 plus SSIM loss, with densification and pruning, makes it learn.
