1. A Gaussian process, and the plan
@@ 13
A <strong>Gaussian process (GP)</strong> is a probability distribution over functions with one defining property: the values of the function at any finite set of times are jointly Gaussian. It is fixed by a mean function and a kernel function \(\mathcal{K}(t,t')\), which gives the covariance between the values at two times. We write \(\mathbf{x}(t) \sim \mathcal{GP}(\mathbf{0}, \mathcal{K}(t,t'))\). Yesterday you saw that the kernel matrix is the prior covariance of the trajectory at the measurement times, so choosing a kernel is the same as choosing a GP prior.
@@ 14
Yesterday also ended with a problem: equation (2.45) needs \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\), which is dense for a general kernel. The book's plan is to construct kernels whose inverse is sparse by design. <span class="src">§2.2.3 · p. 66</span>

@@ 47
Write \(\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{x}(t_i)\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{i,j} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}(t_i,t_j)\). Call the initial state \(\mathbf{w}_1 = \mathbf{x}_1\), and use the one-step form from section 3:
@@ 48
The \(\mathbf{w}_i\) are independent, because they integrate white noise over intervals that do not overlap. Their covariances are \(\mathcal{K}(t_1,t_1), \mathbf{Q}_2, \dots, \mathbf{Q}_M\). Stack these on the diagonal of a block-diagonal matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\). (Figure 2.4 labels each step covariance by the index of the interval's first state, so its \(\mathbf{Q}_1\) is the \(\mathbf{Q}_2\) here.)
@@ 49
<strong>Forward.</strong> Unroll the recursion: \(\mathbf{x}_2 = \boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2,1}\mathbf{w}_1 + \mathbf{w}_2\), \(\mathbf{x}_3 = \boldsymbol{\Phi}_{3,1}\mathbf{w}_1 + \boldsymbol{\Phi}_{3,2}\mathbf{w}_2 + \mathbf{w}_3\), and so on, using \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{3,2}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2,1} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}_{3,1}\). Every state is a sum over all earlier noises. Stacked, \(\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w}\) with the full lower-triangular \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) of equation (2.50). The covariance of a linear function of \(\mathbf{w}\) is then \(\mathbf{K} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\T\).
@@ 50
<strong>Backward.</strong> Solve the same recursion for the noises: \(\mathbf{w}_1 = \mathbf{x}_1\) and \(\mathbf{w}_i = \mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\Phi}_{i,i-1}\mathbf{x}_{i-1}\). Each noise involves only two neighbouring states. Stacked, \(\mathbf{w} = \boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\), and you can read off \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-1}\) with no computation: identity blocks on the diagonal and \(-\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{i,i-1}\) just below it, which is equation (2.51).

5. Markov, and why the state carries velocity
@@ 58
The inverse is sparse because the state of the SDE is <strong>Markovian</strong>: once you know \(\mathbf{x}_{i-1}\), nothing earlier helps to predict \(\mathbf{x}_i\). <span class="src">§2.2.3 · p. 68</span> In <a href="#d13">Week 3 · Day 1</a> you saw that a zero block in the information matrix means "no factor between these two variables". Here it is the same statement, and \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\) is the information matrix of the prior.
@@ 59
The price is that the state must contain everything the model needs to predict the next step. For the constant-velocity prior a position alone is not enough: to predict where the robot goes next you also need its velocity. So the state is \(\mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{v})\), as in equation (2.52), and the estimator solves for a velocity at every estimation time alongside the pose. The book notes this variant is sometimes called <strong>simultaneous trajectory estimation and mapping (STEAM)</strong>. <span class="src">§2.2.3 · p. 68</span>
@@ 60
The widget's third option shows what happens if you refuse. Dropping the velocities from the constant-velocity kernel is the marginalization of <a href="#d15">Week 3 · Day 3</a>, and it fills the information matrix in: positions alone are not Markovian, because two earlier positions together reveal the velocity.
@@ 61
The prior is a rule of the form "the next state should be close to what the current state predicts". A rule like that only ever compares neighbours. A thousand such rules in a row describe a smooth path of any length, and none of them mentions two states that are far apart.
@@ 62
<strong>"A sparse inverse means distant poses are uncorrelated."</strong> No. \(\mathbf{K}\) is full: every pose is correlated with every other. The zeros are in \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\), and mean there is no <em>direct</em> factor between distant poses.
@@ 63
<strong>"Constant-velocity prior means the robot moves at constant velocity."</strong> It means deviations from constant velocity are penalized, with a strength set by \(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\). The measurements can still bend the trajectory.
@@ 64
<strong>"The velocities are extra work."</strong> They are what keeps the factor graph a chain. Leaving them out makes the prior dense.
