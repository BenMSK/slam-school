Semantic SLAM has unknowns you can differentiate (poses, shapes) and unknowns you can only enumerate (classes, associations). Fix either kind and the other becomes easy: least squares for the continuous ones, a small choice per measurement for the discrete ones. Hybrid solvers alternate, with hard choices or soft weights.
@@ 10
1. Where the discrete unknowns come from
@@ 11
Discrete variables enter in four ways: the <strong>class</strong> \(\sigma\) of each object, which is tied to its shape; <strong>symmetries</strong> (a square table looks the same every 90°); several <strong>pose hypotheses</strong> from a learned front-end; and <strong>data association</strong>, the question of which mapped object produced a detection (<a href="#d31">Week 6 · Day 1</a>). <span class="src">§16.2.2 · p. 466</span>
@@ 12
Put together, the estimation problem is a MAP problem over robot poses \(X\), landmarks \(L\), and associations \(D\), given all measurements \(Z\): <span class="src">§16.2.2 · p. 467</span>
@@ 13
Classes and associations belong in one problem because they inform each other: a detection labelled "table" is unlikely to be a chair. The price is a search space that is combinatorial as well as nonconvex. If 6 detections can each come from any of 6 objects there are \(6^6 = 46{,}656\) assignments, and in the worst case each needs its own continuous optimization. <span class="src">§16.2.2 · p. 468</span>

2. The hybrid factor graph
@@ 15
Group the continuous variables into \(\Theta\) and the discrete ones into \(D\). The posterior factors as before, and taking the negative logarithm turns the product into a sum: <span class="src">§16.2.2 · p. 468</span>
@@ 16
\(V_k\) is the set of variables factor \(k\) touches. There are three kinds of factor: discrete \(\phi_k(D_k)\), continuous \(\phi_k(\Theta_k)\), and <strong>discrete-continuous</strong> \(\phi_k(\Theta_k, D_k)\). The last kind is assumed to be a squared residual once the discrete value is fixed: <span class="src">§16.2.2 · p. 469</span>

A detection factor is the typical case. Its discrete variable \(d_k \in \{1, \dots, J\}\) picks which landmark the residual is computed against: \(r_k = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\left(\mathbf{z}_k - h(\mathbf{T}, \boldsymbol{\ell}_{d_k})\right)\).

3. Alternating minimization: fix one kind, solve the other
@@ 22
Two observations make a local method possible. <span class="src">§16.2.2 · p. 469</span>
@@ 23
With \(D\) fixed, only continuous variables remain, and the Gauss-Newton tools of <a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a> apply.
@@ 24
With \(\Theta\) fixed, what remains is a discrete factor graph. If the discrete variables split into small groups \(D_j\) that are conditionally independent given \(\Theta\),
\[ p(D \mid \Theta, Z) \propto \prod_j p(D_j \mid \Theta, Z) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \max_D\, p(D \mid \Theta, Z) \propto \prod_j \Big[\max_{D_j} p(D_j \mid \Theta, Z)\Big], \tag{16.20, 16.21} \]
each group can be optimized on its own.

4. Expectation-maximization: soft associations
@@ 33
Bowman et al. keep a probability for every candidate where alternating minimization commits to one. <span class="src">§16.2.2 · p. 470</span>
@@ 34
<strong>E-step.</strong> Fix poses and landmarks. For each detection \(k\) compute the probability that it came from object \(j\):
\[ w_{kj} = \frac{\pi_{kj}\; \N\!\big(\mathbf{z}_k;\, h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j),\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big)}{\sum_{j'} \pi_{kj'}\; \N\!\big(\mathbf{z}_k;\, h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_{j'}),\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big)}, \qquad \pi_{kj} = p(\text{label of } k \mid \text{class of } j). \]
@@ 35
<strong>M-step.</strong> Fix the weights. Optimize poses and landmarks with every detection-object pair counted in proportion to its weight:
\[ \Theta \leftarrow \argmin_\Theta \sum_k \sum_j w_{kj}\, \norm{\mathbf{z}_k - h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} + (\text{other factors}). \]
@@ 36
The loop converges gradually to a local optimum. The E-step probabilities can be computed exactly through a matrix permanent, or more cheaply by approximating it. <span class="src">§16.2.2 · p. 470</span>

Filling the gap: one detection, three answers
@@ 38
A robot in a corridor believes it is at \(p = 2.5\) m. The map has a table at \(4.0\) m and a chair at \(5.2\) m. A detection says "a chair, \(z = 2.0\) m ahead", which puts the object at \(4.5\) m: \(0.5\) m from the table and \(0.7\) m from the chair. Take \(\sigma = 0.3\) m, and a detector whose label is right 90% of the time.
@@ 39
The geometry term \(e^{-d^2/2\sigma^2}\) is \(e^{-1.389} = 0.249\) for the table and \(e^{-2.722} = 0.066\) for the chair. The label term \(\pi\) is \(0.1\) for the table and \(0.9\) for the chair. The products are \(0.0249\) and \(0.0592\), which normalize to weights \(0.30\) and \(0.70\).
@@ 40
<strong>Nearest neighbour</strong> looks at distance only and picks the table. The detection then says the robot is at \(4.0 - 2.0 = 2.0\) m.
@@ 41
<strong>Max-mixture</strong> picks the largest product, the chair: \(5.2 - 2.0 = 3.2\) m.
@@ 42
<strong>EM</strong> uses both: \(0.30 \times 2.0 + 0.70 \times 3.2 = 2.84\) m. Without the label the weights would be \(0.79\) and \(0.21\), and the answer \(2.25\) m.
@@ 43
Why the M-step is a weighted least squares: EM maximizes the log-likelihood averaged over the associations, \(\sum_k\sum_j w_{kj} \log\big[\pi_{kj}\,\N(\mathbf{z}_k; h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j), \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\big]\), and its only part that depends on \(\Theta\) is \(-\tfrac12\sum_k\sum_j w_{kj}\norm{\mathbf{z}_k - h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\). It is the "weights, then weighted least squares" loop of IRLS (<a href="#d34">Week 6 · Day 4</a>) with probabilities as weights.

5. The three methods on one problem
@@ 45
Try it: a robot returns to a mapped room with four chairs and two tables. Its odometry has drifted 0.8 m, more than half the spacing of the row. Press Step three times. Then switch the class labels off and repeat. Then try EM with the labels still off.
@@ 46
Notice that with max-mixture and labels, three steps take the error from 0.62 to 0.23 to 0.03 m and the wrong associations from 3 to 1 to 0, although detection z4 carries a wrong label. Without labels the same method stays at 0.82 m with four wrong associations, and so does nearest neighbour. EM without labels still escapes, in about six steps, because each wrong pairing leaves some weight on the right object. At \(\sigma = 0.15\) m all three are stuck: an overconfident noise model turns soft weights into hard ones. At \(\sigma = 0.8\) m the wrong label on z4 outvotes the geometry.
@@ 47
The book names three more families. <strong>Multi-hypothesis</strong> solvers (MH-iSAM2, iMHS) keep several assignments alive and prune the search. <strong>Continuous relaxations</strong> replace a label by a probability vector on the simplex and optimize it as a continuous variable. <strong>Marginalization</strong> sums the discrete variables out, which leaves a non-Gaussian mixture over \(\Theta\) for specialized solvers. <span class="src">§16.2.2 · pp. 470–471</span>

Q2. A detection is at distance 0.3 m from candidate A and 0.6 m from candidate B, with σ = 0.3 m and no label information. What is the EM weight of A?
@@ 55
Geometry terms: exp(−0.09/0.18) = 0.607 for A and exp(−0.36/0.18) = 0.135 for B. Weight of A = 0.607/(0.607 + 0.135) = 0.82.

Classes, symmetries, pose hypotheses, and associations are discrete unknowns. With poses and shapes they form the MAP problem (16.16), a hybrid factor graph with cost \(\mathcal{L}(\Theta, D)\).
@@ 69
Fixing \(D\) leaves least squares. Fixing \(\Theta\) leaves a discrete problem that splits into conditionally independent groups.
@@ 70
Alternating minimization and max-mixtures make hard choices, re-made every iteration. EM keeps weights \(w_{kj}\) and solves a weighted least squares.
@@ 71
All of them are local. Labels and a sensible noise model decide whether the local answer is the right one.
