About 75 min · No new pages
@@ 5
A legged robot can read its own motion from its joints. Encoders give the joint angles; forward kinematics turns them into the foot's pose in the base frame, and the Jacobian turns joint rates into foot velocity. While a foot stands still, the foot's motion seen from the body is the body's motion seen from the ground, with the sign flipped. That gives a relative-pose measurement, \(\mathbf{f}_p(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{R}\T(\mathbf{c}_i - \mathbf{t})\), and a velocity measurement, \(\mathbf{v} = -\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{f}_p - \mathbf{J}_v\dot{\mathbf{q}}\). Both depend on knowing which feet are truly stationary, which is decided by thresholding the normal force as a stand-in for the friction cone. With covariances from encoder noise and from a slip model, the measurements become factors: forward-kinematics and contact factors for bipeds, a preintegrated velocity factor for quadrupeds. Slip, soft ground, and bending legs break the assumptions and appear as biased motion.

A planar leg has \(l_1 = l_2 = 0.3\) m and joint angles \(q = (45^\circ, -90^\circ)\). (a) Find the foot position \(\mathbf{f}_p\) and the Jacobian \(\mathbf{J}_v\). (b) Check the determinant against \(l_1 l_2 \sin q_2\). (c) The joint rates are \(\dot{\mathbf{q}} = (-2,\ 1)\) rad/s, the foot is in stance, and the body does not rotate. What is the body velocity?
@@ 53
(a) \(q_1 + q_2 = -45^\circ\) and \(\sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = 0.707\). \(x = 0.3(0.707) + 0.3(-0.707) = 0\), \(z = -0.3(0.707) - 0.3(0.707) = -0.424\) m. The Jacobian is
@@ 54
(b) \(\det = 0.424 \times (-0.212) = -0.090\), and \(l_1 l_2 \sin q_2 = 0.09 \times (-1) = -0.09\). They agree.
@@ 55
(c) Foot velocity: \(\mathbf{J}_v\dot{\mathbf{q}} = (0.424(-2) + 0.212(1),\ -0.212(1)) = (-0.636,\ -0.212)\) m/s. With no rotation, \(\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{J}_v\dot{\mathbf{q}} = (0.636,\ 0.212)\) m/s: the body moves forward and rises.

An upright planar biped starts with its base at \(\mathbf{t} = (0,\ 0.50)\) m. Leg A is in stance with \(\mathbf{f}_p^A = (0.10,\ -0.50)\) m. At the end of A's stance, \(\mathbf{f}_p^A = (-0.10,\ -0.50)\) and leg B touches down with \(\mathbf{f}_p^B = (0.12,\ -0.50)\). Later in B's stance, \(\mathbf{f}_p^B = (-0.08,\ -0.52)\). (a) Find the two contact points and the final base position. (b) If foot A in fact slid 3 cm backward during its stance, where is the base really, and does the error go away once B is planted?
@@ 57
(a) Use \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{t} + \mathbf{f}_p\) at touchdown and \(\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{f}_p\) afterwards. \(\mathbf{c}_A = (0, 0.50) + (0.10, -0.50) = (0.10,\ 0)\). End of A's stance: \(\mathbf{t} = (0.10, 0) - (-0.10, -0.50) = (0.20,\ 0.50)\). Then \(\mathbf{c}_B = (0.20, 0.50) + (0.12, -0.50) = (0.32,\ 0)\). Finally \(\mathbf{t} = (0.32, 0) - (-0.08, -0.52) = (0.40,\ 0.52)\) m.
@@ 58
(b) During A's stance the body really advanced \(0.20 - 0.03 = 0.17\) m, so the true final position is \(x = 0.37\) m. The error does not go away: \(\mathbf{c}_B\) was computed from the wrong \(\mathbf{t}\), so it is 3 cm too far forward, and every later estimate inherits that.
@@ 59
The leg of problem 1 stands still (ignore \(\mathbf{h}\)) and its motors hold \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = (-8.49,\ 15.0)\) N·m. (a) Find the ground reaction force \((f_x, f_z)\). (b) Does the foot hold on a floor with \(\mu = 0.3\)? With \(\mu = 0.2\)? (c) What would a 50 N threshold on \(f_z\) conclude in each case?
@@ 60
(a) Solve \(\mathbf{J}_v\T\mathbf{f} = -\boldsymbol{\tau}\) with \(\mathbf{J}_v\T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.424 & 0 \\ 0.212 & -0.212 \end{bmatrix}\). First row: \(0.424 f_x = 8.49\), so \(f_x = 20.0\) N. Second row: \(0.212(20.0) - 0.212 f_z = -15.0\), so \(f_z = (15.0 + 4.24)/0.212 = 90.7\) N.
@@ 61
(b) The cone allows \(\mu f_z\): \(0.3 \times 90.7 = 27.2\) N, which is more than 20 N, so the foot holds. With \(\mu = 0.2\) the limit is 18.1 N, less than 20 N: the foot slips.
@@ 62
(c) \(f_z = 90.7\) N exceeds 50 N in both cases, so the detector reports contact both times. On the slippery floor it is wrong, and leg odometry would use a moving foot.
@@ 63
(Stretch.) In the walking widget of <a href="#d105">Day 3</a>, the rows of \(\mathbf{J}_v\) that map joint noise to the foot's \(x\) coordinate are \((0.65,\ 0.275)\) for the leg that lifts and \((0.65,\ 0.375)\) for the leg that lands, in m/rad. Each encoder reading has independent noise \(\sigma_q = 0.3^\circ\). (a) Find the 1σ error in \(x\) added at one foot switch. (b) The foot also slips 3 mm per step. After how many steps does slip exceed the noise-driven error? (c) Give both after 100 steps, as a fraction of the 30 m walked.
@@ 64
(a) \(\sigma_q = 0.3 \times \pi/180 = 0.00524\) rad. Each leg contributes \(\sigma_q\) times the length of its row: \(0.00524\sqrt{0.65^2 + 0.275^2} = 0.00524 \times 0.706 = 3.7\) mm and \(0.00524\sqrt{0.65^2 + 0.375^2} = 0.00524 \times 0.750 = 3.9\) mm. They are independent, so \(\sigma_{\text{step}} = \sqrt{3.7^2 + 3.9^2} = 5.4\) mm.
@@ 65
(b) Noise grows as \(5.4\sqrt{n}\) mm, slip as \(3n\) mm. They are equal when \(\sqrt{n} = 5.4/3 = 1.8\), that is \(n = 3.2\). From the fourth step on, slip is the larger error.
@@ 66
(c) Noise: \(5.4\sqrt{100} = 54\) mm, 0.18% of 30 m. Slip: \(300\) mm, 1%. Over long distances, the bias decides the drift rate, and no encoder upgrade changes it.

4. Part II in review: six sensors, one factor graph
@@ 68
Part I built the back-end: a factor graph and the tools to optimize it. Part II was about front-ends: how each sensor's raw data becomes factors. The Prelude's organizing idea is that almost every front-end has the same modules. An <strong>odometry</strong> module produces a relative measurement between consecutive nodes. A <strong>loop closure</strong> module first recognizes a place and then estimates a relative pose to an old node. Some sensors add <strong>unary factors</strong> on single nodes, such as GPS. <span class="src">§II.1 · pp. 185–187</span> Each chapter then filled in those modules for one sensor.

Three ideas recurred across the chapters. Odometry drifts, whatever the sensor, and only loop closures or absolute measurements bound it (<a href="#d61">Week 11 · Day 1</a>). High-rate measurements are summarized between keyframes by preintegration, first for the IMU (<a href="#d94">Week 16 · Day 4</a>), then for contacts and leg velocities (<a href="#d107">Day 5</a>). And every sensor leaves some directions unobservable: scale for one camera, global position and yaw for a camera with an IMU (<a href="#d98">Week 17 · Day 2</a>), so a second modality is added to fill them.
