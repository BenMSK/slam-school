SLAM back-ends went through three designs: the extended Kalman filter, particle filters, and optimization over a factor graph. The third won because of one structural fact: the inverse of the covariance matrix, the <strong>information matrix</strong>, is sparse. Information is also the quantity that behaves simply: when independent measurements are combined, their information adds.

1. Three generations of back-ends
@@ 12
Estimating a map from noisy measurements is much older than robotics. Gauss triangulated the Kingdom of Hannover in the 1820s, and Cholesky invented the matrix factorization that carries his name while surveying, before the First World War. <span class="src">§I.4.1 · p. 13</span> You will use that factorization on <a href="#d5">Day 5</a>.
@@ 13
In robotics, the early work reached two insights. To avoid drift in an unknown environment you must estimate the robot poses and the positions of fixed external things (landmarks) together. And an existing tool, the <strong>extended Kalman filter (EKF)</strong>, can do it if you stack the robot pose and all landmark positions into one long state. An EKF keeps a single Gaussian belief over that state, a mean and a covariance matrix, and updates both after every measurement, using a linear approximation of the motion and measurement models. The acronym SLAM dates from 1995. <span class="src">§I.4.1 · p. 13</span>

2. Where the field is heading, and how the book is laid out
@@ 31
SLAM estimates geometry. That is enough for "go to position (x, y, z)" and not enough for "pick up the clothes in the bathroom and take them to the laundry room", which needs the robot to know what a bathroom is. <strong>Spatial AI</strong> is the name for a perception system that reasons about geometry, semantics, and possibly physics together, with SLAM as the submodule that handles geometry. It closes a third, slower loop over task planning, on top of the two loops from <a href="#d2">Day 2</a>. <span class="src">§I.4.2 · pp. 16–17</span>
@@ 32
The handbook has three parts. Part I (Chapters 1–6) is foundations: factor graphs and optimization, poses on manifolds, outliers, differentiable optimization, map representations, and advanced solvers. Part II (Chapters 7–12) is the state of practice, one sensor per chapter: cameras, LiDAR, radar, event cameras, inertial sensors, and wheel and legged odometry. Part III (Chapters 13–18) covers trends, from learned components and new map representations to Spatial AI and foundation models. <span class="src">§I.5 · pp. 17–18</span> The history above is the history of the back-end, which is why Part I comes first.

4. See it in two dimensions
@@ 62
A worked example first. Estimate A of a landmark is \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_A = (0, 0)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_A = \diag(4,\ 0.25)\): vague in \(x\) (σ = 2), sharp in \(y\) (σ = 0.5). Estimate B is \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_B = (2, 1)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_B = \diag(0.25,\ 4)\): the other way round.
@@ 63
The fused estimate takes its \(x\) almost entirely from B and its \(y\) almost entirely from A: each is trusted where it is sharp.
@@ 64
Try it: drag the centres A and B, and turn the two ellipses with the sliders. Each ellipse is the 1σ contour of a Gaussian with half-axes 1.5 and 0.4.
@@ 65
Notice: at the default settings the two ellipses are at right angles and the fused ellipse is a circle of radius 0.39, tighter than the short axis of either input and a quarter of A's area. The fused mean is not midway between A and B; it sits near the point where the two long axes cross. Set both sliders to the same angle and the fused ellipse stays long (1.06 × 0.28), because neither estimate knows that direction. Drag A or B: the fused mean moves, but the fused ellipse never changes size.
@@ 66
An elongated ellipse says "the landmark is somewhere along this line". Two such statements along different lines pin down a point, the way two bearings fix a ship's position. The last thing you noticed is a general property of linear-Gaussian fusion: the fused covariance depends on how good the measurements are, not on what they read.

5. Why the information matrix is sparse
@@ 68
The book's key sentence: the EKF's covariance matrix is dense, but its inverse, the information matrix, is very sparse with a predictable pattern, as long as past robot poses are kept in the state. <span class="src">§I.4.1 · p. 15</span> The smallest example shows why. A robot moves along a line through poses \(x_0, x_1, x_2\). There is a prior on \(x_0\) and two odometry measurements, \(u_1 = x_1 - x_0 + \text{noise}\) and \(u_2 = x_2 - x_1 + \text{noise}\), all with \(\sigma = 1\).

@@ 79
The same belief in two forms. The information matrix has an entry only where a factor connects two variables. The covariance has an entry wherever two variables are correlated, which is everywhere.
@@ 80
Read the two matrices. \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is full: every pose is correlated with every other, because an error in \(x_0\) is inherited by all later poses. Its diagonal, 1, 2, 3, is the random-walk drift from <a href="#d1">Day 1</a>: one unit of variance per step. \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) has a zero between \(x_0\) and \(x_2\) because no measurement involves both. For a chain of \(n\) poses, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) has \(n^2\) non-zero entries and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) has \(3n-2\). With \(n = 10{,}000\) that is \(10^8\) numbers against about 30,000. An EKF stores and updates \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\), which is the third EKF problem in the table of Section 1; a factor-graph solver works with \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\).
@@ 81
The condition "as long as past poses are kept" matters. A filter removes old poses from the state, and removing a variable from a Gaussian connects all the variables it was connected to, so the zeros of \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) fill in. Keeping the whole trajectory makes the state larger but keeps \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) sparse.
@@ 82
<strong>"Fusing means averaging the covariances."</strong> Covariances never add when fusing estimates of the same quantity. Information matrices add; the fused covariance is smaller than either input.
@@ 83
<strong>"A zero in Λ means the two variables are uncorrelated."</strong> \(x_0\) and \(x_2\) have a zero in \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) and covariance 1 in \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). The zero means only that no factor links them directly.
