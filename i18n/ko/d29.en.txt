@@ 13
Poses cannot be added or scaled, so a weighted sum of poses means nothing on SE(d). The <em>difference</em> between two neighbouring poses, however, can be turned into an ordinary vector with Log, scaled or given a Gaussian prior there, and turned back with Exp. Splines reach this through cumulative basis functions. Gaussian processes reach it through a local variable defined between control points. For the simplest case of each, the result is the same interpolation formula.

@@ 18

The \(\mathbf{p}_k\) are now called <strong>control points</strong>. @@ 22
In the double sum, the step \(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_{j-1}\) is multiplied by every \(\psi_k\) with \(k \ge j\). Collecting them gives \(\sum_{j=2}^{K}\big(\sum_{k=j}^{K}\psi_k\big)(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_{j-1})\), which is (2.54). The first bracket is \(\psi^c_1\), and it equals 1 wherever the basis functions sum to one.
@@ 23
For a linear spline with knots \(T\) seconds apart, each \(\psi^c_k\) is a ramp: 0 before its segment, rising linearly from 0 to 1 across it, and 1 afterwards (equations (2.58)–(2.59), lower half of Figure 2.5). Write \(\alpha_k(t)\) for the fraction of the segment that has passed. On the segment that ends at control point \(k\), every earlier step has weight 1, every later step has weight 0, and one step is in transition: <span class="src">§2.2.4.1 · p. 70</span>
@@ 24
