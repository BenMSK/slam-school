Yesterday's equations are only as good as the claim "this foot is not moving". For leg odometry a foot is <strong>in contact</strong> only when its contact point is stationary, which is a statement about friction: the ground reaction force must stay inside the <strong>friction cone</strong>. A large enough normal force guarantees that, so in practice contact estimation is a threshold on the normal force \(f_z\), and methods differ only in how they get \(f_z\).

Filling the gap: where the cone comes from
@@ 15
Inequality (12.22) is Coulomb's law of dry friction. Friction is the tangential part of the ground's force. It takes whatever value is needed to keep the foot from sliding, up to a ceiling proportional to how hard the surfaces are pressed together: \(\mu f_z\). Ask for more and the foot slides. Geometrically, the set of all forces that satisfy (12.22) is a cone standing on the contact point, with its axis along the surface normal and a half-angle of \(\arctan\mu\).

Filling the gap: the center of pressure
@@ 28
The ground pushes on a flat sole over an area. Replace that spread-out push by one force \(f_z\) acting at one point \((x_c, y_c)\) of the sole, the <strong>center of pressure</strong> (CoP). A force \((0, 0, f_z)\) applied at \((x_c, y_c, 0)\) has a torque about the foot frame's origin of \(\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{f} = (y_c f_z,\ -x_c f_z,\ 0)\). The right side of (12.23) holds those edge limits, which depend on the shape of the sole. Example: \(f_z = 400\) N and \(\tau_y = -20\) N·m give \(x_c = 0.05\) m, 5 cm ahead of the sensor. 3. Why a threshold on the normal force is enough
@@ 31
All three inequalities have \(f_z\) on the large side. Make \(f_z\) big enough and they hold whatever the other components of the contact wrench are doing. So the most common detector by far is: call the leg a stance leg when \(f_z\) exceeds a threshold. <span class="src">§12.3 · p. 346</span>
@@ 32
Try it: lower the threshold to 20 N, then raise it to 180 N. Then return it to 100 N and lower the friction coefficient to 0.2.
@@ 33
Notice the trade. At 100 N the detector agrees with the friction cone almost everywhere (2 wrong samples of 130). At 20 N it claims contact during 25 samples in which the foot is not actually holding. At 180 N it is never wrong but throws away 22% of the usable stance. With μ = 0.2 no threshold helps: at 100 N, 42% of the claimed contact is slipping, because the dashed "needed" curve now rises above the force the foot actually gets.
@@ 34
The widget also shows a third cost. With noise, the measured force crosses the threshold several times on its way up, so the detector reports 8 contact switches for 3 strides instead of 6. Real detectors require the condition to hold for some time before they believe it.
@@ 35
Press a finger lightly on a table and push sideways: it slides. Press hard and it sticks. A robot foot is the same, so "is this foot pressing hard?" is a good stand-in for "is this foot stuck?". It stops being good on ice, where even a hard press does not stick.

Two things are still missing before this force can be tested against the cone. <span class="src">§12.3.4 · pp. 347–348</span>
@@ 68
<strong>The slope of the ground.</strong> The estimate is in base coordinates, but "normal" and "tangential" are defined by the contact surface. A humanoid's ankle angles approximate the slope. A quadruped cannot know it without exteroceptive sensing, and falls back on heuristics such as fitting a plane through the other feet in contact.
@@ 69
<strong>The friction coefficient.</strong> It depends on the material underfoot. It must be known in advance or inferred from how much the robot is observed to slip.
@@ 70
The book calls contact estimation from joint sensing an open problem. Current answers are probabilistic detectors that combine kinematics with dynamics, and learned detectors (<a href="#d107">Day 5</a>). <span class="src">§12.3.4 · p. 348</span>
@@ 71
<strong>"In contact means touching."</strong> For leg odometry it means stationary. A foot that touches and slides must be treated as a swing foot.
@@ 72
<strong>"A higher threshold is always safer."</strong> It discards real stance time. With fewer legs counted as planted, there are more moments in which no leg supplies a measurement.
@@ 73
<strong>"Thresholding the normal force checks the friction cone."</strong> It assumes the friction coefficient is large enough. The detector never sees \(\mu\) or the tangential force.
