A network can be trained to output depth from one image or relative pose from two. Used alone, such predictions are less accurate than geometry. Used as extra factors in a factor graph, they fix what a single camera cannot: scale, robustness, and density. And the network does not need 3D labels: if depth and pose are right, one image can be re-drawn from the other, and the mismatch is a loss you can differentiate.

1. Depth from one image, and what SLAM gains from it
@@ 12
Give a network many pairs of an image and its depth map, from depth sensors, LiDARs, or rendering engines, and it learns to predict depth from a single image. The book describes the current recipe as a pre-trained vision transformer followed by a convolutional decoder that brings the coarse features back to image resolution, trained on a mix of more than 20 real and synthetic datasets so that it generalizes. <span class="src">§13.1.1 · p. 369</span>
@@ 13
A monocular SLAM system gains three things from such a network. <span class="src">§13.1.1 · p. 369</span>
@@ 14
<strong>Scale.</strong> One moving camera recovers the scene only up to a scale factor, and that factor drifts over time (<strong>scale drift</strong>). Depth predicted in meters pins it down.
@@ 15
<strong>Robustness.</strong> Predicted depth helps in cases that break monocular geometry: pure rotation, changing camera parameters, moving objects.
@@ 16
<strong>Density.</strong> Feature-based SLAM tracks a sparse set of points, and textureless areas barely appear in the map. A depth map has a value at every pixel.
@@ 17
Filling the gap: why one camera cannot see scale
@@ 18
A pinhole camera with focal length \(f\) and principal point \((c_x, c_y)\) projects a point \((X, Y, Z)\) in its own frame to the pixel
@@ 19
Multiply the whole world by \(s\): every point and every camera translation. A point's coordinates in any camera frame become \((sX, sY, sZ)\), and the ratios \(sX/sZ = X/Z\) do not change. Every image is identical, so no amount of image data can tell \(s\). A network that has seen many scenes with metric depth knows roughly how large cars and doorways are, and that is information the geometry does not contain. (Camera model: <a href="#d63">Week 11 · Day 3</a>.)
@@ 20
<strong>From depth maps to a dense map.</strong> Lifting every pixel into 3D with its predicted depth and the SLAM pose, and accumulating the points, gives a dense reconstruction. The book names two problems with doing only that, and two systems that go further. <span class="src">§13.1.1 · pp. 369–370</span>

Filling the gap: reading equation (13.1)
@@ 33
If the prediction is exact, \((\mathbf{T}^2_1)^{-1}\mathbf{T}^{2*}_1\) is the identity. Otherwise it is the leftover transformation between prediction and truth. The book does not say which norm measures its size. A standard choice, in the language of <a href="#d20">Week 4 · Day 2</a>, is the length of its tangent vector, \(\norm{\Log\!\big((\mathbf{T}^2_1)^{-1}\mathbf{T}^{2*}_1\big)}\), which is zero exactly at the identity.
@@ 34
Example: the network predicts a rotation of 10° about the vertical axis, the truth is 12° about the same axis, and the translations agree. The leftover is a rotation of 2°, and its tangent vector has length \(2 \cdot \pi / 180 = 0.035\) rad. The book mentions alternatives: penalize the rotation angle, or the distance between quaternions.
@@ 35
Early networks stacked the two frames and regressed axis-angle or Euler-angle parameters. Chaining relative poses gives a trajectory, and chained errors compound, as with any odometry (<a href="#d1">Week 1 · Day 1</a>). DeepVO addressed this with a recurrent network (an LSTM) that reads a sequence of feature maps and outputs a sequence of poses. TartanVO improved generalization by giving the network predicted optical flow and the camera intrinsics as inputs. <span class="src">§13.1.2 · p. 371</span>
@@ 36
The book's verdict is plain: predicting pose directly is often less accurate than classical SfM or SLAM, where the geometry is built into the system, and these networks tend to overfit to their training data. <span class="src">p. 371</span> That is the motivation for the rest of the chapter.

3. Training without 3D labels: view synthesis
@@ 38
Ground-truth depth and pose are expensive. <strong>View synthesis</strong> supplies a training signal from images alone. If you know the depth of every pixel of \(I_1\) and the relative pose, you know where each pixel of \(I_1\) appears in \(I_2\), so you can rebuild \(I_1\) out of colours taken from \(I_2\). If depth and pose are right, the rebuilt image matches \(I_1\). <span class="src">§13.1.3 · p. 371</span>

4. DVSO and D3VO: predictions as factors
@@ 64
<strong>DVSO</strong> (Deep Virtual Stereo Odometry) takes a classical direct system, DSO, and adds a factor to it: a term that pulls the depth computed by DSO toward the depth map predicted by a network. That network was trained with the view-synthesis loss on stereo pairs, where the distance between the two cameras is known in meters, so its depths are metric. The result behaves as if a second camera were present, hence "virtual stereo": better reconstruction, more accurate and robust poses, and correct scale from one camera. <span class="src">§13.1.3 · p. 372</span>
@@ 65
<strong>D3VO</strong> adds two more predictions. A pose network supplies relative motion between consecutive frames. And a network predicts <strong>uncertainty</strong>: where brightness is likely to stay constant between frames and where it is not. Direct methods assume a surface point keeps its brightness, which fails on shiny, metallic, or glass surfaces. D3VO down-weights the photometric residuals at those pixels. Depth and pose networks are trained self-supervised with view-synthesis losses, and their outputs enter the factor graph as additional terms. <span class="src">§13.1.3 · pp. 372–373 · Fig. 13.5</span>

The pattern behind DVSO and D3VO. The classical factors stay. Network predictions are added as further factors, so the optimizer weighs them against the image evidence instead of trusting them outright.
@@ 71
A prediction used as a factor is advice, and a prediction used as the answer is an order. The factor graph can overrule advice when the images disagree with it. That is why "network plus factor graph" ends up more accurate than the network alone and more robust than the geometry alone.
@@ 72
<strong>"Self-supervised means no geometry is used."</strong> The view-synthesis loss is built from projection, the relative pose, and depth. The geometry is in the loss, not in the labels.
@@ 73
<strong>"View synthesis from monocular video gives metric depth."</strong> The loss is unchanged if all depths and translations are scaled together. DVSO's network is metric because its training pairs came from a stereo rig with a known baseline. <span class="src">p. 372</span>
@@ 74
<strong>"A pose network replaces the back-end."</strong> The book reports that direct pose prediction is often less accurate than classical methods and overfits. <span class="src">p. 371</span>
