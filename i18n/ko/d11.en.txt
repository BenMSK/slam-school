The Gauss-Newton model is only trustworthy close to the current estimate. A <strong>trust-region method</strong> limits the step to the region where the model can be believed, checks afterwards whether the cost really went down, and adjusts the region. Levenberg-Marquardt does this with a damping number \(\lambda\). Powell's dogleg does it with an explicit radius.

1. Damping the normal equations
@@ 12
Levenberg's change to Gauss-Newton is one term: add a non-negative constant \(\lambda\) to the diagonal of the normal equations, equation (1.37). <span class="src">§1.4.3 · p. 34</span>
@@ 13
Filling the gap: what problem does (1.37) solve?
@@ 14
It is the normal equation of the linearized cost plus a penalty on the length of the step:
@@ 15
Check: the gradient of this expression is \(2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}-2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}+2\lambda\boldsymbol{\delta}\), and setting it to zero gives (1.37). So \(\lambda\) is the price of moving. A larger \(\lambda\) gives a shorter step.
@@ 16
This is how LM is a trust-region method. Minimizing the model subject to a hard limit \(\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\le\Delta\) leads, whenever the limit is active, to the same equation (1.37), with \(\lambda\) playing the role of the Lagrange multiplier of the constraint. Each \(\lambda\) corresponds to some radius \(\Delta\), and raising \(\lambda\) shrinks it. LM steers the radius indirectly, through \(\lambda\).
@@ 17
It is also the book's Bayesian reading. The penalty \(\lambda\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^2\) is a squared Mahalanobis distance with covariance \(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{I}\). In factor-graph terms, LM adds to every variable a unary Gaussian factor that says "the update is zero, give or take \(1/\sqrt{\lambda}\)". <span class="src">§1.4.3 · p. 34</span>

2. Between Gauss-Newton and steepest descent
@@ 19
With \(\lambda=0\), (1.37) is Gauss-Newton. With \(\lambda\) much larger than the entries of \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\), the matrix on the left is almost \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) and the step is almost \(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\). Since \(\nabla J=-2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\), that is a steepest-descent step with \(\alpha=1/(2\lambda)\). LM blends the two methods, and \(\lambda\) is the dial. <span class="src">§1.4.3 · p. 34</span>

Filling the gap: the blend, direction by direction
@@ 21
Write the eigen-decomposition \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=\sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i\mathbf{v}_i\T\), with orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_i\) and eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\gt 0\). Adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) keeps the eigenvectors and adds \(\lambda\) to every eigenvalue, so
@@ 22
Gauss-Newton is the same sum with denominators \(\lambda_i\). In a steep direction (\(\lambda_i\gg\lambda\)) the damping changes almost nothing. In a flat direction (\(\lambda_i\ll\lambda\)) the denominator is about \(\lambda\) and the component becomes a gradient step. Flat directions are where GN divides by a small number and takes its wildest steps, so the damping acts where it is needed.

<strong>Marquardt's scaling.</strong> Marquardt replaced the identity by the diagonal of \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\), equation (1.38): <span class="src">§1.4.3 · p. 34</span>
@@ 30
Now each variable is damped in proportion to its own diagonal entry, which measures how sharply the cost curves along that variable. A variable with small curvature gets little damping and may take a larger step; a variable with large curvature is held more tightly. In the table's example the step becomes \((1,1)/(1+\lambda)\): both directions shrink by the same factor. A practical benefit is that the result no longer depends on the units chosen for each variable, whereas \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) penalizes one radian and one meter alike.

5. Powell's dogleg
@@ 53
Every rejected LM step costs a new factorization of the damped matrix, and factorization is the most expensive part of an iteration. <strong>Powell's dogleg (PDL)</strong> avoids that. It computes the Gauss-Newton step and the steepest-descent step once, separately, and builds the actual step out of the two. If that step is rejected, the two ingredients are still valid and are combined differently. Each update of the estimate needs only one factorization. <span class="src">§1.4.4 · p. 35</span>
@@ 54
PDL keeps an explicit <strong>trust region</strong>: a ball of radius \(\Delta\) around the current estimate inside which the linearization is trusted. The step runs along the steepest-descent step, bends sharply toward the Gauss-Newton step (the bend is the "dogleg"), and stops where it meets the boundary. Figure 1.6 shows one case. The complete rule has three:

Filling the gap: the three cases, with numbers
@@ 61
The steepest-descent step used here goes to the lowest point of the quadratic model along the gradient direction \(\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\). Minimizing \(\norm{\mathbf{A}(s\,\mathbf{g})-\mathbf{b}}^2\) over the scalar \(s\) gives \(s=\norm{\mathbf{g}}^2/\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}}^2\) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}=s\,\mathbf{g}\). It is never longer than the Gauss-Newton step. Then:
@@ 62
If \(\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{gn}}}\le\Delta\): take \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{gn}}\).
@@ 63
Otherwise, if \(\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}}\ge\Delta\): take \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}\) shortened to length \(\Delta\).
@@ 64
Otherwise: take \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}+\beta\,(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{gn}}-\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}})\) with \(\beta\) between 0 and 1 chosen so that the length is exactly \(\Delta\).
@@ 65
<em>Example.</em> Again \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=\diag(100,1)\) and \(\mathbf{g}=(100,1)\), so \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{gn}}=(1,1)\) with length \(1.41\). For the SD step, \(\norm{\mathbf{g}}^2=10001\) and \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}}^2=\mathbf{g}\T\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}=100\cdot100^2+1=1000001\), so \(s=0.0100\) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}=(1.00,\,0.01)\) with length \(1.00\). With \(\Delta=2\) the step is \((1,1)\). With \(\Delta=0.5\) it is \((0.5,\,0.005)\). With \(\Delta=1.2\) we are in the third case: \((1,\;0.01+0.99\beta)\) must have length \(1.2\), so \(0.01+0.99\beta=\sqrt{1.44-1}=0.663\), \(\beta=0.66\), and the step is \((1,\,0.663)\).
@@ 66
Whether to trust the model more or less is decided by the <strong>gain ratio</strong>, equation (1.39): <span class="src">§1.4.4 · p. 35</span>
@@ 67
The numerator is how much the true cost fell. The denominator is how much the linearized model said it would fall: read \(L(\boldsymbol{\delta})\) as the linearized cost \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}-\mathbf{b}}^2\) at the current estimate. If \(\rho\lt 0.25\) the model promised much more than it delivered, and the trust region is reduced. If \(\rho\gt 0.75\) the model was right or pessimistic, the step is accepted, and the trust region is enlarged, by an amount that depends on the length of the step.
@@ 68
<em>Example.</em> At \(y=2\) in the one-unknown problem, \(L(0)=b^2=122.6\). The full Gauss-Newton step predicts \(L(\delta)=0\), a reduction of \(122.6\). The true cost rose to \(167.7\), a reduction of \(-45.2\). So \(\rho=-0.37\): shrink. For the damped step \(\delta=-1.58\), the model gives \(L(\delta)=(2\cdot(-1.58)+11.07)^2=62.5\), a predicted reduction of \(60.0\). The true reduction was \(122.6-15.7=106.9\), so \(\rho=1.78\): accept and grow.
@@ 69
Gauss-Newton trusts its map of the terrain completely and jumps to where the map says the valley floor is. Levenberg-Marquardt and dogleg trust the map only within a certain distance, take a step no longer than that, and then look around. If the ground is lower, as promised, they trust the map a bit farther next time. If not, they stay put and trust it less.
@@ 70
<strong>"λ is a step size."</strong> It works the other way round: a larger λ gives a shorter, more gradient-like step.
@@ 71
<strong>"LM finds the global minimum."</strong> It only refuses steps that raise the cost. It converges to whichever local minimum its path leads to, as the widget shows after New noise.
@@ 72
<strong>"LM is a slower Gauss-Newton."</strong> Near the solution every step is accepted, λ falls toward zero, and LM takes Gauss-Newton steps. The extra cost arises only when steps are rejected.
