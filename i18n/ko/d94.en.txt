Notice that the stored increments (Δθ = 19.90°, Δv = (2.036, 10.787) m/s, Δp = (1.008, 5.402) m) never change, and that the arrow chain always ends exactly where the integrated path ends: the two predictions differ by about 10⁻¹⁵ m. @@ 29
Naive integration is like giving directions from a fixed street address: "from the station, go north 200 m, then east 50 m". If the starting point turns out to be wrong, every instruction must be rewritten. Preintegration writes the directions relative to the walker: "200 m ahead, then 50 m to your right". They stay valid wherever the walk starts and whichever way the walker faced. Gravity's pull is the one thing that depends on the outside world, so it is kept out of the directions and added back afterwards.

5. How noisy are the increments?
@@ 41
The factor needs the covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}\) of the 9-vector \(\boldsymbol{\eta}^\Delta_{ij} = [\delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{ij}\T, \delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\T, \delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}\T]\T\) (11.24). To first order, the rotation noise is a weighted sum of the raw gyroscope noises: <span class="src">§11.2.2.2 · p. 314</span>
@@ 42
\(\delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}\), shown in (11.21), and \(\delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}\) are in turn sums of accelerometer noises and of the rotation noise (11.29). A linear combination of independent zero-mean Gaussians is a zero-mean Gaussian, and its covariance follows by the rule \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\eta}) = \mathbf{C}\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\mathbf{C}\T\). The book notes that the covariance can also be built up incrementally, one sample at a time. <span class="src">§11.2.2.2 · p. 314</span>
@@ 43
Filling the gap: the incremental form, in the plane
@@ 44
In the widget's planar world a rotation is one angle, \(\mathbf{J}_r = 1\), and rotations commute, so (11.28) is simply \(\delta\theta_{ij} = \sum_k\eta^{gd}_k\Delta t\). The cross-product term \(-\mathbf{f}^\wedge\delta\boldsymbol{\phi}\) becomes \(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{f}\,\delta\theta\), with \(\mathbf{J}\) the 90° rotation matrix and \(\mathbf{f}_k = \tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^a_i\). Each new sample updates the noise state as
@@ 45
This has the form \(\boldsymbol{\eta}^\Delta_{i,k+1} = \mathbf{A}_k\boldsymbol{\eta}^\Delta_{ik} + \mathbf{B}_k\boldsymbol{\eta}^d_k\), so the covariance obeys \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i,k+1} = \mathbf{A}_k\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ik}\mathbf{A}_k\T + \mathbf{B}_k\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^d_k)\mathbf{B}_k\T\), starting from \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ii} = \mathbf{0}\). It is the covariance propagation of <a href="#d22">Week 4 · Day 4</a>, run alongside the integration. The widget does exactly this. A check by hand for the angle: \(N\) samples each contribute variance \((\sigma_g^2/\Delta t)\Delta t^2\), so \(\operatorname{var}(\delta\theta_{ij}) = \sigma_g^2\Delta t_{ij}\). With the widget's \(\sigma_g = 0.002\) rad/s/√Hz and \(\Delta t_{ij} = 1\) s the standard deviation is 0.002 rad = 0.115°, the value it displays.
@@ 46
Two things to remember about \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}\). It grows with the time between keyframes, so widely spaced keyframes give weaker IMU factors. And it is not diagonal: an error in the integrated rotation mis-rotates every later accelerometer sample, so the velocity and position noises are correlated with the rotation noise.
@@ 47
<strong>"\(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}\) is how far the robot moved."</strong> It is the double integral of the specific force in the frame of keyframe \(i\). A robot at rest for 1 s has \(\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij} = 4.9\) m.
@@ 48
<strong>"Preintegration is a cheaper, approximate integration."</strong> The increments are an exact regrouping of (11.13), as the 10⁻¹⁵ m in the widget shows. Approximations enter only in the first-order treatment of noise, and tomorrow in the bias correction.
@@ 49
<strong>"The increments depend on nothing but the IMU data."</strong> They still depend on the bias estimate used while integrating.
