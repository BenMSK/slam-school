2. Why the chapter bets on hybrids
@@ 32
Learning has two attractions. Networks can do jobs nobody managed to design by hand, such as labeling a scene or guessing depth from one picture. And a network compresses an iterative computation into one feed-forward pass that runs fast on current processors. <span class="src">§18.3 · pp. 527–528</span>
@@ 33
Will one network do all of SLAM end to end? Work in that direction keeps improving, from DeepVO to VGGT. The chapter still expects hybrids to win for the foreseeable future, for two connected reasons: the uncertainty of a network's output is poorly understood, and a long-lived system must be able to <em>update</em> its model. Its example: a network builds a scene from 100 images, and then image 101 arrives. Running everything again on 101 images cannot be the answer. Accept that, and you need long-term representation and fusion, which is what probabilistic state estimation provides. <span class="src">§18.3 · p. 528</span>

3. Why processors stopped getting faster the easy way
@@ 41
For decades SLAM researchers could count on single-core speed doubling every one to two years. That ended, although <strong>Moore's law</strong> (the transistor count per chip keeps doubling) has continued. The rule that failed is <strong>Dennard scaling</strong>: as transistors shrink, their power density stays constant. At nanometre sizes transistors leak current and heat up, and the resulting <strong>power wall</strong> holds clock speeds near 4 GHz. <span class="src">§18.4.1 · pp. 528–529</span>
@@ 42
Filling the gap: where Dennard scaling comes from, and how it breaks
@@ 43
The power a transistor spends switching is \(P = C V^2 f\): its capacitance \(C\), the supply voltage \(V\) squared, and the switching frequency \(f\). Shrink every length by a factor \(k\) (so \(k = 2\) halves all dimensions) and, as Dennard proposed, shrink the voltage by \(k\) as well.
@@ 44
Capacitance is area over thickness, \((1/k^2)/(1/k)\), so \(C \to C/k\).
@@ 45
The transistor switches \(k\) times faster, so the clock can go to \(kf\).
@@ 46
Power per transistor becomes \(\dfrac{C}{k}\cdot\dfrac{V^2}{k^2}\cdot kf = \dfrac{CV^2f}{k^2}\).
@@ 47
Each transistor occupies \(1/k^2\) of the area, so \(k^2\) times as many fit per square millimetre.
@@ 48
The catch is the voltage. Below a certain voltage a transistor no longer turns off properly and leaks, which is the leakage the book mentions, so \(V\) stopped shrinking. Hold \(V\) fixed and repeat the sum: power per transistor is \((C/k)\,V^2\,(kf) = CV^2f\), unchanged, and power density grows as \(k^2\). Freeze the clock as well and it still grows as \(k\).
@@ 49
Try it: step through transistor generations under the three regimes.
@@ 50
Notice that under Dennard scaling six generations give 64 times the transistors and 8 times the clock at the same power per square millimetre. Switch the regime to "voltage stuck, clock still rising": the same six generations multiply the heat per square millimetre by 64. Freezing the clock reduces that to 8, which is still not flat.

4. What efficient processing looks like
@@ 52
If the clock cannot rise, performance must come from doing many things at once. The chapter's recipe: divide the computation among a large number of simple, low-clock-rate cores and <strong>minimize the movement of data</strong> between them. <span class="src">§18.4.1 · p. 529</span> A CPU does the reverse. It pulls small pieces of data one at a time from a separate main memory and pushes results back, with a cache as the only relief. That makes a CPU easy to program, but the piece-by-piece traffic has a large power cost. <span class="src">§18.4.1 · pp. 529–530</span>

5. Sensors, and the cloud
@@ 70
For this chapter a camera is any device that captures an array of light measurements, and each design trades information quality against size and power. <span class="src">§18.4.2 · p. 531</span> Two sensors point at the future:
@@ 71
The <strong>event camera</strong> (<a href="#d85">Week 15 · Day 1</a>) sends only intensity changes, so it carries the content of video at a much lower bit rate.
@@ 72
The <strong>SCAMP5</strong> vision chip puts a small analog processor at every pixel of a 256 × 256 array and runs at 1.2 W. Pixels can add, subtract, divide, square, and pass values to neighbours, so a useful amount of vision is computed on the sensor itself. <span class="src">§18.4.2 · p. 532</span>
@@ 73
The purpose of processing near the sensor is to cut the bit rate on the link to the main processor. To see what is at stake, a plain 640 × 480 camera at 8 bits and 30 frames per second produces \(640 \times 480 \times 8 \times 30 \approx 73.7\) million bits every second, most of them repeating what the previous frame already said.
@@ 74
The cloud changes the sums less than you might hope. From the device's side, cloud compute and memory are close to infinite and free. Communication is neither: sending video costs power, and the round trip adds a delay of a significant fraction of a second. Some places (space, undersea, underground) have no link at all. <span class="src">§18.4.2 · p. 532</span> A robot moving at 1 m/s travels 30 cm during a 0.3 s delay, so the tight loop has to stay on board.
@@ 75
<strong>"Moore's law ended, so chips stopped improving."</strong> Transistor density kept growing. What ended is Dennard scaling, the ability to switch those transistors faster without more heat per area.

@@ 77
