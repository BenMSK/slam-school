Week 20 review: maps built by rendering
@@ 3
After today you can render a pixel by hand from densities or from Gaussians, project a Gaussian covariance into the image, and weigh NeRF-style and splat maps against the needs of SLAM.
@@ 4
Chapter 14 treats the map as whatever set of parameters an image can be rendered from by a differentiable procedure. Render, compare with the camera's image, and push the gradient of the difference back into the parameters, which may include the camera pose. NeRF stores density and colour as a field and renders by marching along each pixel's ray: a pixel is a sum of sample colours weighted by transmittance times opacity. How the field is stored (one MLP, a grid, points) decides its speed, its size, whether it fills holes, whether it can grow, and whether it forgets. 3D Gaussian splatting keeps the same blending sum but gets its opacities from explicit Gaussian primitives projected onto the image, which renders far faster and gives SLAM primitives it can add and move. Both are slow to optimize and, as trackers, still less accurate than classical sparse methods.

Q7. At one pixel, Gaussians A and B both have α = 0.5, with A in front. Then B is moved in front of A, with both α unchanged. What is A's weight now?
@@ 53
Behind B, A receives only the transmittance 1 − 0.5 = 0.5, so its weight is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25. In front it was 0.5. Depth order matters.

A ray is sampled at four points with equal spacing \(\delta = 0.5\). The densities are \(\sigma = (0,\ 1,\ 0,\ 4)\), the sample positions are \(t = (0.5,\ 1.0,\ 1.5,\ 2.0)\), and the grey levels at samples 2 and 4 are \(1.0\) and \(0.2\). Compute every \(\alpha_i\), \(T_i\), and \(w_i\), the pixel value over a black background, the light that passes all four samples, and the expected depth.
@@ 61
\(w_2 = 0.3935\), \(w_4 = 0.6065 \times 0.8647 = 0.5244\), the others are 0. Check: \(0.3935 + 0.5244 + 0.0821 = 1\).
@@ 62
Pixel: \(0.3935 \times 1.0 + 0.5244 \times 0.2 = 0.498\). Expected depth: \(0.3935 \times 1.0 + 0.5244 \times 2.0 = 1.44\).
@@ 63
(a) Show that \(w_i = T_i - T_{i+1}\). (b) Deduce that \(\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i = 1 - T_{N+1}\). (c) A ray crosses fog of constant density \(\sigma = 0.4\) over a length of 5, cut into \(N\) equal segments. Show that the total weight does not depend on \(N\), and compute it.
@@ 64
(a) By definition \(T_{i+1} = T_i(1 - \alpha_i)\), so \(T_i - T_{i+1} = T_i\alpha_i = w_i\).
@@ 65
(b) The sum telescopes: \((T_1 - T_2) + (T_2 - T_3) + \dots + (T_N - T_{N+1}) = T_1 - T_{N+1} = 1 - T_{N+1}\).
@@ 66
(c) \(T_{N+1} = \exp(-\sum_j \sigma\delta_j) = \exp(-0.4 \times 5) = e^{-2} = 0.135\), whatever the number of segments, because the lengths always add up to 5. The total weight is \(1 - 0.135 = 0.865\). This is the reason opacity is defined as \(1 - e^{-\sigma\delta}\) and not as \(\sigma\delta\): the result must not depend on how finely a uniform region is cut.
@@ 67
A 2D world with the camera at the origin looking along \(z\) and the image line at \(z = 1\). Gaussian A is at \((x, z) = (0.5, 2)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_A = \diag(0.09, 0.01)\), opacity 0.9, grey level 1.0. Gaussian B is at \((0, 4)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_B = \diag(0.64, 0.04)\), opacity 0.8, grey level 0. The background has grey level 0.5. Find each footprint's centre and standard deviation, then the value of the pixel at \(u = 0.25\).
@@ 68
A: \(\mu'_A = 0.5/2 = 0.25\). \(\mathbf{J} = [\,1/z \;\; -x/z^2\,] = [\,0.5 \;\; -0.125\,]\). \(\sigma'^2_A = 0.25 \times 0.09 + 0.0156 \times 0.01 = 0.0227\), so \(\sigma'_A = 0.151\).
@@ 69
B: \(\mu'_B = 0\). \(\mathbf{J} = [\,0.25 \;\; 0\,]\). \(\sigma'^2_B = 0.0625 \times 0.64 = 0.04\), so \(\sigma'_B = 0.2\).
@@ 70
At \(u = 0.25\): the pixel is at A's centre, so \(\alpha_A = 0.9\). For B, \(G = \exp(-0.25^2 / (2 \times 0.04)) = e^{-0.781} = 0.458\), so \(\alpha_B = 0.8 \times 0.458 = 0.366\).
@@ 71
A is nearer, so it is blended first. Weights: A \(0.9\); B \(0.1 \times 0.366 = 0.037\); background \(0.1 \times 0.634 = 0.063\). Pixel: \(0.9 \times 1.0 + 0.037 \times 0 + 0.063 \times 0.5 = 0.932\).
@@ 72
Tracking as least squares. In the same 2D world the camera's sideways position \(c\) is unknown. The centre of a Gaussian at \((1, 2)\) is observed at \(u_1 = 0.35\), and the centre of a Gaussian at \((-1, 4)\) at \(u_2 = -0.33\). (a) Write the predicted centres as functions of \(c\) and the residuals at \(c = 0\). (b) Take one Gauss-Newton step from \(c = 0\) (<a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a>). (c) Which Gaussian influences the answer more, and why?
@@ 73
(a) \(\mu'_1(c) = (1 - c)/2\) and \(\mu'_2(c) = (-1 - c)/4\). At \(c = 0\) they are \(0.5\) and \(-0.25\), so the residuals (predicted minus observed) are \(r_1 = 0.15\) and \(r_2 = 0.08\).
@@ 74
(b) The Jacobians are \(J_1 = -1/2\) and \(J_2 = -1/4\). Gauss-Newton solves \((J_1^2 + J_2^2)\,\Delta = -(J_1 r_1 + J_2 r_2)\): \(0.3125\,\Delta = 0.075 + 0.02 = 0.095\), so \(\Delta = 0.304\) and \(c = 0.304\). The predictions are linear in \(c\), so this single step is the exact least-squares answer. The residuals fall to \(-0.002\) and \(0.004\).
@@ 75
(c) Gaussian 1. Alone it would give \(c = 0.30\), and Gaussian 2 alone \(c = 0.32\); the answer \(0.304\) sits close to the first. Its Jacobian is twice as large because it is half as far away, so it carries four times the weight in \(J_1^2 + J_2^2\). Nearby structure moves more in the image when the camera translates, and therefore constrains the translation more.
