1. The MAP estimate
@@ 12
The posterior \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\) is a whole density. Most of the time we want one answer: the state at its peak. That is the <strong>maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate</strong>, equation (1.6): <span class="src">§1.2.1 · p. 26</span>
@@ 13
\(\argmax_{\mathbf{x}}\) means "the value of \(\mathbf{x}\) at which the expression is largest", not the largest value itself. The denominator \(p(\mathbf{z})\) does not contain \(\mathbf{x}\). Dividing a function by a positive constant rescales its height and does not move its peak, so it can be dropped.
@@ 14
Yesterday's graph is given a formal definition here. A factor graph is a <strong>bipartite graph</strong> \(F = (\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})\): there are two kinds of nodes, factors \(\phi_i \in \mathcal{U}\) and variables \(\mathbf{x}_j \in \mathcal{V}\), and every edge in \(\mathcal{E}\) joins a factor to a variable, never two nodes of the same kind. Writing \(\mathbf{x}_i\) for the variables joined to factor \(\phi_i\), the graph defines the function <span class="src">§1.2.1 · p. 26</span>

MAP inference is then \(\mathbf{x}^{\text{MAP}} = \argmax_{\mathbf{x}} \prod_i \phi_i(\mathbf{x}_i)\), equation (1.8). <span class="src">§1.2.1 · p. 27</span> Be careful with the index: \(\mathbf{x}_i\) here is "the variables of factor \(i\)", a subset of the state, not the \(i\)-th variable.

@@ 17
To compute anything we must say what the factors are. The usual choice is the <strong>multivariate Gaussian density</strong> with mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\R^n\) and \(n\times n\) covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\): <span class="src">§1.2.2 · p. 27</span>

The quantity in (1.10) is the <strong>squared Mahalanobis distance</strong>. The fraction in front of the exponential makes the density integrate to one. It does not depend on \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\).

Filling the gap: what the Mahalanobis distance measures
@@ 20
In one dimension, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^2\) and (1.10) is \((\theta-\mu)^2/\sigma^2\): the squared distance from the mean, counted in standard deviations. With a diagonal covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\diag(\sigma_1^2,\dots,\sigma_n^2)\) the inverse is \(\diag(1/\sigma_1^2,\dots,1/\sigma_n^2)\), so
@@ 21
Each component is divided by its own standard deviation before squaring. Example: \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\diag(0.09,\,0.01)\), so \(\sigma_1=0.3\) and \(\sigma_2=0.1\). The offset \(\mathbf{e}_A=(0.24,\,0)\) has \(\norm{\mathbf{e}_A}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = 0.0576/0.09 = 0.64\), a distance of \(0.8\) standard deviations. The offset \(\mathbf{e}_B=(0,\,0.24)\) has the same ordinary length but \(\norm{\mathbf{e}_B}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = 0.0576/0.01 = 5.76\), a distance of \(2.4\). The density at \(B\) is lower than at \(A\) by the factor \(\exp(-\tfrac12(5.76-0.64)) \approx 0.08\).
@@ 22
When \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is not diagonal the same thing happens along the axes of its ellipse instead of the coordinate axes. Points with equal Mahalanobis distance from the mean lie on an ellipse and have equal density.

@@ 33
<em>Worked example.</em> Pose position \((1,1)\), landmark \((4,5)\). The prediction is \(\operatorname{atan2}(4,3) = 0.927\) rad (53.1°). Suppose the sensor reported \(z=0.950\) rad with \(\sigma=0.05\) rad. The error is \(0.950-0.927=0.023\) rad, which is \(0.023/0.05 = 0.45\) standard deviations, and the squared Mahalanobis distance is \(0.45^2 \approx 0.21\).
@@ 34
<strong>Motion.</strong> The prior on the trajectory is \(p(\mathbf{p}_1)\) times conditional densities \(p(\mathbf{p}_{t+1}\mid\mathbf{p}_t)\). With a <strong>motion model</strong> \(\mathbf{g}\) that predicts the next pose from the current pose and a known control input \(\mathbf{u}_t\), the Gaussian version is equation (1.15), whose exponent is \(-\tfrac12\norm{\mathbf{p}_{t+1}-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{p}_t,\mathbf{u}_t)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{Q}}}\). If instead the motion is <em>measured</em> by an odometry reading \(\mathbf{o}_t\), and odometry is taken to measure the difference of poses, the exponent is \(-\tfrac12\norm{\mathbf{o}_t-(\mathbf{p}_{t+1}-\mathbf{p}_t)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{S}}}\), equation (1.16). <span class="src">§1.2.2 · p. 28</span>
@@ 35
<em>Worked example.</em> \(\mathbf{p}_t=(1.0,\,2.0,\,0.10)\), \(\mathbf{p}_{t+1}=(1.9,\,2.1,\,0.15)\), odometry \(\mathbf{o}_t=(1.0,\,0.0,\,0.05)\), and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{S}}=\diag(0.1^2,\,0.1^2,\,0.05^2)\). The error is \(\mathbf{o}_t-(\mathbf{p}_{t+1}-\mathbf{p}_t) = (0.1,\,-0.1,\,0)\), and its squared Mahalanobis distance is \(1+1+0=2\).

This is a <strong>nonlinear least-squares problem</strong>: "least squares" because the cost is a sum of squares, "nonlinear" because the \(\mathbf{h}_i\) are not linear functions of the state. Minimizing it combines all factors into one answer, which the book calls <strong>sensor fusion</strong>. <span class="src">§1.2.3 · p. 29</span>
@@ 55
<em>Worked example of fusion.</em> Two sensors measure the same scalar \(x\) directly: \(z_1=10\) with \(\sigma_1=1\), and \(z_2=12\) with \(\sigma_2=2\). The cost is \((10-x)^2/1+(12-x)^2/4\). Setting the derivative to zero, \(-2(10-x)-\tfrac12(12-x)=0\), gives \(2.5\,x=26\) and \(x=10.4\). The answer is not the midpoint 11. The measurement with the smaller \(\sigma\) is weighted \(1/\sigma^2\), four times as heavily.
@@ 56
Every clue complains when the estimate disagrees with it, and a precise clue complains louder than a vague one. The MAP estimate is the state at which the total of the squared complaints is smallest.

5. One factor is rarely enough
@@ 58
The book makes a point that is easy to skip: most factors are "under-specified". A measurement usually has fewer numbers than the unknowns it touches, so a factor on its own gives the same value to an infinite set of states. A 2D pixel measurement is consistent with a whole ray of 3D points. <span class="src">§1.2.3 · p. 29</span> A bearing is one number about a landmark that has two coordinates. Only the product of several factors has a single peak.
@@ 59
Try it: the widget shows the product of the factors that touch one landmark, for every landmark position on a grid. It starts with one bearing factor. Switch on the bearing from p₂, then the prior, and raise the bearing noise.
@@ 60
Notice that one bearing factor is a wedge, equally high all along the line of sight, so the MAP estimate is not unique. Two bearing factors multiply to a single blob where the lines of sight cross, with its peak at (2.03, 1.71). That is 0.11 away from the true landmark, because each measured bearing was slightly off. With the prior on and the bearing noise at 15°, the peak moves toward the prior mean, to (2.21, 1.41): a factor's pull depends on its σ.
@@ 61
The cost (1.18) is not convex, which means it can have several local minima. The methods of this chapter find the global minimum if they start from a decent initial guess, and may stop in a wrong local minimum if the guess is poor. Solvers that can certify a global optimum are the subject of Chapter 6. <span class="src">§1.2.3 · p. 29</span> You will see a local minimum happen on Day 5.
@@ 62
<strong>"MAP gives the true state."</strong> It gives the most probable state under the model. With noisy measurements it is near the truth, not on it, as in the widget.
@@ 63
<strong>"Least squares means all errors count equally."</strong> Each error is divided by its standard deviation first. An error of 0.1 m on a sensor with σ = 0.01 m costs 100, and on a sensor with σ = 1 m it costs 0.01.
@@ 64
<strong>"The ½ and the normalization constants matter for the answer."</strong> They change the value of the cost, not the location of its minimum. They do matter if you need the actual probability.

Q2. Two sensors measure the same scalar x directly: z1 = 4 with σ1 = 1, and z2 = 10 with σ2 = 2. What is the MAP estimate of x (no prior)?
@@ 68
Minimize (4 − x)² + (10 − x)²/4. The derivative gives 2(x − 4) + (x − 10)/2 = 0, so 2.5x = 13 and x = 5.2. The more precise sensor has weight 1/σ² = 1 against 0.25.
