@@ 9
A foot that stands on the ground without slipping is a fixed point, and the joint encoders tell the robot where that point is relative to its body. So while the foot stays put, every change in the shape of the leg is a motion of the body. <strong>Leg odometry</strong> is that observation turned into a measurement: decide which feet are planted, then read the body's motion off their joints.
@@ 10
1. From wheels to legs
@@ 11
Wheel odometry counts how far the wheels turn. A wheel of radius 0.1 m whose encoder reports 3 full turns has rolled \(2\pi \times 0.1 \times 3 = 1.885\) m, as long as it did not skid. The name "leg odometry" was chosen by analogy: the robot infers its motion from the motion of the parts that touch the ground. <span class="src">§12.1 · p. 334</span>
@@ 12
A leg, unlike a wheel, makes and breaks contact. Each stride has a <strong>swing phase</strong> (the book also calls it the aerial phase), in which the foot is lifted and carried forward, and a <strong>stance phase</strong>, in which the foot stays on the ground without slipping. Only stance legs push the robot, so only stance legs say anything about how the body moved. That gives the two sub-problems the rest of the chapter solves: <span class="src">§12.1 · p. 334</span>

@@ 26
Stand on one foot and bend your knee. Your foot did not move, so your body did. If you know your own joint angles, you know how far. A legged robot does this hundreds of times a second, switching to the other foot each time it takes a step.

3. Four kinds of frame
@@ 49
The chapter uses these reference frames (Figure 12.1): <span class="src">§12.1.2 · p. 335</span>
@@ 50
The <strong>inertial (world) frame</strong> \(\mathcal{F}^w\), fixed to the ground.
@@ 51
The <strong>base frame</strong> \(\mathcal{F}^b\), fixed to the robot's main body, which is called the <strong>floating base</strong> because nothing bolts it to the world. The IMU is assumed to sit exactly at \(\mathcal{F}^b\).
@@ 52
A <strong>foot frame</strong> \(\mathcal{F}^{f}\) at the end of each leg.
@@ 53
A <strong>contact frame</strong> \(\mathcal{F}^k\), created each time a foot lands. It is fixed to the ground, not to the robot. For a humanoid's flat foot it coincides with the foot frame at touchdown. For a quadruped's point foot only its position is defined by the foot.

Filling the gap: how to read \(\mathbf{T}^{a}_{b}\)
@@ 64
The chapter writes a pose as a \(4 \times 4\) matrix with two indices and never stops to explain them. \(\mathbf{T}^{w}_{b}\) is the pose of frame \(b\) expressed in frame \(w\). It holds a rotation \(\mathbf{R}^w_b\) and a translation \(\mathbf{t}^w_b\) (the origin of \(b\), in \(w\) coordinates), and it converts the coordinates of a point from \(b\) to \(w\):
@@ 65
Two rules do all the work this week.
@@ 66
<strong>Chain:</strong> \(\mathbf{T}^w_f = \mathbf{T}^w_b\,\mathbf{T}^b_f\). The lower index of the first factor must match the upper index of the second, and they cancel.
@@ 67
<strong>Invert:</strong> \((\mathbf{T}^w_b)^{-1} = \mathbf{T}^b_w = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R}\T & -\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{0} & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), which swaps the indices.

4. The state
@@ 71
A legged robot's full state is the pose and velocity of its base plus the positions and velocities of all its joints. The chapter assumes the joints are measured directly by their own sensors (<a href="#d104">Day 2</a>), so only the base remains to be estimated: <span class="src">§12.1.3 · pp. 335–336</span>

Filling the gap: a velocity in base coordinates
@@ 82
\(\mathbf{v}^b_b\) is the velocity of the base relative to the world, with its three numbers written along the base's own axes. To get the world-frame velocity, rotate it: \(\mathbf{v}^w = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{v}\). Example: a robot walks straight ahead at 0.5 m/s. Then \(\mathbf{v} = (0.5, 0, 0)\) whichever way it faces. @@ 83
Base coordinates are the natural choice here: the IMU and the leg joints are both attached to the base, so what they measure comes out in base coordinates. You saw the same convention for the gyroscope in <a href="#d91">Week 16 · Day 1</a>.
@@ 84
<strong>"The contact frame is a frame on the robot."</strong> It is attached to the ground. It is created at touchdown, and it is abandoned when the foot lifts.
@@ 85
<strong>"The velocity in (12.1) is the world-frame velocity."</strong> Both \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) are in base coordinates. Only \(\mathbf{t}\) and \(\mathbf{R}\) refer to the world.
@@ 86
<strong>"A foot on the ground is a foot in stance."</strong> Stance means the contact point is not moving. A foot that touches and slides tells you nothing reliable (<a href="#d106">Day 4</a>).

Q1. A wheel of radius 0.15 m turns 2 full revolutions without skidding. How far did the vehicle roll?
@@ 88
Distance = circumference × turns = 2π × 0.15 × 2 = 1.88 m. Leg odometry replaces "the wheel did not skid" with "the stance foot did not slip".

With R = I, the world position is p + t = (0.2 + 1.0, −0.5 + 0.5) = (1.2, 0.0): the foot is on the ground.
