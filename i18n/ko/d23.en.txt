The tangent space lets you do vector-style arithmetic on poses, with one complication that vectors do not have: order matters. Three tools deal with it. The operator \(\ominus\) measures a difference. The <strong>adjoint</strong> moves a perturbation across a transformation, exactly. The <strong>Jacobian</strong> says how two tangent vectors combine, approximately.

2. Inverting a perturbed pose
@@ 22
Sometimes the quantity you need is the inverse of the pose you perturbed. <span class="src">§2.1.5.2 · p. 61, eq. (2.26)</span>
@@ 23
Two facts are used. The inverse of a product reverses the order. And \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{-1} = \Exp(-\boldsymbol{\xi})\): undoing a constant-velocity motion means running it backward. (Formally, \(\boldsymbol{\xi}^\wedge\) commutes with \(-\boldsymbol{\xi}^\wedge\), so here the exponents may be added, giving \(\exp(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{I}\).) The message: a <em>right</em> perturbation of \(\mathbf{T}\) by \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) is a <em>left</em> perturbation of \(\mathbf{T}^{-1}\) by \(-\boldsymbol{\xi}\). This is the subtlety behind footnote 1 on p. 57: perturbing \(\mathbf{T}^c_w\) on the right is the same as perturbing \(\mathbf{T}^w_c\) on the left.

If the axes are parallel all cross products vanish and the angles add. Otherwise there are corrections. In estimation one of the two is usually a small perturbation. Keeping every term that is linear in the small one, and dropping the rest, gives the book's formula: <span class="src">§2.1.5.4 · p. 62, eqs. (2.34)–(2.35)</span>
@@ 43
\(\mathbf{J}\) is the <strong>(left) Jacobian</strong> of \(\SO(3)\). You can see the first terms of the BCH series in it: \(\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_2)^{-1} = \mathbf{I} - \tfrac12\boldsymbol{\theta}_2^\wedge + \tfrac1{12}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_2^\wedge)^2 - \dots\), and applying that to \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1\) reproduces \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 + \tfrac12\boldsymbol{\theta}_1\times\boldsymbol{\theta}_2 + \tfrac1{12}\boldsymbol{\theta}_2\times(\boldsymbol{\theta}_2\times\boldsymbol{\theta}_1)\).

@@ 46
The odd powers of the series collect into \(\tfrac{\phi}{2!} - \tfrac{\phi^3}{4!} + \dots = \tfrac{1-\cos\phi}{\phi}\) and the even ones into \(\tfrac{\phi^2}{3!} - \tfrac{\phi^4}{5!} + \dots = 1 - \tfrac{\sin\phi}{\phi}\). At \(\phi = 0\) both coefficients vanish and \(\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{I}\): for small rotations, vectors simply add.
@@ 47
<strong>Right Jacobian.</strong> If the <em>second</em> rotation is the small one, the mirror-image formula holds: \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1)\Exp(\boldsymbol{\theta}_2)\approx\Exp\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1 + \mathbf{J}_r(\boldsymbol{\theta}_1)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta}_2\big)\) with \(\mathbf{J}_r(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{J}(-\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\T\).
@@ 48
<strong>Kinematics.</strong> The book introduces \(\mathbf{J}\) through \(\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \mathbf{J}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\), equation (2.33). In words: the rate of change of the rotation vector is not the angular velocity, unless the axis stays fixed. \(\mathbf{J}\) converts between the two. In the plane \(\mathbf{J} = 1\) and \(\dot\theta = \omega\).
