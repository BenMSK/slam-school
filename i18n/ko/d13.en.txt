@@ 11
Each measurement in SLAM involves only a few variables, so almost every entry of the matrices we factor is zero. The factor graph is a drawing of those zeros: a factor is a block-row of \(\mathbf{A}\), a variable is a block-column, and two variables share a nonzero block of \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) exactly when they share a factor. Solvers that skip the zeros handle millions of unknowns, and how many zeros survive the factorization depends on the order of the variables.

@@ 14
<strong>Pose to pose.</strong> About 100 odometry factors in a chain, where 100² pairs would be possible.
@@ 15
<strong>Pose to landmark.</strong> About 400, where 100 × 20 = 2000 would be possible: a landmark is seen from nearby poses only.
@@ 16
<strong>Landmark to landmark.</strong> None. Nothing measures one landmark relative to another.
@@ 17
The reason is physical: a sensor has a limited range and a robot moves a short distance per step, so one measurement can only relate things that are close in space or time. The book calls this structure typical of most SLAM problems.
@@ 18
Ignoring it gets expensive fast. Dense Cholesky costs about \(n^3/3\) operations (Week 2). For the \(n = 333\) unknowns of Figure 1.9 that is 12 million operations and no trouble. For \(n = 10^5\) it is \(3.3 \times 10^{14}\), about four days at a billion operations per second, and a dense \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) of \(10^{10}\) numbers would fill 80 GB. Real problems have "thousands or even millions of unknowns", <span class="src">§1.5 · p. 36</span> and they are solvable only because the zeros are never stored or touched.

Filling the gap: why the other blocks are exactly zero
@@ 23
A factor comes from a measurement model \(h_i\) that reads only its own variables. Factor \(\phi_8\) of the toy example predicts a measurement from \(\mathbf{p}_2\) and \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\) and from nothing else. Its row of the Jacobian holds the derivatives of that prediction with respect to all five variables, and the derivative with respect to a variable that does not appear in \(h_8\) is zero. No approximation is involved.

Filling the gap: which blocks of \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) are nonzero
@@ 41
Write \(\mathbf{A}_{ij}\) for the block of factor \(i\) in the column of variable \(j\). Block \((j,k)\) of the product is a sum over the rows:
@@ 42
A term survives only if factor \(i\) touches both variable \(j\) and variable \(k\). So \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{jk} \neq \mathbf{0}\) needs at least one factor that contains both. In the toy example \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{14} = \mathbf{A}_{81}\T\mathbf{A}_{84}\) comes from \(\phi_8\) alone (in numbers, \(1 \cdot (-1) = -1\)), and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{15} = \mathbf{0}\) because no factor joins \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\).
@@ 43
The diagonal block \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{jj} = \sum_i \mathbf{A}_{ij}\T\mathbf{A}_{ij}\) collects every factor on variable \(j\): the 4 in the \(p_1\) position is \(\phi_1, \phi_2, \phi_6, \phi_7\), one unit each. A unary factor such as a prior adds to the diagonal and to nothing else.
@@ 44
This gives a second graph. Draw one node per variable and join two variables if they ever appear in the same factor. Call it \(G\). At the block level, the nonzero pattern of \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) is the adjacency matrix of \(G\). A factor on three or more variables joins all of them pairwise (a <strong>clique</strong>). <span class="src">§1.5.2 · p. 37</span> The book notes that \(G\) is the graph of a Markov random field and leaves it there; we will use \(G\) on Days 2 and 3 to predict where a factorization creates new nonzeros.

4. Sparse factorization, fill-in, and the ordering
@@ 52
Both routes from Week 2 have sparse versions: Cholesky on \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) and QR on \(\mathbf{A}\). They skip the zeros and so need far fewer operations than the dense counts. The book names the libraries most packages call (CHOLMOD and SuiteSparseQR) and reports that on sparse problems Cholesky or LDU beats QR by more than a constant factor. <span class="src">§1.5.3 · p. 38</span>
@@ 53
There is a catch. The triangular factor \(\mathbf{R}\), with \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\), can have nonzeros where \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) has zeros. These extra entries are called <strong>fill-in</strong>, and their number depends on the <strong>variable ordering</strong>, the order of the columns. In Figure 1.9 the natural order (poses, then landmarks) gives an \(\mathbf{R}\) with 9399 nonzeros. Reordering the columns with the <strong>COLAMD</strong> heuristic (column approximate minimum degree) gives 4168. Back-substitution returns the same estimate from both. <span class="src">§1.5.3 · p. 38</span>
@@ 54
Try it: the widget builds a small problem, draws \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\), and computes the pattern of \(\mathbf{R}\) for the ordering you pick. Switch the ordering from "poses, then landmarks" to "minimum degree", then try the others.
@@ 55
Notice that Λ has 91 nonzeros out of 361. With poses first, R has 109 nonzeros, 54 of them fill-in. Minimum degree brings that down to 70 with 15 fill-ins, and a random order lands in between. Nothing about the problem changed, only the order of the columns.

@@ 59
No fill-in at all. Removing the hub first forces its three neighbours to account for each other; removing it last never does. Day 3 turns this into a rule you can apply to a graph.
@@ 60
<strong>Why the answer cannot change.</strong> Reordering is a permutation matrix \(\mathbf{P}\): \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P})(\mathbf{P}\T\mathbf{x})\), because \(\mathbf{P}\mathbf{P}\T = \mathbf{I}\). The cost \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}}^2\) is the same function, written with its unknowns in a different order, so its minimizer is the same vector with its entries shuffled.
@@ 61
Finding the ordering with the least fill-in is NP-hard, so solvers use heuristics such as COLAMD. <span class="src">§1.5.3 · p. 38</span> The book also mentions iterative solvers (preconditioned conjugate gradient, and power iterations in visual SLAM) as alternatives, and says sparse factorization remains the method of choice for most SLAM problems.
@@ 62
<strong>"Sparse means there are few measurements."</strong> It means each measurement involves few variables. Figure 1.9 has 1126 measurement rows and is still very sparse.
@@ 63
<strong>"A better ordering gives a better estimate."</strong> Every ordering gives the same MAP estimate. The ordering only changes how much work and memory the factorization needs.
@@ 64
<strong>"If Λ is sparse, its inverse is sparse too."</strong> The covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\) is usually completely full: every variable is correlated with every other through chains of measurements. Sparsity lives in the information matrix and its factor, which is why we solve with them and never form the inverse.
