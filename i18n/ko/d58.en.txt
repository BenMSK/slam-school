@@ 10
When orientations are known and the noise is isotropic, the Fisher information of a pose graph is \(\mathbf{L}_w\otimes\mathbf{I}_3\): the weighted Laplacian of the graph, with one pose anchored, copied once per axis. The best achievable accuracy is therefore a property of the graph, of who measured whom and how well, and not of where the robot was.
@@ 11
1. More edges never hurt, but they do not help equally
@@ 12
Yesterday's formula \(\mathcal{I}=\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\) is a sum over measurements, and each measurement adds a positive semidefinite matrix. So a new measurement can only make the FIM larger in the Loewner order \(\succeq\), and its inverse, the bound on the covariance, only smaller. The book calls this "information never hurts". <span class="src">§6.2.2 · p. 176</span>
@@ 13
How much it helps depends on <em>which</em> two variables the new edge joins. Closing a large loop helps more than closing a small one. To see why, the book works out the FIM for a simplified problem in which the Jacobian can be written down in terms of the graph alone. <span class="src">§6.2.2 · p. 176</span>

2. The graph as a matrix
@@ 15
The simplified problem: estimate the positions \(\mathbf{t}_i\in\R^3\) of a 3-D pose graph whose rotations \(\mathbf{R}_i\) are already known. Measurement \(k\) is taken by pose \(i_k\) of pose \(j_k\), in its own frame, with isotropic noise of precision \(w_k\): <span class="src">§6.2.2 · p. 176</span>
@@ 16
The <strong>incidence matrix</strong> records which edge joins which nodes. In this section it has one <em>row per node</em> and one <em>column per edge</em>: the column of edge \(k=(i,j)\) has \(-1\) in row \(i\), \(+1\) in row \(j\), and zeros elsewhere. (Box 6.1 on p. 157 uses the transpose, edges as rows. The Laplacian is the same either way; only the side on which the transpose appears changes.)
@@ 17
Relative measurements cannot tell where the whole graph sits: shifting every position by the same vector changes no measurement. This is the <strong>gauge freedom</strong>. It is removed by <strong>anchoring</strong> one pose, usually the first, at the origin, and deleting its row. What is left is the <strong>reduced incidence matrix</strong> \(\mathbf{A}\). <span class="src">§6.2.2 · p. 177, footnote 12</span>

3. From the measurement model to the Laplacian
@@ 26
Filling the gap: the Kronecker product in three rules
@@ 27
\(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B}\) is the block matrix obtained by replacing every entry \(a_{ij}\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) with the block \(a_{ij}\mathbf{B}\). So \(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{I}_3\) is "\(\mathbf{A}\), acting on 3-vectors instead of numbers". Three rules are all today needs:
@@ 28
Stack the positions of the non-anchored poses into \(\mathbf{t}\). Then \((\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{I}_3)\T\mathbf{t}\) is the stack of differences \(\mathbf{t}_{j_k}-\mathbf{t}_{i_k}\), one per edge (try it on the example: the column of \(e_2\) gives \(\mathbf{t}_2-\mathbf{t}_1\), and that of \(e_4\) gives \(-\mathbf{t}_3=\mathbf{t}_0-\mathbf{t}_3\)). With \(\mathbf{R}=\operatorname{BlockDiag}(\mathbf{R}_{i_1},\dots,\mathbf{R}_{i_m})\) rotating each difference into the frame of the observing pose, the whole measurement model and its Jacobian are <span class="src">§6.2.2 · p. 177</span>
@@ 29
Now apply \(\mathcal{I}=\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\), equation (6.52), one step at a time:
@@ 30
The one step that uses the assumptions is \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{R}\T=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\). Block \(k\) of the left side is \(\mathbf{R}_{i_k}(w_k\mathbf{I}_3)\mathbf{R}_{i_k}\T=w_k\mathbf{R}_{i_k}\mathbf{R}_{i_k}\T=w_k\mathbf{I}_3\). Isotropic noise looks the same in every frame, so the rotations drop out. With anisotropic noise they would not, for the same reason as on Day 2.
@@ 31
By rule 3 the Cramér-Rao bound is \(\cov(\hat{\mathbf{t}})\succeq\mathbf{L}_w^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{I}_3\). For pose \(i\) this reads: its position covariance is at least \([\mathbf{L}_w^{-1}]_{ii}\,\mathbf{I}_3\), a sphere whose variance per axis is one diagonal entry of the inverse Laplacian.
@@ 32
Filling the gap: here the bound is attained exactly
@@ 33
With known rotations the model is linear in \(\mathbf{t}\). The weighted least-squares estimate is \(\hat{\mathbf{t}}=\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{z}\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{t}}-\mathbf{t}=\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\). It is unbiased, and its covariance is \(\mathcal{I}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathcal{I}^{-1}=\mathcal{I}^{-1}\). So in this problem \(\mathbf{L}_w^{-1}\otimes\mathbf{I}_3\) is not only a bound: it is the covariance of the optimal estimate.

@@ 35
Odometry alone gives variances 1, 2, 3 along the chain: the variance grows by one step's worth per step, which is the random-walk drift of <a href="#d1">Week 1 · Day 1</a>. The single loop closure brings them to 0.75, 1, 0.75. The pose that gains most is pose 3, the far end of the chain, and the worst pose is now the one in the middle of the loop.
@@ 36
Intuition: a circuit of resistors
@@ 37
Replace each edge by a resistor with conductance \(w_k\) (resistance \(1/w_k\)) and connect the anchor to ground. Then \([\mathbf{L}_w^{-1}]_{ii}\) is the <strong>effective resistance</strong> between pose \(i\) and the anchor. The two school rules do the rest. In series, resistances add: a chain of \(k\) unit edges gives variance \(k\). In parallel, \(R=\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}\): pose 2 above is reached by two paths of resistance 2, so \(\frac{2\cdot 2}{2+2}=1\); pose 1 by paths of resistance 1 and 3, so \(\frac{1\cdot 3}{1+3}=0.75\). A loop closure is a second wire to ground. It helps most where the existing path was long.

5. Beyond the simplified problem
@@ 43
For full pose-graph optimization, where rotations are estimated too, the FIM is no longer a pure Laplacian, but the graph structure is still there: <span class="src">§6.2.2 · pp. 177–178</span>
@@ 44
where \(\mathbf{a}_k\) is column \(k\) of the reduced incidence matrix, \(\mathbf{L}_k\) is the <strong>elementary Laplacian</strong> of edge \(k\), \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\) is that measurement's \(6\times 6\) noise covariance, and \(\Ad\) is the adjoint from <a href="#d23">Week 4 · Day 5</a>. Each edge contributes "its place in the graph" Kronecker "its geometry and noise". Today's result is the special case in which the second factor is \(w_k\mathbf{I}_3\) for every edge, since \(\sum_k w_k\mathbf{a}_k\mathbf{a}_k\T=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\T\). The book reports that design criteria computed from the Laplacian alone closely match those from the full FIM under certain conditions. <span class="src">§6.2.2 · p. 178</span>
@@ 45
<strong>"The covariance of pose i depends only on the edges at pose i."</strong> \(\mathbf{L}_w\) is sparse and local, but its inverse is dense: every edge in the graph affects every pose.
@@ 46
<strong>"Anchoring pose 0 is an approximation."</strong> It is a choice of coordinates. All covariances are then relative to pose 0, which is why they grow with graph distance from it.
@@ 47
<strong>"A loop closure fixes the pose where it lands."</strong> It lowers the uncertainty of every pose on the loop, most of all those far from the anchor along the old path.

Q1. A chain 0–1–2–3–4–5 has unit-weight odometry edges and pose 0 anchored. What is the variance per axis of pose 5?
@@ 49
Five unit resistors in series: the effective resistance to the anchor is 5. Variance grows linearly along a chain.
@@ 50
Q2. Add one loop closure of weight 1 between poses 5 and 0 in that chain. What is the variance per axis of pose 5 now?
@@ 51
Two parallel paths to the anchor, of resistance 5 (the chain) and 1 (the new edge): 5·1/(5+1) = 5/6 ≈ 0.83.
