Radar gets every quantity by comparing two almost identical measurements: two chirp slopes separate range from Doppler, and two antennas give the angle. 1. Triangular modulation: separating range and Doppler
@@ 12
Day 1 treated the beat frequency as pure range. That is not exact. If the target moves, the echo is also Doppler-shifted, and the beat frequency of an up chirp is the sum of a time-of-flight part \(f_{0,t}\) and a Doppler part \(f_{0,d}\). <strong>Triangular modulation</strong> follows each up chirp with a down chirp of the same slope. 2. Angle of arrival
@@ 37
A spinning radar knows the angle of a return: it is where the antenna points. An SoC radar has to compute it. Take two receive antennas (RX) a distance \(\ell\) apart, and a far target at angle \(\theta\) from the array's forward direction. The reflected wave reaches the two antennas along parallel paths, and the path to the second antenna is longer by \(\Delta d = \ell\sin\theta\) (Figure 9.5). A path difference is a phase difference: <span class="src">§9.1.2.5 · p. 257</span>
@@ 38
The factor is \(2\pi/\lambda\) here and \(4\pi/\lambda\) in equation (9.3). In (9.3) a range change is travelled twice, out and back. Here the extra path is travelled once, on the way in.
@@ 39
<strong>Worked example.</strong> Antennas half a wavelength apart, \(\ell = \lambda/2\), measure \(\Delta\phi = \pi/2\). Then \(\sin\theta = (\lambda\cdot\pi/2)/(2\pi\cdot\lambda/2) = 1/2\), so \(\theta = 30°\).
@@ 40
Filling the gap: antenna spacing and angular resolution
@@ 41
As with velocity, the phase is unambiguous only while \(|\Delta\phi| \lt \pi\). From (9.10) that means \(|\sin\theta| \lt \lambda/(2\ell)\). With \(\ell = \lambda/2\) every angle from −90° to 90° is covered, which is why arrays use half-wavelength spacing. With \(\ell = \lambda\) only angles within ±30° are unambiguous.
@@ 42
With \(N\) antennas in a row, the phase steps by the same \(\Delta\phi\) from one antenna to the next, and an FFT across the antennas finds it, exactly like the FFT across chirps on Day 1. <span class="src">§9.1.2.5 · p. 257</span> By the same resolution argument, \(N\) samples resolve \(\Delta\phi\) to \(2\pi/N\), so two targets can be separated when
@@ 43
Eight antennas give 0.25 rad, about 14° straight ahead. This is the reason radar angles are coarse next to LiDAR, and the reason a point far away is uncertain sideways: 0.25 rad at 50 m is 12.5 m. Keep it in mind for Day 3, where registration methods give far points a larger covariance.
@@ 44
The two-receiver example is a single-input, multiple-output (SIMO) array. Most SLAM radars are <strong>MIMO</strong>: several TX and several RX antennas. Adding one more transmitter gives the same angular resolution as doubling the receivers, provided the receivers can tell the transmitters apart. They can if the transmitters use different frequency bands, different codes, or different time slots (TDMA). A two-dimensional array measures elevation as well, which gives 3+1D data. <span class="src">§9.1.2.5 · p. 258</span>

3. What goes wrong: speckle, multipath, motion distortion
@@ 46
Radar data is harder to use than LiDAR data, and the reasons are physical. <span class="src">§9.1.3 · pp. 258–259</span>
@@ 47
<strong>Speckle noise.</strong> One pulse collects energy from everything inside a wide beam. The scattered waves add up, sometimes reinforcing and sometimes cancelling. Together with thermal noise and changing RCS this produces peaks where there is no object and removes peaks where there is one. Without a way to tell them apart, the output is a scatter of spurious points.
@@ 48
<strong>Multipath.</strong> Part of the energy reaches the target and returns by way of the ground or a wall. The radar only knows the path length and the arrival direction, so it places a second target beyond the reflecting surface: a <strong>ghost object</strong>.

The bounced path has the same length as the straight path to the mirror image of the target. The ghost is not random: it appears in the same place in every scan from this spot, which is why the book calls ghosts static outliers.
@@ 58
<strong>Motion distortion.</strong> A spinning radar builds one image during one rotation. The book's example sensor rotates at 4 Hz, so a scan takes 0.25 s. A car at 20 m/s moves 5 m in that time, and an object seen at the start and at the end of the rotation is recorded 5 m apart. Faster sensors exist (10 Hz, up to 50 Hz), but on a fast vehicle the effect stays significant. <span class="src">§9.1.3.3 · p. 259</span> Day 3 shows how to undo it. The book also lists biased and sparse range readings caused by the wide beam, and receiver saturation. <span class="src">§9.1.3 · p. 258</span>

4. Filtering with CFAR
@@ 60
The obvious filter, keeping every cell above a fixed power, fails because the noise level is not fixed. It changes with range, direction and surroundings, so one threshold is too strict in one place and lets false alarms through in another. <span class="src">§9.1.4 · p. 259</span>
@@ 61
The <strong>constant false alarm rate (CFAR)</strong> filter works along one azimuth and slides a window over the range cells. The cell being judged is the <strong>cell under test (CUT)</strong>. The <strong>training cells</strong> on both sides estimate the local noise level. Optional <strong>guard cells</strong> next to the CUT are left out, so that a target that spills into its neighbours does not raise its own noise estimate. In <strong>cell-averaging CFAR (CA-CFAR)</strong> the threshold is the mean power of the training cells times a multiplier chosen from the wanted probability of false alarm. <span class="src">§9.1.4 · p. 260</span>

Filling the gap: where the multiplier comes from
@@ 70
The book says the multiplier is "selected from the desired probability of false alarm" and stops. Here is the calculation for the standard noise model, in which the power \(x\) of a noise-only cell is exponentially distributed with mean \(\mu\): \(p(x) = \frac{1}{\mu}e^{-x/\mu}\). This is what a receiver produces when it squares the two components of complex Gaussian noise.
@@ 71
<em>If \(\mu\) were known</em>, a threshold \(\tau\) would be exceeded by noise with probability \(P(x \gt \tau) = e^{-\tau/\mu}\). Setting this equal to \(P_{fa}\) gives \(\tau = -\mu\ln P_{fa}\). For \(P_{fa} = 10^{-3}\) the threshold is \(6.91\,\mu\).
@@ 72
<em>But \(\mu\) is unknown</em>, so CA-CFAR estimates it from \(N\) training cells, \(\hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_i x_i\), and uses \(\tau = \alpha\hat\mu\). The threshold is now random, so average the false alarm probability over it. The training cells are independent, and for an exponential variable \(\E[e^{-s x}] = 1/(1 + s\mu)\):

@@ 74
For \(N = 20\) and \(P_{fa} = 10^{-3}\): \(\alpha = 20\,(10^{0.15} - 1) = 20 \times 0.4125 = 8.25\). That is more than 6.91, the price of having to estimate the noise. With only \(N = 4\) cells, \(\alpha = 4\,(10^{0.75} - 1) = 18.5\). As \(N\) grows, \(\alpha\) falls towards \(-\ln P_{fa}\).

@@ 76
Notice: at the start CA-CFAR finds 4 of the 5 targets with no false alarm, while the fixed threshold finds all 5 and adds 24 false alarms inside the clutter. The target CFAR misses is the one at 31 m: the stronger target 3 m behind it lies in its training window and lifts the threshold. With 3 training cells per side the window is short enough, all 5 are found, and the multiplier has risen from 8.25 to 12.97. At "1 in 100" two false alarms appear, exactly at the two edges of the clutter, where half the window sees quiet noise.
@@ 77
The misses and edge effects you just saw are the known weaknesses of averaging. <strong>Ordered-statistic CFAR (OS-CFAR)</strong> replaces the mean by a robust statistic such as the median, which a single strong neighbour cannot move. <strong>BFAR</strong> (bounded false alarm rate), designed for radar odometry, applies an affine map \(T = aZ + b\) to the CFAR output \(Z\), with a learnable \(b\), so that the result blends the adaptive threshold with a fixed level. <span class="src">§9.1.4 · p. 260</span>
@@ 78
Many odometry and SLAM pipelines use something simpler than CFAR. CFAR was built for detection, where missing a weak target is costly. Odometry wants only points that will be found again in the next scan and from another viewpoint. Two common choices are to keep the <strong>k strongest returns</strong> above a static threshold along each azimuth, or to keep every return more than one standard deviation above the mean. In Figure 9.7 CFAR reports several false alarms, and k-strongest with k = 12 keeps only points around the main targets. <span class="src">§9.1.4 · pp. 260–261</span> A third route is learned filtering: networks trained with LiDAR as ground truth that keep only the returns a LiDAR would also see. <span class="src">§9.1.4 · pp. 260–261</span>
@@ 79
<strong>"CFAR removes false alarms."</strong> It fixes their rate. At \(P_{fa} = 10^{-3}\), a radargram with a million cells still produces about a thousand of them per scan.
@@ 80
<strong>"More training cells are always better."</strong> They lower the multiplier, but they assume the noise is the same across the whole window. A window that reaches another target or a clutter edge gives a wrong noise estimate.
@@ 81
<strong>"Ghosts average out like noise."</strong> A multipath ghost is repeatable from the same place. It has to be rejected as an outlier, not averaged.

An up chirp measures \(f_{0,t} + f_{0,d}\) and a down chirp \(-f_{0,t} + f_{0,d}\). Half the difference gives range, half the sum gives the Doppler shift \(2v/\lambda\).
@@ 101
Two antennas \(\ell\) apart see a phase difference \((2\pi/\lambda)\ell\sin\theta\), so \(\theta = \arcsin(\lambda\Delta\phi/(2\pi\ell))\). More antennas, real or virtual (MIMO), give finer angles.
