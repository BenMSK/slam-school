A quadratic function of a vector \(\mathbf{x}\) is a <em>linear</em> function of the matrix \(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T\). Rewrite the problem in \(\mathbf{X}\) and all the non-convexity collects in one statement, "\(\mathbf{X}\) has rank 1". Delete that statement and what is left is a convex problem whose optimum bounds the original from below, and often equals it.

2. The trace trick and the lift
@@ 25
The <strong>trace</strong> \(\tr(\mathbf{M})\) of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries. Two facts: a \(1\times1\) matrix equals its own trace, and \(\tr(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})=\tr(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})\) whenever both products are defined (the cyclic property; both sides equal \(\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}\)). Apply them to the number \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\), moving the leading \(\mathbf{x}\T\) to the back: <span class="src">eq. (6.1) · p. 152</span>
@@ 26
The matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) holds all products \(X_{ij}=x_ix_j\), and \(\tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}X_{ij}\) is a weighted sum of them: linear in \(\mathbf{X}\). Check with numbers. For \(\mathbf{x}=(1,2)\) and \(\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}3 &amp; 1\\ 1 &amp; 0\end{bmatrix}\):
@@ 27
Moving from \(\mathbf{x}\) to \(\mathbf{X}\) is called <strong>lifting</strong>. Every lifted matrix has three properties: it is symmetric, it is <strong>positive semidefinite</strong> (written \(\mathbf{X}\succeq0\)) because \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{v})^2\ge0\), and it has rank 1 because every column is a multiple of \(\mathbf{x}\). The book states that the converse also holds: <span class="src">eq. (6.3) · p. 152</span>

Its rank is the number of nonzero eigenvalues, and it is positive semidefinite exactly when no eigenvalue is negative. 3. Drop the rank constraint
@@ 32
Using (6.1) on the objective and on every constraint, and (6.3) to say which \(\mathbf{X}\) are allowed, (QCQP) becomes the equivalent problem (6.4). Deleting its last line gives <strong>Shor's relaxation</strong>: <span class="src">eq. (6.4), (SDP) · p. 153</span>
@@ 33
Look at what remains on the right. The objective is linear in \(\mathbf{X}\). The equality constraints are linear, so they cut out a flat (affine) set. The set \(\mathbf{X}\succeq0\) is convex (Day 1, section 4). An intersection of convex sets is convex, and a linear function is convex. So the right-hand problem is a convex problem. Problems of this shape are called <strong>semidefinite programs (SDPs)</strong> and can be solved in polynomial time. <span class="src">§6.1.1 · p. 153</span>
@@ 34
The price is size. \(\mathbf{x}\) has \(n\) entries; a symmetric \(\mathbf{X}\) has \(n(n+1)/2\) free entries (footnote 1 on p. 153). For \(n=3\) that is 6 instead of 3; for \(n=3000\) it is about 4.5 million.

Filling the gap: see the relaxation for n = 2
@@ 36
Take the QCQP "minimize \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}\) over unit vectors \(\mathbf{x}\in\R^2\)". Write \(\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}a &amp; b\\ b &amp; c\end{bmatrix}\). The constraint \(\tr(\mathbf{X})=1\) says \(a+c=1\). A symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix with positive trace is positive semidefinite exactly when its determinant is non-negative, \(ac-b^2\ge0\). Change coordinates to \(u=a-c\) and \(v=2b\). Then \(ac=\tfrac14\bigl((a+c)^2-(a-c)^2\bigr)=\tfrac14(1-u^2)\), and
@@ 37
So the feasible set of the SDP is a <em>disk</em>. Rank 1 means determinant zero, which is the boundary circle. A unit vector \(\mathbf{x}=(\cos t,\sin t)\) lifts to \(u=\cos^2t-\sin^2t=\cos2t\), \(v=2\sin t\cos t=\sin2t\): the point at angle \(2t\) on that circle. Shor's relaxation fills the circle in.
@@ 38
The objective becomes \(\tr(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}) = \tfrac12(C_{11}+C_{22}) + \tfrac12(C_{11}-C_{22})\,u + C_{12}\,v\), a linear function on the disk. A linear function on a disk takes its smallest value at a boundary point, so here the SDP always has a rank-1 solution and \(d^\star=p^\star\). Example: \(\mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}3 &amp; 2\\ 2 &amp; 0\end{bmatrix}\) gives \(1.5+1.5u+2v\), smallest at \((u,v)=-(1.5,2)/2.5=(-0.6,-0.8)\) with value \(1.5-2.5=-1\). Then \(a=0.2\), \(c=0.8\), \(b=-0.4\), which is \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T\) for \(\mathbf{x}=(1,-2)/\sqrt5\). And \(-1\) is indeed the smallest eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{C}\).

The disk example is always exact. Here is one that is not. Three variables, each \(\pm1\), every pair penalized for agreeing:
@@ 60
Since \((x_1+x_2+x_3)^2 = 3+2(x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3)\), the cost equals \((x_1+x_2+x_3)^2-3\). Three signs cannot sum to zero; the best is a sum of \(\pm1\), so \(p^\star=1-3=-2\). In the relaxation, \(\mathbf{X}\) is positive semidefinite with ones on its diagonal, and the cost is \(2(X_{12}+X_{13}+X_{23})=\mathbf{1}\T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{1}-3\), where \(\mathbf{1}\) is the vector of ones. Because \(\mathbf{X}\succeq0\), \(\mathbf{1}\T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{1}\ge0\), so the cost is at least \(-3\); and \(X_{12}=X_{13}=X_{23}=-\tfrac12\) reaches it with a matrix whose eigenvalues are \(0,\tfrac32,\tfrac32\). So \(d^\star=-3\lt p^\star=-2\), and \(\mathbf{X}^\star\) has rank 2.

@@ 63
Any positive semidefinite \(\mathbf{X}\) with ones on the diagonal is a table of dot products \(X_{ij}=\mathbf{y}_i\cdot\mathbf{y}_j\) of unit vectors. The QCQP forces those vectors onto a line, where a unit vector can only be \(+1\) or \(-1\). The relaxation lets them turn into extra dimensions. If the best arrangement still fits on a line, the relaxation is exact. For three variables a plane is always enough room, which is why the widget can draw the answer.

<strong>"The SDP solution is a better solution of the QCQP."</strong> If its rank is above 1 it is not a solution of the QCQP at all. Its value is a bound, not an achievable cost.
@@ 66
<strong>"A relaxation that is not tight is useless."</strong> The bound \(f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})-d^\star\) still limits how wrong any candidate can be. In the three-variable example it says "at most 1 above optimal".
@@ 67
<strong>"Lifting changes the problem."</strong> Problem (6.4) with the rank constraint is exactly (QCQP). Only deleting the rank constraint changes it.

Q1. What is the lift X = xxᵀ of x = (2, −1)?
@@ 69
The entries are the products x_i x_j: 2·2 = 4, 2·(−1) = −2, (−1)·(−1) = 1. Its determinant is 4 − 4 = 0, as it must be for rank 1.

Q2. A QCQP has n = 4 unknowns. How many free numbers does the matrix variable of its Shor relaxation have?
@@ 71
A symmetric 4 × 4 matrix has 4 diagonal and 6 off-diagonal free entries: n(n+1)/2 = 10.
