1. Inliers obey geometry
@@ 12
A SLAM front-end first produces a set of candidate measurements that may contain many outliers, then filters it. <span class="src">§3.2 · p. 78</span> The book's running case (Example 3.1) is feature-based visual SLAM. The front-end matches 2D feature points between the image at time \(k-1\) and the image at time \(k\). Each matched pair is a <strong>2D-2D correspondence</strong>, and some matches are wrong. <span class="src">§3.2.1 · p. 79</span>

3. The RANSAC loop
@@ 36
<strong>RANdom SAmple Consensus (RANSAC)</strong> rests on one assumption: \(\mathbf{x}\) is low-dimensional and can be computed from a handful of measurements. The smallest number of measurements that determines \(\mathbf{x}\) is the size \(n\) of the <strong>minimal set</strong>, and a fast routine that computes \(\mathbf{x}\) from exactly \(n\) measurements is a <strong>minimal solver</strong>. <span class="src">§3.2.1 · p. 80</span>

The set kept at the end is the <strong>consensus set</strong>. <span class="src">§3.2.1 · pp. 80–81</span> The logic: if all \(n\) sampled measurements happen to be inliers, \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) is close to the truth and all the other inliers agree with it. If the sample contains an outlier, \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) is wrong and few measurements agree. 4. How many iterations?
@@ 54
Let \(\omega\) be the probability that a randomly drawn measurement is an inlier, which is the inlier fraction if sampling is uniform. The book states that the expected number of iterations to draw an all-inlier sample is \(1/\omega^n\). <span class="src">§3.2.1 · p. 81</span>

@@ 56

5. When RANSAC is the right tool, and when it is not
@@ 64
RANSAC is the default for outlier rejection in landmark-based front-ends because minimal solvers are fast (hundreds of iterations in a few milliseconds), and because it returns an estimate \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), for example the camera motion, that serves as an initial guess for the back-end. <span class="src">§3.2.1 · p. 81</span>
@@ 65
It stops being practical in two situations. <span class="src">§3.2.1–3.2.2 · p. 81</span>
@@ 66
<strong>Few inliers.</strong> The book's rule of thumb is that RANSAC works well below about 70% outliers. Strong perceptual aliasing can push the outlier rate far higher.
@@ 67
<strong>Large minimal sets.</strong> A pose graph with \(N\) poses needs at least \(N-1\) relative-pose measurements to determine every pose (a spanning tree), and \(N\) is in the thousands. There is no small sample to draw.
@@ 68
Stopping RANSAC early in those cases does not give a slightly worse answer. It gives a wrong \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) and a wrong set of correspondences. Tomorrow's method avoids sampling altogether.
@@ 69
<strong>"RANSAC solves consensus maximization."</strong> It is a heuristic for (3.4). It returns the best set among the samples it happened to try, with no guarantee that it is the largest one.
@@ 70
<strong>"A larger sample gives a better model, so sample more than the minimum."</strong> Every extra point multiplies the chance of a clean sample by \(\omega\). Accuracy comes afterwards, from refitting to the whole consensus set.
@@ 71
<strong>"The threshold γ is a detail."</strong> Too small, and noisy inliers are rejected so that good samples score badly. Too large, and outliers near the model are accepted. Move γ in the widget to 0.10 and to 1.50 and watch the consensus set.

Q1. Half of the points are inliers and you fit a line (n = 2). What is the expected number of RANSAC iterations until a sample contains only inliers?
@@ 73
One sample is clean with probability 0.5² = 0.25, and the expected number of trials until the first success is 1/0.25 = 4.
@@ 74
Q2. Same setting (ω = 0.5, n = 2). How many iterations make the probability of at least one all-inlier sample 99%?
@@ 75
k ≥ log(0.01)/log(1 − 0.25) = (−4.605)/(−0.2877) = 16.01, so 17. The expected count of 4 is far from a guarantee: after 4 iterations the failure probability is still 0.75⁴ = 0.32.
