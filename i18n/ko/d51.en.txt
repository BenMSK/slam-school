With the right noise model, pose-graph optimization has a cost that is quadratic in the entries of the rotations and translations. For fixed rotations the best translations solve a linear least-squares problem, so they can be written down and substituted away. What remains is \(\min\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})\) over rotations: one data matrix, a quadratic objective, and rotation constraints. That is a QCQP waiting for Shor's relaxation.

1. Poses, relative poses, and the pose graph
@@ 12
<strong>Pose-graph optimization (PGO)</strong> estimates \(n\) poses \(\mathbf{T}_1,\dots,\mathbf{T}_n\in\SE(d)\), with \(d=2\) or \(3\), from noisy measurements of relative poses. <span class="src">§6.1.2.1 · p. 154</span> A pose is a pair \(\mathbf{T}_i=(\mathbf{t}_i,\mathbf{R}_i)\) of a position \(\mathbf{t}_i\in\R^d\) and a rotation \(\mathbf{R}_i\in\SO(d)\), or as a matrix (<a href="#d19">Week 4 · Day 1</a>)
@@ 13
So the true relative pose "pose \(j\) seen from pose \(i\)" has rotation \(\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}=\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_j\) and translation \(\bar{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}=\mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i)\). Example in the plane: pose \(i\) is at \((1,0)\) facing 90° (up), pose \(j\) is at \((1,2)\). Then \(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i=(0,2)\), and rotating by \(-90^\circ\) gives \(\bar{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}=(2,0)\): two meters straight ahead, as it should be.
@@ 14
A measurement \(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{ij}=(\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij},\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij})\) is a noisy version of \(\mathbf{T}_i^{-1}\mathbf{T}_j\). The <strong>pose graph</strong> has one node per pose and one directed edge \(i\to j\) per measurement \(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{ij}\). The book assumes the graph is connected; otherwise the problem splits into independent pieces. <span class="src">§6.1.2.1 · p. 155</span>

2. The noise model, and the cost it produces
@@ 16
To do maximum likelihood (<a href="#d8">Week 2 · Day 2</a>) you need a probability density for the noise. For translations the book uses a Gaussian. For rotations it uses the <strong>isotropic Langevin distribution</strong> \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{M},\kappa)\) on \(\SO(d)\): <span class="src">eq. (6.7) · p. 155</span>
@@ 17
\(\mathbf{M}\) is the most likely rotation (the <strong>mode</strong>), \(\kappa\ge0\) is the <strong>concentration</strong>, and \(c_d(\kappa)\) normalizes. In the plane, \(\mathbf{M}\T\mathbf{R}\) is a rotation by the angle \(\theta\) between the two, whose trace is \(2\cos\theta\). The density is then proportional to \(\exp(2\kappa\cos\theta)\), the von Mises distribution on the circle with concentration \(2\kappa\). <span class="src">p. 156</span> For large \(\kappa\) only small \(\theta\) matter, \(\cos\theta\approx1-\theta^2/2\), and the density is close to \(\exp(-\kappa\theta^2)\): a Gaussian with variance \(1/(2\kappa)\). So \(\kappa=50\) means a standard deviation of \(0.1\) rad, about 5.7°. With \(\kappa=0\) every rotation is equally likely.

5. What Problem 6.2 looks like
@@ 61
Problem 6.2 has \(n\) rotations as unknowns and a single \(dn\times dn\) data matrix \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\) that is computed once from the measurements. \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\) is positive semidefinite, since \(\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})\) is a sum of squared norms for <em>any</em> \(d\times dn\) matrix \(\mathbf{R}\). The book points out that the problem now has the form of rotation averaging, with a more complicated data matrix. <span class="src">§6.1.2.2 · p. 160</span>
@@ 62
One practical point. \(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\) is a dense \(m\times m\) matrix, which would be expensive for a large graph. Equation (6.18) rewrites it using a sparse triangular factor of the (reduced) weighted incidence matrix, so that products with \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\) stay cheap. <span class="src">eq. (6.18) · p. 160</span> The solver of Day 5 needs only such products.
@@ 63
The objective is quadratic in the entries of \(\mathbf{R}\). The only obstacle left is the constraint set \(\SO(d)^n\). Tomorrow it gets the treatment of Day 2.

Q4. Why can the translations be eliminated in closed form, but not the rotations?
@@ 71
Translations are measured more accurately
@@ 72
For fixed rotations the cost is an unconstrained linear least-squares problem in the translations; the rotations are constrained to SO(d)
@@ 73
There are fewer translations than rotations
@@ 74
The Laplacian of the graph is invertible
@@ 75
Translations are free vectors that enter the residuals linearly, so the normal equations solve them. Rotations enter linearly too, but must satisfy quadratic constraints, so no single linear solve gives the optimum.
