@@ 10
Elimination removes one variable at a time. Collect the factors that touch the variable, multiply them, and split the product into a conditional density of that variable given its neighbours and a new factor on the neighbours alone. When every variable is gone, the factor graph has become a Bayes net that hands you the MAP estimate by back-substitution. For linear-Gaussian factors each split is a small QR factorization, and the whole procedure is sparse QR.

1. Where we are going: a product of small conditionals
@@ 12
A factor graph stores the unnormalized posterior as a product of factors, \(\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \phi(\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n)\), equation (1.41). The <strong>variable elimination algorithm</strong> rewrites it, for a chosen ordering \(\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n\), as equation (1.42):
@@ 13
A product of conditionals in which each variable depends only on variables that come later is a <strong>Bayes net</strong>: a directed graph with an arrow into \(\mathbf{x}_j\) from each variable it is conditioned on. The set \(\mathbf{s}_j\) of those variables is the <strong>separator</strong> of \(\mathbf{x}_j\). <span class="src">§1.6.1 · p. 40</span> The \(p(\mathbf{x})\) here is the posterior \(p(\mathbf{x} \mid \mathbf{z})\) of Week 2; the measurements are fixed numbers inside the factors, so the book stops writing them.
@@ 14
Any density can be written as a chain, \(p(x_1 \mid x_2, \dots, x_n)\, p(x_2 \mid x_3, \dots, x_n) \cdots p(x_n)\), by the chain rule of probability, but in general the first conditional depends on all the other variables. A sparse factor graph gives small separators: in the toy example no conditional depends on more than two variables.
@@ 15
Why want this form? The last conditional \(p(\mathbf{x}_n)\) depends on nothing, so you can maximize it alone. Then \(p(\mathbf{x}_{n-1} \mid \mathbf{s}_{n-1})\) has all its parents known, and so on backwards. The book adds that sampling and marginalization are easy in this form too.

@@ 20
By construction the conditional integrates to 1 over \(\mathbf{x}_j\) for every value of \(\mathbf{s}_j\), so it is a proper density, and the product of the two pieces is \(\psi\) again. Nothing is lost or approximated. The factor \(\tau\) is a summary: everything the removed factors implied about the separator once \(\mathbf{x}_j\) itself is no longer asked about.
@@ 21
Only the factors touching \(\mathbf{x}_j\) are involved. Every other factor is a constant as far as \(\mathbf{x}_j\) is concerned and stays in the graph untouched. This locality is the whole reason elimination is cheap on a sparse graph.

5. Do it for every variable and you have sparse QR
@@ 64
Each step moves one finished row \([\mathbf{R}_j \;\, \mathbf{T}_j \mid \mathbf{d}_j]\) to the top part of the matrix and returns the rows \([\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_\tau \mid \tilde{\mathbf{b}}_\tau]\) to the pool. Figure 1.12 shows the remaining four steps. At the end the finished rows form an upper-triangular \(\mathbf{R}\) with the same pattern as the Bayes net: row \(j\) has a nonzero in column \(k\) exactly when \(\mathbf{x}_k\) is in the separator of \(\mathbf{x}_j\). The elimination algorithm with partial QR <em>is</em> sparse QR factorization, and the book states that sparse Cholesky is a special case of the same algorithm. <span class="src">§1.6.2 · pp. 42–44</span> Tomorrow's Schur complement is its Cholesky form.
@@ 65
Because each variable here is a block of several numbers (a pose, a landmark), every step eliminates a group of scalar columns at once. The book identifies this as <strong>multi-frontal QR</strong>, and notes that sparse linear algebra libraries form such groups on their own for speed; in SLAM the groups come for free from the meaning of the variables. <span class="src">§1.6.2 · pp. 44–45</span>
@@ 66
<strong>"Eliminating a variable discards it."</strong> Its conditional is kept, and \(\psi = p(\mathbf{x}_j \mid \mathbf{s}_j)\,\tau(\mathbf{s}_j)\) holds exactly. Elimination reorganizes the information; it loses none.
@@ 67
<strong>"The new factor is an extra measurement."</strong> It is a summary of measurements that were already there. In the worked step, "p₂ − p₁ = 0.8" was implied by ϕ₇ and ϕ₈ all along.
@@ 68
<strong>"The separator is the set of neighbours in the original graph."</strong> It is the set of neighbours at the moment of elimination. Factors created by earlier steps count, which is how new edges (fill-in) appear.

Elimination turns the product of factors \(\phi(\mathbf{x})\) into a Bayes net \(\prod_j p(\mathbf{x}_j \mid \mathbf{s}_j)\), one variable at a time.
