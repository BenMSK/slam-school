The regularized spline problem can be rewritten so that the basis functions appear only through one matrix, \(\mathbf{K} = \boldsymbol{\Psi}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\T\), whose entry \((i,j)\) says how strongly the trajectory at time \(t_i\) is tied to the trajectory at time \(t_j\). 1. Too many basis functions, or too few
@@ 13
A parametric method makes you pick the type and number of basis functions before you see the data. Pick too many and the curve has enough freedom to follow the noise: it <strong>overfits</strong>. Pick too few and the curve cannot bend as much as the real trajectory did: it is too smooth. <span class="src">§2.2.2 · p. 65</span>
@@ 14
Try it: the dots are noisy position measurements (noise standard deviation 0.25) taken at irregular times, with nothing between 5.2 s and 7 s. First shrink the knot spacing to 0.4 s and set λ to 0.001. Then set the spacing to 3 s. Then try 0.4 s with λ = 0.
@@ 15
Notice the two error numbers. At the start (knots every 1 s, 12 coefficients for 24 measurements) the residual is 0.21, about the noise level, and the error against the true curve is 0.16. With knots every 0.4 s and λ = 0.001 the residual drops to 0.06, far below the noise, while the true error rises to 0.39: the curve is fitting noise and wanders in the gap. With knots every 3 s both numbers are near 0.4: the curve is too stiff. With λ = 0 and close knots the problem has no unique solution at all.

Going deeper (optional): when does the inverse of K exist?
@@ 34
\(\mathbf{K} = \boldsymbol{\Psi}\boldsymbol{\Psi}\T\) has as many rows as there are measurement times but rank at most the number of coefficients. With more times than coefficients, which the book notes is the usual case, this \(\mathbf{K}\) is singular and step 4 cannot be taken literally. Step 3 is always valid. The issue disappears in the next section: there \(\mathbf{K}\) is built from a kernel function and, for the kernels used in practice and distinct times, it is positive definite.

4. The kernel trick
@@ 36
Look at what \(\mathbf{K}\) contains. Its entry \((i,j)\) is row \(i\) of \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}\) times row \(j\):
@@ 37
an inner product of the basis functions evaluated at two times. It is large when the same basis functions are active at both times, and zero when no basis function covers both. The matrix \(\mathbf{K}\) is called the <strong>kernel matrix</strong>. <span class="src">§2.2.2 · p. 66</span>
@@ 38
Filling the gap: K is a prior covariance
@@ 39
The regularizer said the coefficients have prior \(\mathbf{c} \sim \N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\). The trajectory values at the measurement times are \(\mathbf{p} = \boldsymbol{\Psi}\mathbf{c}\), a linear function of a Gaussian, so they are Gaussian with mean zero and covariance
@@ 40
So \(K_{ij}\) is the covariance, before any measurement, between the trajectory at \(t_i\) and at \(t_j\). Its inverse \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\) is the information matrix of that prior (<a href="#d3">Week 1 · Day 3</a>), and (2.45) says: information from the measurements, \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\), plus information from the prior, \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\). A penalty on coefficients has turned into a prior directly on the trajectory.
@@ 41
Equation (2.45) needs the basis functions only through these inner products. The <strong>kernel trick</strong> is to skip the basis functions and choose a function of two times, the <strong>kernel function</strong> \(\mathcal{K}(t,t')\), that returns the inner product directly. The kernel matrix is then filled in by evaluating it at every pair of measurement times: <span class="src">§2.2.2 · p. 66</span>

A spline says "the trajectory is one of the shapes I can build from these pieces". A kernel says "any shape is allowed, but values at nearby times must agree". 5. What this costs
@@ 64
Two things look worse in (2.45). The system is larger, because there are usually more measurement times than coefficients; the book returns to this at the end of §2.2.3, and so do we on <a href="#d28">Day 4</a>. And it needs \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\). For a kernel such as the squared exponential every pair of times has a nonzero entry in \(\mathbf{K}\), and in general its inverse is dense too. A dense \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\) is a factor that ties every pose to every other pose, which destroys the sparsity that made SLAM tractable in Week 3. <span class="src">§2.2.2 · p. 66</span>
@@ 65
Tomorrow's question is therefore: is there a family of kernels for which \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\) is sparse by construction? You have already seen a hint. In the numeric check above, \(\mathbf{K} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 &amp; 1 \\ 1 &amp; 2 \end{bmatrix}\) is full, but its inverse has the pattern of a chain.
@@ 66
<strong>"Nonparametric means there is nothing to tune."</strong> It means no fixed, finite set of basis-function coefficients. The kernel's hyperparameters still have to be chosen, and they set how smooth the result is.
@@ 67
<strong>"More basis functions always fit better."</strong> They fit the measurements better. They fit the true trajectory worse once they start following the noise, as the widget shows.
@@ 68
<strong>"K and its inverse have the same sparsity."</strong> They generally do not. A full \(\mathbf{K}\) can have a sparse inverse, and that is exactly what the next section exploits.

Q2. At time t₁ the active basis-function values are (0.5, 0.5, 0) and at time t₂ they are (0, 0.5, 0.5). What is the kernel-matrix entry K₁₂?
@@ 76
K₁₂ is the inner product of the two rows: 0.5·0 + 0.5·0.5 + 0·0.5 = 0.25. Only the shared basis function contributes.
