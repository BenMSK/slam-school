Almost every computation in a SLAM back-end ends in the same small problem: find the \(\mathbf{x}\) that makes \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\) as close as possible to \(\mathbf{b}\). The answer solves the <strong>normal equations</strong> \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\). Dividing each equation by its noise level first turns this into the maximum likelihood estimate under Gaussian noise.

Two differentiation rules are all you need: \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{c}\T\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{c}\) and, for a symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\), \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}) = 2\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}\). They are the vector versions of \((cx)' = c\) and \((qx^2)' = 2qx\). With \(\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\):
@@ 27
Is this stationary point a minimum, and the only one? The second derivative of \(f\) is the constant matrix \(2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\). For any direction \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}_2^2 \ge 0\), so \(f\) curves upward everywhere. If the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) are linearly independent (\(\mathbf{A}\) has <strong>full column rank</strong>), then \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\) whenever \(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\), the curvature is strictly positive in every direction, and \(f\) is a bowl with exactly one lowest point. If the columns are dependent, a whole line of \(\mathbf{x}\) values gives the same cost. That happens in SLAM when nothing anchors the map: with odometry only and no prior, shifting every pose by the same amount changes no residual.
@@ 28
The normal equations also have a geometric reading. Move everything to one side: \(\mathbf{A}\T(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^\star) = \mathbf{0}\). Each row of \(\mathbf{A}\T\) is a column of \(\mathbf{A}\), so this says that the residual is perpendicular ("normal") to every column of \(\mathbf{A}\).
@@ 29 attr
A tilted plane represents the column space of A, the set of all vectors A x. The vector b starts at the origin in the plane and points out of the plane. The vector A x-hat lies in the plane and ends directly below the tip of b. The dashed residual joins the two tips and meets the plane at a right angle.

3. Weights: trusting some measurements more
@@ 34
Plain least squares treats every equation alike. If measurement \(i\) has noise standard deviation \(\sigma_i\), a residual should count in units of its own \(\sigma_i\). <strong>Weighted least squares</strong> minimizes \(\sum_i r_i^2/\sigma_i^2\). With the covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) of the measurement noise (diagonal with entries \(\sigma_i^2\) when the noises are independent) this is
@@ 35
The quantity \(\norm{\mathbf{e}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \mathbf{e}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{e}\) is the squared <strong>Mahalanobis distance</strong>, the book's equation (1.10). <span class="src">§1.2.2 · p. 27</span> It measures an error in standard deviations. Example: \(\mathbf{e} = (3, 1)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \diag(9,\ 0.25)\) gives \(9/9 + 1/0.25 = 1 + 4 = 5\). The first component is 3 units off where \(\sigma = 3\): one sigma. The second is 1 unit off where \(\sigma = 0.5\): two sigma, so it contributes four times as much, although it is the smaller number.
@@ 36
You never need a separate weighted solver. Divide row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) and entry \(i\) of \(\mathbf{b}\) by \(\sigma_i\) (in general, multiply both by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\)) and the weighted problem becomes a plain one. This is <strong>whitening</strong>, and the book does it once in equations (1.22)–(1.23) and then drops the covariances from the notation. <span class="src">§1.3.1 · p. 30</span> Whitening also removes units, so rows that came from ranges in metres and rows that came from angles in radians can sit in the same \(\mathbf{A}\).
@@ 37
Try it: the amber measurement disagrees with the other five. Change how much noise you believe it has. You can also drag any point.
@@ 38
Notice: at σ = 1 the weighted line and the equal-weight line coincide, and the single amber point tilts the fit a long way because it sits far out at the right end. Raise its σ to 5 and the solid line swings back onto the other five points, while the amber residual, though large, is under one of its own σ. Lower σ to 0.2 and the line passes almost through the amber point.
@@ 39
Six witnesses describe the same event. Least squares finds the story that disagrees with all of them as little as possible. Weights say how reliable each witness is. A witness you barely trust (large σ) can be far from the final story without it counting for much.

4. Why squares? Gaussian maximum likelihood
@@ 41
Squaring the residuals is not a matter of taste. It is what Gaussian noise implies.

@@ 50
<strong>"Least squares minimizes the perpendicular distance from the points to the line."</strong> It minimizes the error in the measured quantity, here the vertical distance. The perpendicularity in the figure is in the space of measurement vectors, not in the plot of the data.
@@ 51
<strong>"A larger σ gives a measurement more weight."</strong> The weight is \(1/\sigma^2\). Doubling σ quarters the weight.
@@ 52
<strong>"The subscript in \(\norm{\mathbf{e}}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) means multiply by Σ."</strong> The subscript names the covariance; the formula uses its inverse.
@@ 53
<strong>"If \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) is singular, the software is at fault."</strong> A singular \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) means the measurements do not determine \(\mathbf{x}\). The cure is another measurement or a prior.

Q1. Three equally good measurements of one unknown x read 2, 3, and 7. What is the least-squares estimate?
@@ 55
A is a column of three ones, so AᵀA = 3 and Aᵀb = 12. The estimate is 12/3 = 4, the mean. The mean is the least-squares answer to "one number that fits them all".
@@ 56
Q2. Two measurements of one unknown: 10 with σ = 1 and 16 with σ = 2. What is the weighted least-squares estimate?
@@ 57
Weights are 1/σ²: 1 and 0.25. (10 × 1 + 16 × 0.25) / (1 + 0.25) = 14 / 1.25 = 11.2. This is the fusion rule of Day 3.

Q4. An error e = (4, 1) has covariance diag(4, 1). What is its squared Mahalanobis distance?
@@ 65
eᵀΣ⁻¹e = 16/4 + 1/1 = 5. The value 17 is the plain squared length, and 3 comes from dividing by σ instead of σ².
@@ 66
Q5. One row of a least-squares problem reads x₁ + 2x₂ = 5, from a sensor with σ = 0.1. What is the whitened row?
@@ 67
Whitening divides the row and its right-hand side by σ, here multiplying by 10. Squaring the whitened residual then gives the 1/σ² = 100 weight.
@@ 68
Least squares picks the \(\mathbf{x}\) that minimizes \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}}_2^2\), the sum of squared residuals.
@@ 69
The minimizer solves the normal equations \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\), and is unique when \(\mathbf{A}\) has full column rank. The residual is orthogonal to the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\).
@@ 70
Weighted least squares minimizes the Mahalanobis norm \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\). Whitening (dividing each row by its σ) reduces it to the plain problem.
@@ 71
Under independent Gaussian noise the weighted least-squares solution is the maximum likelihood estimate.
@@ 72
\(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \mathbf{A}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\) is the information matrix of the estimate, and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\) is its covariance.
