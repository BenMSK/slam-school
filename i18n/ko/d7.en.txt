The belief about all poses and landmarks, given all measurements, is one huge probability density. It splits into a product of small pieces, each involving only one or two unknowns. A <strong>factor graph</strong> draws that product: one node per unknown, one node per piece, and an edge wherever a piece depends on an unknown. Everything the back-end does later this week is computed on this graph.

1. Smoothing: estimate the whole trajectory
@@ 12
Chapter 1 is about the back-end only. The front-end has already extracted the measurements and decided which measurement belongs to which landmark (data association). <span class="src">Ch. 1 · p. 19</span>
@@ 13
The chapter takes the <strong>smoothing</strong> view: the unknowns are every pose the robot has had so far, not only the latest one. If the unknowns are poses alone, the problem is <strong>pose-based SLAM</strong>. If landmark parameters are estimated too, it is the <strong>full SLAM</strong> problem. <span class="src">Historical note · p. 19</span>
@@ 14
Keeping all the old poses sounds wasteful. It pays off because of structure. A filter that keeps only the latest pose ends up with a covariance or information matrix in which every entry is non-zero. With the whole trajectory kept, the information matrix has few non-zero entries and stays that way. <span class="src">Historical note · p. 19</span> The method this chapter builds toward factorizes that matrix, or the measurement Jacobian, into a triangular "square root", hence the name square-root smoothing and mapping (SAM). <span class="src">Historical note · p. 20</span> You meet the matrix on Day 3 and its sparsity in Week 3.

2. The toy example: unknowns and measurements
@@ 16
The book's running example has a robot at three successive poses \(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3\) and two landmarks \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1,\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\). The robot measures the <strong>bearing</strong> (the direction) to \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\) from \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_2\), and to \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\) from \(\mathbf{p}_3\). There is also one absolute measurement of \(\mathbf{p}_1\). <span class="src">§1.1.1 · pp. 20–21</span>
@@ 17
The <strong>state</strong> \(\mathbf{x}\) stacks all unknowns into one vector, equation (1.2):
@@ 18
In the plane a pose is three numbers (position \(x, y\) and heading \(\theta\)) and a landmark is two, so \(\mathbf{x}\) has \(3\cdot 3 + 2\cdot 2 = 13\) entries. In this chapter the book treats a pose as an ordinary vector and postpones the special handling that rotations need to Chapter 2. <span class="src">§1.1.1 · p. 20, footnote 1</span>
@@ 19
Measurements are noisy, so the true \(\mathbf{x}\) cannot be recovered. What can be computed is a <strong>probability density function (PDF)</strong> \(p(\mathbf{x})\): a non-negative function that integrates to one, equation (1.1), and says how much belief each value of \(\mathbf{x}\) deserves. The density we want is the <strong>posterior</strong> \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\), the belief about the unknowns after seeing the measurements \(\mathbf{z}\), equation (1.3). Computing it, or its peak, is called <strong>probabilistic inference</strong>. <span class="src">§1.1.1 · p. 21</span>

Filling the gap: where equation (1.4) comes from
@@ 24
The book gives the result in one line. Here are the steps.
@@ 25
<strong>Step 1, Bayes' law.</strong> \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) = p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x})/p(\mathbf{z})\). The measurements are fixed numbers, so \(p(\mathbf{z})\) is a constant, and \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) \propto p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x})\). The first factor is the <strong>likelihood</strong> (how probable the measurements are if the state were \(\mathbf{x}\)). The second is the <strong>prior</strong> (what we believed before measuring).
@@ 26
<strong>Step 2, split the prior.</strong> Assume that, before any measurement, the landmarks are independent of the trajectory and of each other: \(p(\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3)\,p(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\,p(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2)\). The chain rule of probability, which is always true, gives
@@ 27
The <strong>Markov assumption</strong> says the next pose depends on the past only through the current pose: \(p(\mathbf{p}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2) = p(\mathbf{p}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_2)\). That gives the first two lines of (1.4).
@@ 28
<strong>Step 3, split the likelihood.</strong> Assume the noise of each measurement is independent of the noise of the others. Then, once the state is given, the measurements are independent: \(p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \prod_k p(\mathbf{z}_k\mid\mathbf{x})\). Each measurement depends only on the variables it physically involves, so \(p(\mathbf{z}_2\mid\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{z}_2\mid\mathbf{p}_1,\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\), and likewise for the others. That gives the last two lines.
@@ 29
All the sparsity of SLAM comes from steps 2 and 3: motion is local in time, and a sensor reading involves one pose and at most a few other variables.
@@ 30
Before introducing factor graphs, the book draws this model as a <strong>Bayesian network</strong> (Figure 1.2): a node for every variable, the measurements included, each carrying a prior or conditional density. Known quantities are drawn as squares. <span class="src">§1.1.1 · pp. 21–22</span> A Bayes net is good for describing how the data were generated. For optimization we want a picture that contains only what we solve for.

4. The factor graph
@@ 32
A factor graph has two kinds of nodes. A <strong>variable node</strong> stands for one unknown. A <strong>factor node</strong>, drawn as a small black dot, stands for one term of the product, and is joined only to the variables that term depends on. The measurements do not appear as nodes: they are known numbers, already inside the factors. <span class="src">§1.1.2 · pp. 22–23</span>

A factor joined to one variable is called <strong>unary</strong>, and one joined to two is <strong>binary</strong>. <span class="src">§1.1.3 · p. 25, footnote 2</span> Two details matter later. A factor only has to be <em>proportional</em> to its density: constants that do not depend on the state can be dropped, because they change neither the shape of the posterior nor the location of its peak. @@ 53
Each dot is one clue. "I started about here." "I then moved about this far." "From where I stood, that tree was in this direction." Each clue mentions only one or two things. The graph is the list of clues, drawn so that you can see at a glance which things each clue talks about.

Notice the count of undetermined directions. At the start it is 1: \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\) is seen once, and a single bearing only places it somewhere along a line of sight. A landmark prior, a range measurement, or a second sighting (sensing range 2.0) brings it to 0. With no prior on \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and no landmark priors it never drops below 3: the whole picture can shift in \(x\), shift in \(y\), and rotate without changing any measurement.

5. Factor graphs as a language
@@ 58
Each factor can be read as one equation in the variables it touches. There are usually more equations than unknowns, and they contradict each other slightly because of noise. That is why each equation needs a statement of how much it is trusted, and why the solution will be a least-squares one (tomorrow). <span class="src">§1.1.3 · p. 24</span>

<strong>"The black dots are the measurements."</strong> A dot is a function of the unknowns. A measurement is a number stored inside that function. Priors are dots too, and they involve no measurement at all.
@@ 76
<strong>"A factor must be a properly normalized probability."</strong> It only needs to be proportional to one. Constants that do not depend on \(\mathbf{x}\) are dropped.
@@ 77
<strong>"More factors than variables means the problem is solvable."</strong> Counting is not enough. In the widget's starting state there are 6 factors for 5 variables, and one landmark is still not determined.
