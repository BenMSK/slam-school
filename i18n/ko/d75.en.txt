<strong>LiDAR odometry</strong> is yesterday's ICP surrounded by three practical decisions: correct each point for the motion of the sensor during the sweep, decide which points to match and how to find their partners quickly, and register against a <strong>local map</strong> built from many recent scans instead of the previous scan alone. Done well, this drifts by about one metre per kilometre.

2. Motion compensation
@@ 30
A scan is gathered over a sweep of about 0.1 s while the sensor moves, so its points are measured from slightly different positions. Left alone, this gives a distorted cloud; the same effect in cameras is called rolling shutter. <span class="src">§8.2.2.1 · pp. 231–232</span> The fix is called <strong>motion compensation</strong>, undistortion, or <strong>deskewing</strong>.

<strong>Numbers, in 2D.</strong> The sensor drives forward at \(v = 6\) m/s while turning at \(\omega = 90^\circ\)/s, and \(T = 0.1\) s. Take the point measured half-way, \(\tau_k = 0.05\) s, at range 5 m straight ahead: \(\mathbf{p}_k = (5, 0)\). Without compensation the point would sit at \((5, 0)\), wrong by 0.49 m.

3. Features: keep the edges and the planes
@@ 56
A feature-based system does not match every point. It extracts a small number of informative ones and computes correspondences and residuals only for those, which cuts the cost. <span class="src">§8.2.2.2 · pp. 232–233</span> The usual features are points on <strong>edges</strong> and on <strong>planes</strong>. LOAM finds them from a curvature score computed from the differences between a point and its neighbours: high curvature marks an edge, low curvature a plane. <span class="src">§8.2.2.2 · p. 233</span> The second standard tool is principal component analysis of a point's neighbourhood. <span class="src">§8.2.2.2 · pp. 233–234</span>

The eigenvalues of the neighbourhood covariance are the squared extents of the point set along its three principal axes.
@@ 66
For a plane, the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_3\) is the surface <strong>normal</strong>: the direction in which the points vary least. That is where yesterday's \(\mathbf{n}_i\) comes from. The same ellipsoid, flattened into a disk, is a <strong>surfel</strong>. <span class="src">§8.2.2.2 · p. 234</span>
@@ 67
<strong>Example in 2D.</strong> Five points on a wall: \((0, 0),\ (1, 0.02),\ (2, -0.02),\ (3, 0.01),\ (4, -0.01)\). The mean is \((2, 0)\). The variances are \(\tfrac15(4+1+0+1+4) = 2\) along \(x\) and \(\tfrac15(0 + 0.0004 + 0.0004 + 0.0001 + 0.0001) = 0.0002\) along \(y\), with covariance \(\tfrac15(-0.02 + 0.01 - 0.02) = -0.006\). The eigenvalues are close to 2 and 0.0002, a ratio of ten thousand: a clear line (a wall, in 2D), with normal almost exactly along \(y\).

4. Direct methods and fast search
@@ 70
Features have costs. They discard isolated points that are neither edge nor plane, which hurts among bushes and branches. Their detectors need retuning for each sensor. And the processing load still grows with the size of the scan, which becomes a problem for 64- or 128-beam sensors. <span class="src">§8.2.2.3 · p. 234</span> A <strong>direct</strong> method aligns the points themselves, ICP-style, as direct visual methods align pixels (<a href="#d68">Week 12</a>). What held it back was the cost of the correspondence search. <span class="src">§8.2.2.3 · p. 235</span>

Filling the gap: what the search costs
@@ 72
Equation (8.3) needs, for each of \(N\) scan points, the nearest of \(M\) map points. Checking all of them takes \(N \times M\) distance computations per ICP iteration: for \(N = 10^5\) and \(M = 10^6\) that is \(10^{11}\), far too many at 10 Hz. Two data structures fix this (see <a href="#d45">Week 8 · Day 3</a>):
@@ 73
A <strong>k-d tree</strong> splits space recursively, so a query descends about \(\log_2 M \approx 20\) levels: roughly \(N\log_2 M = 2\times10^{6}\) steps instead of \(10^{11}\). The <strong>incremental k-d tree</strong> of Fast-LIO2 can add and remove points and rebalance itself, so the tree is not rebuilt for every scan. <span class="src">§8.2.2.3 · p. 235</span>
@@ 74
A <strong>voxel grid</strong> stores points in a hash table keyed by the integer cell \(\lfloor \mathbf{x}/s \rfloor\) for voxel size \(s\). A query looks only in the cell of the point and its neighbours, a constant amount of work. It may miss the exact nearest neighbour, which the book accepts as an approximation. <span class="src">§8.2.1.2 · p. 230</span>

5. Scan-to-scan or scan-to-map
@@ 76
The first systems registered each scan to the one before it. A single scan is sparse, so many points have no good partner in the previous scan, the registration errors add up, and the map becomes inconsistent. The modern alternative keeps a <strong>local map</strong> around the sensor and registers each scan to it. <span class="src">§8.2.2.4 · p. 235</span>

The starting guess for the fine registration is a motion prediction from scan-to-scan odometry or IMU pre-integration. LOAM interleaved fast scan-to-scan odometry with slower scan-to-map registration; newer systems use a single scan-to-map stage with direct point-wise correspondences in a voxelized local map. Learned LiDAR odometry exists too, with open questions about generalization. <span class="src">§8.2.2.4 · pp. 235–236</span>
@@ 89
Put together, these systems reach drift rates on the order of 1 m per 1000 m travelled, that is 0.1%, depending on the environment and on how dynamic the scene and the sensor motion are. <span class="src">§8.2.3 · p. 236</span> After a 5 km drive that is still about 5 m of error, and it only grows. Removing it is the job of place recognition, tomorrow.
@@ 90
<strong>"Deskewing needs the motion, and the motion is what we are estimating, so it is circular."</strong> It only needs a prediction of the motion inside one sweep, from the previous step or an IMU. The registration then refines the pose between sweeps.
@@ 91
<strong>"More points always give better odometry."</strong> Points that match badly, on moving objects or with no partner in the reference, hurt. Features, down-sampling, and a dense local map all aim at good pairs, not many pairs.

The constant-velocity assumption breaks for high-frequency motion. An IMU measures it directly at a high rate, but its noise, bias, and timing errors can make it worse than simpler methods if handled carelessly.
@@ 113
A LiDAR odometry pipeline deskews the scan, searches correspondences, and estimates the pose, repeating the last two until convergence.
@@ 114
Deskewing moves each point by the sensor motion at its own time stamp: p(start) = T(0)⁻¹ T(τ) p, with the in-sweep motion from a constant-velocity model, a continuous-time trajectory, or an IMU.
@@ 115
Edges and planes are found from curvature or from the eigenvalues of the neighbourhood covariance; for a plane, the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue is the normal.
@@ 116
Direct methods match all points and depend on fast search structures: k-d trees, incremental k-d trees, voxel grids.
@@ 117
Scan-to-map registration against a dense local map gives drift of about 0.1% of distance travelled, which still grows without bound.
