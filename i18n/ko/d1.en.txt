SLAM estimates two things at once, where the robot has been and what the world looks like, because each one is needed to compute the other. Every system in this book does it in two stages: a <strong>front-end</strong> turns raw sensor data into a small set of geometric constraints, and a <strong>back-end</strong> finds the trajectory and map that best satisfy all of them.

1. One problem, two unknowns
@@ 12
A robot in a place it has never seen needs a <strong>map</strong> (where the obstacles, objects, and landmarks are) and its own <strong>pose</strong> (position and orientation) inside that map. <strong>Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM)</strong> is the problem of building the map while estimating the pose with respect to it. <span class="src">§I.1 · p. 3</span>
@@ 13
The difficulty is circular. To place a new observation in the map you need to know where the robot was when it made it. To know where the robot is you need to compare what it sees with the map. If someone hands you one of the two, the problem collapses into an easier one:

In practice "neither" is the normal case. 2. SLAM is an inverse problem
@@ 26
Sensors do not report poses or maps. They report numbers that <em>depend on</em> the poses and the map: a pixel coordinate, a range, an angular rate. Write the unknowns (all poses and all map parameters) as one long vector \(\mathbf{x}\), and each measurement as
@@ 27
where \(h_i\) is a <strong>measurement model</strong> that predicts what the sensor should read for a given \(\mathbf{x}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\eta}_i\) is noise. Going from \(\mathbf{x}\) to \(\mathbf{z}\) is easy: it is geometry. SLAM asks for the reverse: given all the \(\mathbf{z}_i\), find the \(\mathbf{x}\) that could have produced them. The book calls this an <strong>inverse problem</strong>. <span class="src">§I.2 · p. 5</span> You will write this equation properly in Week 2, where it becomes a least-squares problem.
@@ 28
There are two ways to set it up. <strong>Indirect methods</strong> first compress the raw data into a few easy-to-model quantities, such as keypoints in an image, and only model those. <strong>Direct methods</strong> model the raw data itself, such as the brightness of every pixel. Indirect methods are cheaper and lead to simpler math. Direct methods use all the information and can be more accurate, at the cost of heavier computation and a harder optimization problem. <span class="src">§I.2 · pp. 5–6</span>

@@ 59
The front-end is the part that looks. It says things like "I moved about one meter forward" and "that corner is the same one I saw ten minutes ago". The back-end is the part that thinks. It takes all of those statements, which never agree perfectly, and finds the single story of where the robot went that contradicts them the least.

4. Drift, and why loop closures matter
@@ 61
<strong>Odometry</strong> is the estimate of how the robot moved between two nearby moments. Chaining odometry steps gives a trajectory, but every step carries a small error and the errors add up. The accumulated error is called <strong>drift</strong>. A <strong>loop closure</strong> is a measurement that ties the current pose to a pose from much earlier, made when the robot recognizes a place it has visited before. <span class="src">§I.2 · p. 8</span>
@@ 62
Try it: the robot drives a 40-step square and should end exactly where it started. Raise the heading noise, then press "Close the loop".
@@ 63
Notice two things. Heading noise opens a much larger gap than distance noise does. And closing the loop reduces the error along the whole path, not only at the last pose.
@@ 64
Filling the gap: how fast does drift grow?
@@ 65
The book says drift is "bound to" grow but does not say how fast. A one-line model shows it. Suppose each step moves the robot by \(u_k\) and odometry reports \(u_k + \epsilon_k\), with independent errors of variance \(\sigma^2\). After \(n\) steps the position error is the sum \(\epsilon_1 + \dots + \epsilon_n\), and variances of independent errors add:
@@ 66
So pure distance error grows like \(\sqrt{n}\): slowly, but without bound. Heading error is worse. A heading that is off by a small angle \(\theta\) displaces everything that follows sideways, and after driving a further distance \(L\) the position is off by about \(L\,\theta\). Heading errors therefore get multiplied by distance travelled, which is what you saw in the widget: 1° of heading noise per step matters far more than 3% of distance noise.
@@ 67
A loop closure adds one equation that odometry alone can never supply: "pose 40 equals pose 0". The back-end then adjusts every pose a little so that all odometry steps and this new equation are satisfied as well as possible. In the widget, with equally trusted steps, that spreads the end gap evenly over the 40 steps.
@@ 68
<strong>"SLAM is localization plus mapping, run one after the other."</strong> The two are solved together. An error in a pose corrupts the map points seen from it, and the corrupted map then misleads later poses.
@@ 69
<strong>"A loop closure only fixes the pose where it happens."</strong> It is a constraint between two poses, and the back-end redistributes the error along the entire loop between them.
@@ 70
<strong>"Landmark-based SLAM has no loop closures."</strong> It has them implicitly: seeing an old landmark again is a loop closure. <span class="src">§I.2 · p. 8</span>
