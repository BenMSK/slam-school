1. Why a second front-end method
@@ 12
RANSAC needs a small minimal set and a fair share of inliers. Loop closures in a pose graph offer neither: the smallest sample that determines \(N\) poses has \(N-1\) measurements, and perceptual aliasing can make most candidate loop closures wrong. <span class="src">§3.2.2 · p. 81</span> <strong>Pairwise Consistency Maximization (PCM)</strong> does not sample. It looks for the largest set of measurements that are consistent with one another, and the book reports it sorting through sets with upwards of 90% outliers. <span class="src">§3.2.2 · p. 82</span>
@@ 13
On Day 1 you saw that no estimator survives more than 50% arbitrary outliers. PCM does not contradict that. It relies on an assumption: the wrong measurements are wrong in unrelated ways and so rarely agree with each other, while the right ones always do.

<strong>A one-dimensional example.</strong> In a straight corridor a pose is a position, a relative pose is a signed displacement, composing is adding, and the identity is 0. Suppose the measurements are \(T^a_b = +0.1\) m, \(\bar{T}^b_c = +3.0\) m, \(T^c_d = -0.2\) m, \(\bar{T}^d_a = -3.1\) m. Around the cycle: \(0.1 + 3.0 - 0.2 - 3.1 = -0.2\) m. With \(\gamma = 0.5\) m the pair is consistent. Now replace the second loop closure with an aliased one that claims \(T^c_d = +4.0\) m: the sum is \(4.0\) m, and the pair is inconsistent. In practice \(\gamma\) grows with the length of the loop, because a longer odometry chain accumulates more noise (footnote 9, p. 83).

3. From pairs to a graph, from a graph to a clique
@@ 37
PCM asks for the largest set of measurements in which <em>every</em> pair is consistent:
@@ 38
<span class="src">§3.2.2 · p. 83</span> Compare with consensus maximization (3.4): \(\mathbf{x}\) is gone, and all the constraints can be computed once, in advance.
@@ 39
Build the <strong>consistency graph</strong>: one node per putative measurement, and an edge between \(i\) and \(j\) whenever \(F(\mathbf{z}_i, \mathbf{z}_j) \le \gamma\). A <strong>clique</strong> is a set of nodes in which every two are joined by an edge. The <strong>maximum clique</strong> is the largest one. Problem (3.8) is exactly the maximum clique problem on the consistency graph. <span class="src">§3.2.2 · pp. 83–84</span>
@@ 40
<strong>By hand.</strong> Six measurements A–F have these consistent pairs: A–B, A–C, A–D, B–C, B–D, C–D, D–E, D–F, E–F. That is 9 edges out of \(6\times5/2 = 15\) possible pairs. The set {A, B, C, D} is a clique: all six of its pairs are in the list. {D, E, F} is a clique of size 3. Is there a clique of size 5? Every member of one would have at least 4 neighbours, and only D has that many (A, B, C, E, F). So there is none, and the maximum clique is {A, B, C, D}, and PCM keeps those four and rejects E and F. Note that D is consistent with E and F as well. One edge to an outlier does no harm. What counts is membership of the large mutually consistent group.
@@ 41
Try it: ten point matches between two frames. The widget applies (3.5) to all 45 pairs and searches every subset for the maximum clique.
@@ 42
Notice: at the default γ = 0.15, 22 of the 45 pairs are consistent: the 15 pairs among the six true inliers (1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 10) and 7 chance pairs. The chance edges do not form a large clique, so the maximum clique is exactly the six inliers. Lower γ to 0.05 and two inliers drop out because noise breaks some of their edges. Raise γ to 0.50 and outlier 7 joins the clique. Back at γ = 0.15, set 7 outliers: the three inliers are still found, with 70% outliers.

4. What it costs
@@ 44
The four steps of PCM are: choose \(F\); evaluate it on every pair and build the graph; find the maximum clique; return its measurements. <span class="src">§3.2.2 · p. 84</span>
@@ 45
Filling the gap: counting the work
@@ 46
<strong>Building the graph is cheap.</strong> \(N\) measurements have \(N(N-1)/2\) pairs: 45 for \(N = 10\), 499 500 for \(N = 1000\). Each test is a few arithmetic operations.
@@ 47
<strong>Finding the clique is the hard part.</strong> The widget checks all \(2^{10} = 1024\) subsets. That brute force doubles with every added measurement: \(N = 60\) already gives about \(10^{18}\) subsets. The maximum clique problem is NP-hard, and it is also hard to approximate. <span class="src">§3.2.2 · p. 84</span> In practice two families of algorithms are used:
@@ 48
<strong>Exact</strong> algorithms, usually branch and bound. They explore subsets but discard whole branches that cannot beat the best clique found so far. For example, a node with only 3 neighbours cannot belong to a clique larger than 4, so it is dropped once a clique of size 4 is known. Worst-case time is still exponential.
@@ 49
<strong>Heuristic</strong> algorithms, which are faster and may return a clique that is large but not the largest.
@@ 50
The density of the graph decides which is practical. Recent exact solvers find maximum cliques quickly in large sparse graphs. Dense graphs, where many pairs are consistent, are slow for exact solvers. <span class="src">§3.2.2 · pp. 84–85</span>

Both tests are <strong>necessary but not sufficient</strong> for a measurement to be an inlier (footnote 11, p. 85). A pairwise test can be too permissive. Here is a concrete case for (3.5): a mirror image. If the points at time \(k\) were a reflection of the points at \(k-1\), every pairwise distance would be preserved and every pair would pass, yet no rotation and translation maps one set to the other. A minimal solver would notice. Pairwise distances cannot. The book makes the general point that (3.5) may accept some outliers that RANSAC would reject. <span class="src">§3.2.2 · p. 85</span>
@@ 71
The quality of PCM rests on the choice of \(F\). The useless function \(F \equiv 0\) makes every pair consistent, the graph complete, and the maximum clique the whole set: nothing is rejected. A function that only inliers can pass rejects every outlier. <span class="src">§3.2.2 · p. 85</span> Real functions sit in between, and whatever passes goes on to a back-end that must cope with the rest. That is tomorrow.
@@ 72
<strong>"A measurement with an edge to an inlier is an inlier."</strong> Edges to single inliers happen by chance, and more often as γ grows. Only membership of the maximum clique counts.
@@ 73
<strong>"An inconsistent pair tells you which measurement is wrong."</strong> It says the two cannot both be right. The graph as a whole decides.
@@ 74
<strong>"PCM beats the 50% limit, so it is strictly stronger than RANSAC."</strong> It works when outliers are mutually inconsistent. Outliers that agree with each other, such as many false loop closures between the same two look-alike corridors, can form the largest clique.
