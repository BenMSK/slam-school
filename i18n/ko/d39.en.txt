Filling the gap: the implicit function theorem
@@ 16
The step "remembering that \(x\) depends on \(y\)" assumes a differentiable function \(x(y)\) exists. The <strong>implicit function theorem</strong> says when. Let \(F:\R^n\times\R^m\to\R^n\) be continuously differentiable with \(F(\mathbf{x}_0,\mathbf{y}_0) = \mathbf{0}\). If the \(n\times n\) matrix \(\partial F/\partial\mathbf{x}\) is invertible at \((\mathbf{x}_0,\mathbf{y}_0)\), then near \(\mathbf{y}_0\) there is exactly one continuously differentiable function \(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{y})\) with \(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{y}_0)=\mathbf{x}_0\) and \(F(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}\), and
@@ 17
The formula is what you get by differentiating \(F(\mathbf{x}(\mathbf{y}),\mathbf{y}) = \mathbf{0}\) with the chain rule, \(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}}{\partial\mathbf{y}} + \frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{0}\), and solving. The theorem is local: it describes the solution branch through \((\mathbf{x}_0,\mathbf{y}_0)\), nothing more.

Picture the lower-level cost as a valley whose floor shifts when \(\mathbf{y}\) changes. \(\mathbf{H}_{xy}\) says how hard a change in \(\mathbf{y}\) pushes the ball sideways (it is the change in the slope \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{x}\)). \(\mathbf{H}\) is the stiffness of the valley. Displacement = push divided by stiffness: a stiff, well-constrained SLAM problem moves little when a measurement changes; a floppy one moves a lot.
@@ 27
The theorem's condition matters: \(\mathbf{H}\) must be invertible. At a strict minimum it is positive definite, so this holds. If the lower-level problem has directions that the measurements do not constrain at all, \(\mathbf{H}\) is singular, the minimizer is not unique, and "the derivative of the solution" has no meaning until those directions are fixed.

So define \(\mathbf{q}\) as the solution of \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{v}\), with \(\mathbf{v} = (\partial\mathcal{U}/\partial\mathbf{x}^*)\T\). (The Hessian is symmetric, so \(\mathbf{H}\T = \mathbf{H}\).) This is the same trick as reverse mode on Day 1: push the single output's sensitivity backward instead of pushing every parameter's influence forward. The vector \(\mathbf{q}\) is called the <strong>adjoint</strong>, and the procedure the <strong>adjoint method</strong>.
@@ 37
Even one solve can be too much if \(\mathbf{H}\) must be stored. In Example 4.3, a model with \(10^6\) parameters as 32-bit floats takes \(10^6\times 4\) bytes \(= 4\) MB, and its Hessian \((10^6)^2\times 4\) bytes \(= 4\) TB. <span class="src">Example 4.3 · p. 107</span> The way out is to solve \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{v}\) with a method that only ever asks for products \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{q}\).

Filling the gap: three steps the book states without proof
@@ 44
<strong>Why minimizing Q solves the system.</strong> \(\partial Q/\partial\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{v}\) (using the symmetry of \(\mathbf{H}\)). At a minimum the gradient is zero, so \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{v}\). With \(\mathbf{H}\) positive definite, \(Q\) is a bowl with exactly one minimum.
@@ 45
<strong>Why (4.15) holds.</strong> Hold \(\mathbf{q}\) constant. The scalar \(s(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{q} = \sum_j \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_j}q_j\) has \(i\)-th partial derivative \(\sum_j \frac{\partial^2\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}q_j\), which is the \(i\)-th entry of \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{q}\). In practice: one reverse pass for the gradient, a dot product with \(\mathbf{q}\), and one more reverse pass through that computation. The cost is a small multiple of one gradient, and the memory is that of vectors.
@@ 46
<strong>When (4.16) converges.</strong> Subtract the exact solution \(\mathbf{q}^*\): the error obeys \(\mathbf{q}_k - \mathbf{q}^* = (\mathbf{I} - \eta\mathbf{H})(\mathbf{q}_{k-1} - \mathbf{q}^*)\). Along an eigenvector of \(\mathbf{H}\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) the error is multiplied by \(1 - \eta\lambda\) each step, so it shrinks if and only if \(|1-\eta\lambda| \lt 1\) for every eigenvalue, that is \(0 \lt \eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}\). If the eigenvalues are spread widely (an ill-conditioned Hessian), the small ones converge slowly; conjugate gradient copes with that much better.

Going deeper (optional): what a SLAM back-end already has
@@ 50
For a least-squares cost \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\norm{\mathbf{r}}^2\) the Hessian is \(\mathbf{J}\T\mathbf{J}\) plus a term that multiplies the residuals by their second derivatives. Gauss-Newton drops the second term. A back-end that has just converged holds \(\mathbf{J}\T\mathbf{J}\) and usually a factorization of it, so an approximate adjoint solve \(\mathbf{J}\T\mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{q} = \mathbf{v}\) is nearly free. In the toy problem \(\mathbf{J}\T\mathbf{J} = 4x^{*2}+1 = 8.464\) against the exact \(H = 8.196\); the slope would come out as \(2.732/8.464 = 0.323\) where the exact value is 0.333. The approximation is good when the residuals at the solution are small.
