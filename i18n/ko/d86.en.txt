An event stream is not a fast video. It is sparse, it has no frames, each event is a single bit, and the same scene produces different events when the camera moves differently. Algorithms built for images therefore do not apply as they are. The book sorts the new algorithms along three independent axes: how many events are processed at once, whether the method is designed or learned, and whether it works on features or on all events.

<strong>Step 3: combine.</strong>
@@ 33
Read three facts off this line.
@@ 34
<strong>Rate.</strong> The time between two events at a pixel is \(\Delta t \approx C / |\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v}|\), so the pixel fires about \(|\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v}| / C\) events per second. Strong edges and fast motion give many events.
@@ 35
<strong>Polarity.</strong> \(p = \operatorname{sign}(-\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v})\). Reverse the motion and every ON becomes an OFF.
@@ 36
<strong>Blind direction.</strong> If \(\mathbf{v}\) is perpendicular to \(\nabla L\), the motion runs along the edge, \(\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\), and no event fires. Only the component of \(\mathbf{v}\) along the gradient is seen.

<strong>A check against Day 1.</strong> In the Day 1 simulator (\(v = 0.5\) px/ms, \(C = 0.3\)) the first two ON events of a pixel arrive 0.41 ms apart. The model says the edge is that steep there: \(|\partial L/\partial x| \approx C / (v\,\Delta t) = 0.3 / (0.5 \times 0.41) = 1.46\) per pixel. The exact slope of the simulated edge at that point is 1.49. Two timestamps were enough to measure a local image gradient.
@@ 49
<strong>A 2-D example.</strong> A vertical edge has \(\nabla L = (0.5, 0)\) per pixel, and the image moves with \(\mathbf{v} = (-200, 100)\) px/s. Then \(-\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v} = -(0.5 \times (-200) + 0 \times 100) = +100\) per second. With \(C = 0.25\) each pixel on the edge fires \(100/0.25 = 400\) ON events per second, one every 2.5 ms. The vertical part of the motion, 100 px/s, contributes nothing.
@@ 50
Run a fingertip over a ruler with raised marks. Crossing the marks, you feel each one. Sliding along a mark, you feel nothing, however fast you go. An event camera feels the image the same way, which is why the book calls a moving event camera an asynchronous edge detector. <span class="src">§10.4 · p. 289</span>

3. The questions this raises
@@ 52
The challenges turn into four research questions: how to extract information from events for pose and depth estimation, which map and trajectory representations fit data that is nearly continuous in time and sparse in space, how to find correspondences when the data depends on motion, and how to do all of it without rebuilding the bottlenecks of frame-based processing. <span class="src">§10.2 · p. 287</span>
@@ 53
Event-based SLAM has grown together with two neighbouring tasks. <strong>Image reconstruction</strong> recovers absolute intensity from events; by the model above, the events hold samples of \(\nabla L \cdot \mathbf{v}\), so with a known motion they constrain the image, and with a known image they constrain the motion. <strong>Optical flow estimation</strong> recovers the image velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) itself. The first event-based SLAM systems already estimated an intensity image along with the motion. <span class="src">§10.2 · p. 287</span>

@@ 69
<strong>Choosing the packet size.</strong> The book lists three kinds of rule: a fixed number of events, a fixed duration, and hybrid criteria. Day 1 tells you what each does, because the event rate follows the motion. Take a packet of 20,000 events. If the camera moves slowly and produces 100,000 events per second, the packet spans 200 ms. If it moves fast and produces 2,000,000 per second, the packet spans 10 ms. A fixed count therefore updates more often exactly when the motion is fast, but waits indefinitely when nothing moves. A fixed duration of 10 ms gives a predictable update rate, but the slow case then has only 1,000 events to work with.

5. Data association, and the road so far
@@ 71
<strong>Data association</strong> means deciding which events belong to the same scene point or feature. It is a central problem, because single events carry no descriptor to match. The usual answer exploits the high temporal resolution: events that are close in time and close in pixel position are taken to come from the same edge. Both <strong>hard association</strong> (each event is assigned to one thing) and <strong>soft association</strong> (an event contributes to several things with weights) have been used. <span class="src">§10.3 · pp. 287–288</span>
@@ 72
The field approached the full problem by raising the difficulty step by step along several axes: the number of unknowns (degrees of freedom), the type of motion (from pure rotation or planar scenes to full 6-DoF motion), the scene (from simple high-contrast patterns to natural texture), and whether the scene is static or contains independently moving objects. Early work was model-based; more recent work also explores deep learning. Because events come from moving edges, the maps these systems recover are usually edge maps: sparse or semi-dense 3D points. <span class="src">§10.3 · pp. 288–289, Figure 10.3</span>
