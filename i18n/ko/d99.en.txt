1. Building \(\mathbf{M}\)
@@ 13
Day 2 gave the measurement error at frame \(k\): \(\tilde{\mathbf{z}}_k \simeq \mathbf{H}_{x_k}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k\) plus noise, where \(\mathbf{H}_{x_k}\) stacks the Jacobians of every feature seen at that frame. Day 1 gave the propagation of an initial error without noise: \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_k = \boldsymbol{\Phi}_{(k,1)}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_1\). Substitute one into the other and stack over frames. This is equation (11.50): <span class="src">§11.3.2 · p. 324</span>
@@ 14
It is the matrix of <a href="#d97">Day 1</a>, with two differences. \(\mathbf{H}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) now change from frame to frame, and both depend on the linearization point, which is why the book writes \(\mathbf{M}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\). The <strong>unobservable subspace</strong> \(\mathcal{U}\) is the nullspace of \(\mathbf{M}\): all \(\mathbf{u}\) with \(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\). (The book says the system is fully observable when the nullspace "is empty"; strictly, a nullspace always contains \(\mathbf{0}\), and the meaning is that it contains nothing else.)

<strong>\(\mathbf{u}_{2:4}\) is a shift.</strong> Each of its three columns adds the same unit vector to the robot position and to every feature, and touches nothing else.
@@ 32
<strong>\(\mathbf{u}_1\) is a rotation about gravity.</strong> Rotate the world by a small angle \(\varepsilon\) about the axis \(\mathbf{g}\) (rotation vector \(\varepsilon\mathbf{g}\)). Any world vector \(\mathbf{a}\) becomes \(\mathbf{a} + \varepsilon\,\mathbf{g}\times\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{a} - \varepsilon\,\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{g}\). That is the entry \(-\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{g}\) for \(\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_f\). The body orientation picks up the same rotation, written in body coordinates: \(\mathbf{R}^{c_1}_w\mathbf{g}^w\). The biases live in the body frame and do not change. A rotation about the gravity axis is a change of <strong>yaw</strong>.

3. Compute it: the observability lab
@@ 46
The lab is a 2-D visual-inertial system small enough to compute in your browser. A robot carries one gyroscope, a two-axis accelerometer, and a camera that measures the bearing (optionally also the range) to three landmarks, nine times in four seconds. The unknowns are the initial heading, gyro bias, velocity, accelerometer bias, position, and the landmark positions: 14 numbers, in the book's order. The widget differentiates all camera readings with respect to those 14 numbers while the IMU readings are held fixed. That derivative is \(\mathbf{M}\). Then it takes the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{M}\T\mathbf{M}\).

4. Unobservable, but harmless: gauge freedom
@@ 60
The book stresses that these four directions are not a defect. No measurement refers to a global frame: the IMU measures motion relative to the body, the camera measures landmarks relative to the body, and the landmarks were never surveyed. The one exception is gravity, which the accelerometer senses and which ties down roll and pitch. So position and yaw are not unknown facts about the world; they are a choice of world frame that is ours to make. <span class="src">§11.3.2 · p. 325</span>
@@ 61
This is the <strong>gauge freedom</strong> you met for bundle adjustment in <a href="#d70">Week 12 · Day 4</a>: a family of solutions that all explain the data equally well. Observability analysis finds the family. Fixing the gauge picks one member, for example by declaring the first position to be the origin with zero yaw, or by adding a prior factor on the first state. That adds a convention, not information: relative quantities such as the distance travelled or the angle turned come out the same in every gauge.

@@ 76
<strong>"Four unobservable directions means four state variables are unknown."</strong> Each direction is a combination that moves the robot and every landmark together. No single variable is to blame.
@@ 77
<strong>"Yaw is unobservable, so a VIO cannot track heading."</strong> Changes of heading are observable, through the gyroscope and the landmarks. Only the heading of the starting frame is free.
@@ 78
<strong>"More landmarks will fix it."</strong> Every landmark at an unknown position adds three unknowns and moves with the gauge. The nullspace stays four-dimensional. A landmark at a <em>known</em> position would help.

Q1. A visual-inertial system tracks 50 point landmarks at unknown positions with generic motion. What is the dimension of the nullspace of \(\mathbf{M}\)?
@@ 80
Three for global position and one for yaw, whatever the number of unknown landmarks.

Q2. A feature is at \(\mathbf{p}^w_f = (2, 0, 1)\) and gravity is \(\mathbf{g}^w = (0, 0, -9.81)\). What is the feature block \(-\mathbf{p}^w_f\times\mathbf{g}^w\) of \(\mathbf{u}_1\)?
@@ 82
\(\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{g} = (p_y g_z - p_z g_y,\ p_z g_x - p_x g_z,\ p_x g_y - p_y g_x) = (0,\ 0 - 2\cdot(-9.81),\ 0) = (0, 19.62, 0)\), and the block is its negative. A point on the \(x\) axis moves along \(-y\): a turn about the downward axis. The height of the point plays no role.

Q3. A numerically computed \(\mathbf{M}\) has 27 rows and 14 columns, and \(\mathbf{M}\T\mathbf{M}\) has 11 non-zero eigenvalues. How many independent unobservable directions are there?
@@ 84
The rank is 11 and there are 14 unknowns, so the nullspace has dimension \(14 - 11 = 3\). The number of rows does not matter once it exceeds the rank.
