A learned front-end produces the measurements, and the back-end turns them into the estimate \(\mathbf{x}^*\). To train the front-end on how good the <em>final</em> estimate is, you need to know how \(\mathbf{x}^*\) moves when the front-end's parameters \(\mathbf{y}\) move. That is the Jacobian \(\partial\mathbf{x}^*/\partial\mathbf{y}\) of an <em>arg min</em>, and the whole chapter is about computing it.

1. From a one-way pipeline to a loop
@@ 12
In <a href="#d1">Week 1 · Day 1</a> the front-end handed pseudo-measurements to the back-end and never heard back. Neural networks now do parts of the front-end well, such as feature detection, matching, and frame-to-frame motion estimation, while geometric optimization remains the tool that makes the result globally consistent. <span class="src">Ch. 4 intro · p. 101</span> You could train the network on its own task (say, matching accuracy), freeze it, and plug it in. But the network would then never learn which of its mistakes actually hurt the SLAM estimate.
@@ 13
The alternative is to let the back-end send a training signal to the front-end. For that, the error measured <em>after</em> the back-end must be turned into a gradient with respect to the network's weights, and the gradient has to pass through the optimizer on the way. <span class="src">Ch. 4 intro · pp. 101–102</span>

2. Recap: the back-end is a nonlinear least squares problem
@@ 26
The back-end solves a <strong>nonlinear least squares (NLS)</strong> problem: <span class="src">§4.1 · p. 102</span>
@@ 27
Each <strong>residual</strong> \(\mathbf{r}_i\) depends on a small subset \(\mathbf{x}_i\) of the variables (one factor of the factor graph) and is written as a scalar weight \(w_i\) times a vector cost \(\mathbf{c}_i\). The solver is the one from <a href="#d10">Week 2 · Day 4</a>: linearize, solve the normal equations for a step \(\delta\mathbf{x}\), update, repeat. With \(\mathbf{J}_i = \partial\mathbf{r}_i/\partial\mathbf{x}\), <strong>Gauss-Newton (GN)</strong> uses \(\sum_i \mathbf{J}_i\T\mathbf{J}_i\) as an approximation of the Hessian, and Levenberg-Marquardt and dogleg modify the step to make it safer. <span class="src">§4.1 · pp. 102–103</span>

3. Two nested problems: bilevel optimization
@@ 36
Call the network's parameters \(\mathbf{y} \in \R^m\) and the back-end's variables \(\mathbf{x} \in \R^n\). Training the network on the back-end's result is a <strong>bilevel optimization (BLO)</strong> problem: <span class="src">§4.2 · p. 103</span>
@@ 37
\(\mathcal{L}\) is the <strong>lower-level (LL) cost</strong>: the SLAM objective, minimized over \(\mathbf{x}\) with \(\mathbf{y}\) held fixed. \(\mathcal{U}\) is the <strong>upper-level (UL) cost</strong>: the training loss, minimized over \(\mathbf{y}\), and it is evaluated at the solution \(\mathbf{x}^*\) of the lower level. The constraint "s.t." is itself an optimization problem, which is what "bilevel" means. The variables \(\mathbf{x}\) usually have a physical meaning (poses, landmarks); the variables \(\mathbf{y}\) usually do not (network weights). <span class="src">§4.2 · p. 103</span>

To improve, the cook needs to know: "if I had cut the onions a little finer, how would the chef's best dish have changed, and would the critic have liked it more?" The chef's reaction to a small change in the ingredients is \(\partial\mathbf{x}^*/\partial\mathbf{y}\).

Filling the gap: shapes, and which term is hard
@@ 56
\(\mathcal{U}\) is a scalar, \(\mathbf{y}\) has \(m\) entries, \(\mathbf{x}\) has \(n\). With gradients written as rows:
@@ 57
\(\partial\mathcal{U}/\partial\mathbf{y}\) is \(1\times m\): the <strong>direct</strong> dependence, with \(\mathbf{x}^*\) treated as a constant. Ordinary backpropagation gives it.
@@ 58
\(\partial\mathcal{U}/\partial\mathbf{x}^*\) is \(1\times n\): also ordinary.
@@ 59
\(\partial\mathbf{x}^*/\partial\mathbf{y}\) is \(n\times m\): entry \((j,k)\) says how much the \(j\)-th solution variable moves per unit change of the \(k\)-th parameter. This is the <strong>indirect</strong> part and the hard one, because \(\mathbf{x}^*\) is defined as "the result of running a solver", not by a formula.
@@ 60

5. The tool underneath: automatic differentiation
@@ 63
Both routes rest on <strong>automatic differentiation (AutoDiff)</strong>. Page 105 mentions that it comes in a forward mode and a reverse mode and leaves the details to the libraries. Here they are, because the costs of the two modes decide everything tomorrow.
@@ 64
A program computes its output through a sequence of elementary operations (add, multiply, square, sin, …), each with a derivative you know. AutoDiff applies the chain rule to that sequence with numbers, not symbols. It is exact up to rounding: it is neither a finite-difference approximation nor a symbolic manipulation of formulas.
@@ 65
Filling the gap: the two modes
@@ 66
Write the program as intermediate values \(v_1, v_2, \dots\), each computed from earlier ones.
@@ 67
<strong>Forward mode</strong> picks one input, say \(x\), and carries next to every value its <strong>tangent</strong> \(\dot v = \partial v/\partial x\). The inputs are seeded with \(\dot x = 1\) and 0 for the others, and each operation updates the tangent with its own derivative rule: if \(v = a\,b\) then \(\dot v = \dot a\, b + a\, \dot b\). One pass, done together with the evaluation, gives the derivative of <em>every output</em> with respect to <em>that one input</em>.
@@ 68
<strong>Reverse mode</strong> first runs the program and stores every intermediate value. Then it walks the operations backward and carries for every value its <strong>adjoint</strong> \(\bar v = \partial(\text{output})/\partial v\). The output is seeded with 1, and each node passes its adjoint to the values it was computed from: if \(v = a\,b\) then \(\bar a\) receives \(\bar v\, b\) and \(\bar b\) receives \(\bar v\, a\). A value used in several places adds up what it receives. One backward pass gives the derivative of <em>that one output</em> with respect to <em>every input</em>. Applied to neural networks this is backpropagation.

@@ 92
<strong>"U does not contain y, so its gradient with respect to y is zero."</strong> Only the direct term of (4.3) is zero. \(\mathcal{U}\) depends on \(\mathbf{y}\) through \(\mathbf{x}^*(\mathbf{y})\), and that indirect term is usually the whole signal.
@@ 93
<strong>"AutoDiff is finite differences done by a library."</strong> Finite differences evaluate the function at shifted inputs and have truncation error. AutoDiff propagates exact derivative rules through the same operations and has none.
@@ 94
<strong>"Reverse mode is always the faster one."</strong> It needs one pass per output and stores the whole computation. For a function with few inputs and many outputs, forward mode is cheaper.
