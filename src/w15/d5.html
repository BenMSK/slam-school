Today's big idea

A young field moves at the speed of its data and its yardsticks. Simulators give unlimited events with perfect ground truth, provided their noise is realistic. Datasets give real events with measured ground truth. Metrics compress the comparison of two trajectories into a number: ATE asks how far the whole path is off, RPE asks how wrong each step is.

1. Simulators

Event cameras are new and event data is scarce, which hurts learning-based methods most. Simulators fill the gap. §10.7.1 · pp. 295–297

Filling the gap: from brightness samples to event timestamps

The book says what simulators do but not how a sample of brightness becomes an event with a microsecond timestamp. The standard construction is short.

A simulator knows a pixel's log brightness only at sample times: \(L_a\) at \(t_a\) and \(L_b\) at \(t_b\). Let \(L_\text{ref}\) be the level the pixel memorized at its last event. Assume \(L\) changes linearly between the two samples. If it rises, it crosses the levels \(L_\text{ref} + jC\) for \(j = 1, 2, \dots\) as long as they do not exceed \(L_b\), and the crossing times follow from similar triangles:

\[ t_j = t_a + \frac{L_\text{ref} + jC - L_a}{L_b - L_a}\,(t_b - t_a). \]

Each crossing is one ON event. For a falling signal use \(L_\text{ref} - jC\) and emit OFF events. Afterwards \(L_\text{ref}\) is the last level crossed. This is equation (10.1) applied between samples, and it is what the simulator behind this week's widgets does every 0.05 ms.

Example. \(L_\text{ref} = L_a = -1.0\) at \(t_a = 0\), \(L_b = -0.2\) at \(t_b = 1\) ms, \(C = 0.3\). The levels \(-0.7\) and \(-0.4\) are crossed; \(-0.1\) is not reached. So two ON events fire, at

\[ t_1 = \frac{0.3}{0.8} \times 1\ \text{ms} = 0.375\ \text{ms}, \qquad t_2 = \frac{0.6}{0.8} \times 1\ \text{ms} = 0.75\ \text{ms}, \]

and the memory ends at \(-0.4\), with \(0.2\) of change carried over to the next interval. Thresholding the frame difference would report one event at \(t_b\).

Why the sampling must adapt. The linear assumption fails if too much happens between samples. A thin bright line that crosses a pixel entirely between \(t_a\) and \(t_b\) leaves \(L_a = L_b\), and the simulator emits nothing. A reasonable rule is to sample often enough that the image moves at most about one pixel between samples. An edge moving at 2000 px/s then needs a sample every 0.5 ms, while at 20 px/s one every 50 ms is enough. A renderer can produce frames at any time, so ESIM can adapt. A recorded 30 Hz video cannot: at 2000 px/s the image jumps 67 pixels between frames. That is why Vid2E and V2E first interpolate intermediate frames, and why the book says their number needs care: too few causes aliasing, too many costs computation. §10.7.1 · p. 296

Noise. An algorithm tuned on perfect simulated events may fail on real ones; this is the simulation-to-real gap. ESIM and its descendants use a simple noise model taken from measurements: the contrast threshold is not fixed but drawn from a normal distribution \(\N(C, \sigma_C^2)\) at each simulation step, with separate thresholds \(C^+\) and \(C^-\) for the two polarities. §10.7.1 · p. 296 To see the effect, take the bar of Day 1, whose edge changes \(L\) by \(\ln 10 = 2.30\). With \(C = 0.30\) a pixel fires 7 events. If its threshold happens to be \(0.27\) it fires \(\lfloor 2.30/0.27 \rfloor = 8\), and with \(0.33\) it fires 6. Neighbouring pixels no longer agree, which is what real data looks like.

V2E goes further and models the pixel circuit, and V2CE targets the timestamps: earlier video-based simulators emit events at discrete times, which differs from real data enough to matter for methods that are sensitive to timing. §10.7.1 · p. 297

3. Metrics

Ideally localization and mapping would be graded separately. In practice the two are intertwined, since depth errors cause pose errors and the reverse, and ground-truth poses are far easier to obtain than ground-truth depth. So the quality of the map is usually judged through the trajectory. Event-based SLAM inherits its two main metrics from classical SLAM (Week 11 · Day 1). §10.7.3 · p. 301

  • The absolute trajectory error (ATE) compares estimated and true poses in one fixed world frame. It grades long-term, global accuracy.
  • The relative pose error (RPE) compares the estimated and true motion between nearby time steps. It grades local consistency.

In both, the translational part is a Euclidean distance between positions and the rotational part is the geodesic distance in \(\SO(3)\). §10.7.3 · p. 301

Filling the gap: the formulas

The book describes the metrics in words. Let \(\mathbf{T}^\text{gt}_i\) and \(\mathbf{T}^\text{est}_i\) in \(\SE(3)\) be the true and estimated poses at the same \(n\) timestamps, with positions \(\mathbf{t}^\text{gt}_i\) and \(\mathbf{t}^\text{est}_i\).

ATE. The two trajectories usually live in different world frames, so first find the rigid transform \(\mathbf{S}\) that best maps the estimate onto the ground truth in the least-squares sense (the closed-form alignment of Week 13 · Day 2). Then take the root mean square of what remains:

\[ \text{ATE} = \sqrt{ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \norm{ \mathbf{t}^\text{gt}_i - \mathbf{S}\,\mathbf{t}^\text{est}_i }^2 } . \]

RPE. Choose an interval of \(\Delta\) steps. Form the relative motions \(\Delta\mathbf{T}^\text{gt}_i = (\mathbf{T}^\text{gt}_i)^{-1}\mathbf{T}^\text{gt}_{i+\Delta}\) and \(\Delta\mathbf{T}^\text{est}_i = (\mathbf{T}^\text{est}_i)^{-1}\mathbf{T}^\text{est}_{i+\Delta}\), and the error

\[ \mathbf{E}_i = \left( \Delta\mathbf{T}^\text{gt}_i \right)^{-1} \Delta\mathbf{T}^\text{est}_i . \]

If the estimated step equals the true step, \(\mathbf{E}_i\) is the identity. The translational RPE is the root mean square of the lengths of the translation parts of the \(\mathbf{E}_i\); the rotational RPE uses the angles of their rotation parts. No alignment is needed, because relative motions do not depend on the world frame.

Geodesic distance in \(\SO(3)\). The distance between rotations \(\mathbf{R}_1\) and \(\mathbf{R}_2\) is the angle of the rotation that takes one to the other:

\[ d(\mathbf{R}_1, \mathbf{R}_2) = \norm{ \Log\!\left( \mathbf{R}_1\T \mathbf{R}_2 \right) } = \arccos\!\left( \frac{\tr\!\left(\mathbf{R}_1\T\mathbf{R}_2\right) - 1}{2} \right), \]

since a rotation by angle \(\theta\) about any axis has trace \(1 + 2\cos\theta\). Example: if \(\tr(\mathbf{R}_1\T\mathbf{R}_2) = 2.9696\), then \(\cos\theta = 0.9848\) and the two orientations are \(10^\circ\) apart.

Try it: the estimate below makes a single wrong turn. Move the step at which it happens.

Notice that the RPE is 0.028 steps wherever the wrong turn happens: one bad step in 40 is the same local error anywhere (its size is the chord \(2\sin 5^\circ = 0.174\), and \(0.174/\sqrt{40} = 0.028\)). The ATE is 0.29 at step 8, 0.69 at step 20, and 0.03 at step 39. A turn near either end rotates almost the whole path rigidly, and the alignment removes that; a turn in the middle bends the path, which no rigid alignment can undo. Without alignment the end point is off by 4.47 steps at step 8.

Some authors divide the position error by the mean scene depth or by the distance travelled, so that results from scenes of different size can be compared: an ATE of 0.20 m on a 50 m path is 0.4% of the distance. Further variants average the relative position and rotation errors, and for event cameras the errors in linear and angular velocity are also informative, since fast and abrupt motions are what the sensor is for. §10.7.3 · pp. 301–302

When depth is graded separately, the usual measures are the average depth error up to several cutoff distances, the root mean square distance from each estimated 3D point to the closest ground-truth surface (the preferred one), the relative error, and completion, which is the number of points recovered. §10.7.3 · p. 302

4. Open problems

The book closes with what is unsolved. §10.8 · p. 302

  • The processor does not match the sensor. The camera is asynchronous, yet most systems run on ordinary serial processors. A fully neuromorphic system would pair the event camera with asynchronous, spike-based processors and controllers, and is expected to do better in power consumption and latency. Building one means designing sensor, processor, and algorithm together, which takes expertise from several disciplines.
  • New sensors. Hybrid sensors deliver events and intensity, aligned in space and time. Foveated sensors, like the eye, keep high resolution only in part of the image to save bandwidth.
  • Fusion. Combining event cameras with frame cameras, structured light, LiDAR, or radar leaves much to explore.

Common mistakes

  • "A low RPE means the trajectory is globally accurate." Small errors at every step can add up to a large drift. RPE grades the steps, ATE grades the sum.
  • "ATE is the distance between the two trajectories as given." Without the alignment you would mostly measure the arbitrary choice of world frame.
  • "Simulated events are as good as real ones." Only as far as the noise and timing models are right. That is the reason V2E and V2CE exist.

Check yourself

Q1. A simulated pixel has just fired, so its memory equals its current level, L = 0 at t = 0. The next sample is L = 1.0 at t = 4 ms. With C = 0.4 and linear interpolation, which events does it emit?

The levels 0.4 and 0.8 are crossed; 1.2 is not reached. On a line from 0 to 1.0 over 4 ms they are crossed at 0.4 × 4 = 1.6 ms and 0.8 × 4 = 3.2 ms.

Q2. An odometry system estimates every single step accurately but has no loop closure and runs for an hour. What do you expect?

RPE looks at short intervals and stays small. The small step errors accumulate as drift, which the ATE measures in a fixed world frame.

Q3. The product of the transposed true rotation and the estimated rotation has trace 2.0. What is the rotational error?

cos θ = (2.0 − 1)/2 = 0.5, so θ = 60°.

Q4. You want to evaluate stereo depth from events on a car, at night, against ground-truth depth maps. Which dataset in the table fits best?

DSEC was recorded from a car with two event cameras, in day, night, and direct sunlight, and provides ground-truth depth maps. ECDS and EDS are hand-held with a single event camera, and UZH-FPV is a racing drone.

Recap

  1. A simulator turns brightness samples into events by finding where the interpolated log brightness crosses levels spaced \(C\) apart. Sampling must be dense when the image moves fast.
  2. Realism needs noise: at the least a randomized threshold \(\N(C, \sigma_C^2)\), and in V2E a model of the pixel circuit.
  3. Datasets differ in platform, resolution, and ground truth. Poses are common; ground-truth depth is rarer.
  4. ATE: align the estimate to the ground truth, then take the root mean square position error. RPE: compare relative motions over a short interval. ATE grades global accuracy, RPE local consistency.
  5. Open problems: asynchronous (neuromorphic) processing to match the sensor, hybrid and foveated sensors, and fusion with other sensors.
