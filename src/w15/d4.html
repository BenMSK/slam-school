The back-end, and contrast maximization as an optimization problem

Today's big idea

Bundle adjustment for events is young. Indirect back-ends reuse the reprojection error of classical visual SLAM. Direct back-ends optimize either how well the events align (contrast maximization) or a brightness error for every event. Contrast maximization deserves a close look, because it turns the vague goal "the picture should be sharp" into a function of the motion that an optimizer can climb.

1. Why back-ends are rare here

A back-end improves the agreement between all the unknowns and all the data, and stops errors from passing back and forth between tracking and mapping. In frame-based visual SLAM that job is done by bundle adjustment (BA), which you studied in Week 12 · Day 3. §10.5 · p. 293

Most event-based systems have no such step. They run parallel tracking and mapping: a tracking module estimates the pose assuming the map is right, a mapping module updates the map assuming the poses are right, and each uses the other's latest output. The book's judgement is that these systems chose simplicity and low latency over accuracy and robustness. A joint refinement is hard for events for three reasons: the data is noisy and depends on the motion, the unknowns are many and correlated so the search space is large, and the optimization is costly and has local minima. §10.5 · p. 293

2. Indirect and direct back-ends

The back-end must fit the front-end that feeds it, so the book sorts back-ends the same way. §10.5 · pp. 293–294

The book's evidence that events hold much more than corners is image reconstruction: whole intensity images can be recovered from events alone. §10.5 · p. 293

3. Contrast maximization, step by step

The book describes contrast maximization in one paragraph (p. 292) and names it as one of the two event-only back-end objectives (p. 294). It gives no formula. Here is the method in full for the simplest case.

Filling the gap: warp, accumulate, score

The unknown. A vector of motion parameters \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\). In the simplest case the whole image slides with one constant velocity, and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is that velocity in pixels per millisecond.

Step 1: warp. Pick a reference time \(t_\text{ref}\). Move each event \(e_k = (\mathbf{x}_k, t_k, p_k)\) to where its scene point would have been at \(t_\text{ref}\) if the motion were \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\):

\[ \mathbf{x}'_k = \mathbf{W}(\mathbf{x}_k, t_k; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbf{x}_k - (t_k - t_\text{ref})\,\boldsymbol{\theta} . \]

Step 2: accumulate. Count how many warped events land on each pixel \(\mathbf{x}\). This is the image of warped events:

\[ H(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{k=1}^{N_e} b_k \, \delta\!\left(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'_k\right), \]

where \(b_k = 1\) (count events) or \(b_k = p_k\) (sum polarities), and \(\delta\) is 1 when its argument is zero and 0 otherwise. Since \(\mathbf{x}'_k\) is rarely a whole pixel, each event is in practice shared between its nearest pixels in proportion to closeness, which also makes \(H\) change smoothly with \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\).

Step 3: score. Measure the contrast of \(H\) by its variance over the \(N_p\) pixels:

\[ f(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{N_p} \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} \left( H(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}) - \mu_H \right)^2, \qquad \mu_H = \frac{1}{N_p} \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} H(\mathbf{x}_i; \boldsymbol{\theta}). \]

The estimate is \(\boldsymbol{\theta}^\star = \argmax_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\).

Why the true motion wins. A scene edge that moves at velocity \(\mathbf{v}\) fires its events along a line in space-time: at time \(t\) it is at \(\mathbf{x}_0 + (t - t_\text{ref})\mathbf{v}\). Warp with \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{v}\) and every one of those events lands on \(\mathbf{x}_0\). Warp with anything else and they are spread over a segment of length \(|\mathbf{v} - \boldsymbol{\theta}|\) times the duration.

Why variance rewards that. Expand the square: \(f = \frac{1}{N_p}\sum_i H_i^2 - \mu_H^2\). With \(b_k = 1\), and as long as no event is warped off the image, the total \(\sum_i H_i = N_e\) is the same for every \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), so \(\mu_H\) is a constant. Maximizing the variance is then the same as maximizing \(\sum_i H_i^2\), and a sum of squares with a fixed total is largest when the total is concentrated in few places. Take 12 events on 4 pixels:

By hand. An edge fires at \((x, t) = (10, 0),\ (12, 1),\ (14, 2),\ (16, 3)\), with \(x\) in pixels and \(t\) in ms. Take \(t_\text{ref} = 0\) and an image of 8 pixels, so \(\mu_H = 4/8 = 0.5\).

  • \(\theta = 0\): the events stay at 10, 12, 14, 16. Four pixels hold 1 each. \(f = 4/8 - 0.25 = 0.25\).
  • \(\theta = 1\): they move to 10, 11, 12, 13. Still four pixels with 1 each. \(f = 0.25\).
  • \(\theta = 2\): they move to 10, 10, 10, 10. One pixel holds 4. \(f = 16/8 - 0.25 = 1.75\).

The velocity is 2 px/ms, found without ever deciding which event belongs to which edge.

function contrast(θ, events, t_ref):
    H ← image of zeros
    for each event (x, t, p):
        x' ← x − (t − t_ref)·θ            # undo the candidate motion
        add 1 to H around x'              # shared between the nearest pixels
    return variance(H)

θ* ← the θ that maximizes contrast(θ, events, t_ref)

Try it: 1110 events from two moving bars plus random noise. Drag the candidate speed, then press Reset and Climb.

Notice that at θ = 0 the image of warped events is only the raw event count per pixel: low and spread out (tallest column 32 events, variance 77). At θ = 0.60 the slanted streaks stand upright, the four edges of the two bars appear (tallest column 140), and the variance reaches 1023, thirteen times higher. Climb finds that peak from θ = 0 with 25 evaluations. Set the noise to 100% and the peak stays at 0.60: noise events do not line up for any θ, so they only raise the floor.

In plain words

Take a long-exposure photo of traffic at night and you get streaks. If you knew exactly how each car moved, you could slide every bit of light back along its path and the streaks would shrink to sharp headlights. Contrast maximization runs this backwards: it tries motions until the streaks become points, and the motion that does it is the answer.

4. From one speed to SLAM

The recipe does not care what \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is, as long as it defines a warp. Three steps lead to a back-end.

  • Rotation. For a camera that only rotates, \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is the angular velocity (three numbers), and the warp rotates each event's viewing direction back to the reference time. Table 10.1 lists this as "CMax-ω", for rotational motion. Table 10.1 · p. 295
  • A whole trajectory. In CMax-SLAM the unknowns are the camera's rotations over time. All events are warped onto one panorama, and the trajectory is refined until that panorama is as sharp as possible. This is bundle adjustment with contrast as the objective, and the sharp panorama is an edge map of the scene (Figure 10.3 shows one). §10.5 · p. 294; Table 10.1
  • The other objective. EMBA and EPBA instead write one residual per event from equation (10.1): the brightness change that the current map and trajectory predict at that pixel, minus the \(p_k C\) the event reported. They refine the motion together with a gradient map (EMBA) or an intensity map (EPBA). A gradient map can be turned into an intensity image by Poisson integration (Figure 10.4). §10.5 · p. 294; Table 10.1; Figure 10.4 · p. 290

All three are for rotational motion. The book states that efficient, direct, event-only BA for 6-DoF motion in natural scenes is still open. §10.5 · p. 294

Going deeper (optional): when the sharpest image is wrong

The book warns that when events can be warped onto a few pixels or a line, these methods "can suffer from undesired global optima". §10.4.3 · p. 292 The reason is visible in the table above: variance is highest when all events sit on one pixel. Our 1-D warp only shifts events, so it cannot do that. A warp family that can shrink the image, for instance one with a zoom-like parameter, can send every event toward a single point and score a huge contrast that has nothing to do with the true motion. Practical systems restrict the warp or add a penalty to rule this out.

Two things you can check in the table yourself: none of the first eleven systems has a back-end, and the direct, event-only systems that do have one (CMax-SLAM, EMBA, EPBA) are all rotational.

Common mistakes

  • "Contrast maximization must first decide which events belong to the same edge." It never does. Events are associated implicitly by landing on the same pixel after the warp.
  • "The sharpest image of warped events is always the right motion." Only if the warp cannot collapse the events. See the optional note.
  • "Every SLAM system has a back-end." In Table 10.1 about half of the systems have none, and the early ones never do.

Check yourself

Q1. One edge fires four events at (x, t) = (20, 0), (23, 2), (26, 4), (29, 6), with x in pixels and t in ms. With the reference time at 0, which candidate speed warps all four onto one pixel?

The edge advances 3 pixels every 2 ms, so 1.5 px/ms. Check: 23 − 2 × 1.5 = 20, 26 − 4 × 1.5 = 20, 29 − 6 × 1.5 = 20.

Q2. An image of warped events on 5 pixels is (0, 5, 0, 5, 0). What is its variance?

The mean is 10/5 = 2. The squared deviations are 4, 9, 4, 9, 4, which sum to 30, and 30/5 = 6. The flat image (2, 2, 2, 2, 2) with the same 10 events has variance 0.

Q3. Why does a higher variance of the image of warped events indicate better alignment?

With the mean fixed, variance grows with the sum of squared counts. Events of one edge piling onto one pixel raise that sum; spreading them lowers it.

Q4. Which statement about event-based back-ends agrees with the book?

§10.5 says event-only direct back-ends are recent and limited to constrained motions. Most systems lack a refinement step, indirect back-ends discard most of the event information, and reprojection error is the indirect objective.

Recap

  1. Most event-based systems run tracking and mapping in parallel with no joint refinement. Event-based BA is recent.
  2. Indirect back-ends minimize reprojection error of event features. Direct back-ends minimize a photometric error or maximize event alignment.
  3. Contrast maximization: warp events by a candidate motion, \(\mathbf{x}'_k = \mathbf{x}_k - (t_k - t_\text{ref})\boldsymbol{\theta}\), accumulate them into the image of warped events \(H\), and maximize its variance over \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\).
  4. Variance works because, for a fixed number of events, it grows with \(\sum_i H_i^2\), which is largest when events of the same edge land on the same pixel.
  5. Event-only direct BA (CMax-SLAM, EMBA, EPBA) exists for rotational motion; the 6-DoF case is open.
