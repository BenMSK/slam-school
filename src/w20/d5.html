Today's big idea

A splat map can be both the thing you render and the thing you localize against. Hold the Gaussians fixed and descend on the camera pose: that is tracking. Hold the poses fixed and descend on the Gaussians: that is mapping. The map renders fast and its primitives can be added, moved, and removed, which suits SLAM. But optimizing it is still slow, and tracking by photometric error alone is not yet as accurate as classical sparse methods.

1. Beyond view synthesis: surfaces and semantics

Like NeRF before it, 3DGS has been adapted to other 3D tasks. The book picks two. §14.3.2 · pp. 410–412

GoalProblem with plain 3DGSWhat the cited methods do
Surface reconstruction3DGS is built for photorealistic view synthesis and does not guarantee accurate geometrySuGAR adds a geometric regularizer: it defines a surface normal for each Gaussian and encourages neighbouring Gaussians to be flat, so a mesh can be extracted. 2DGS replaces the 3D Gaussians with 2D discs, which have a normal by construction, and adds normal and depth consistency losses.
Semantic understandingGaussians carry only colourGive each Gaussian a semantic feature and train it from 2D segmentations through the differentiable renderer. LangSplat and FMGS fuse 2D foundation-model features (CLIP, DINO) into the Gaussians, which enables open-vocabulary segmentation.

Because the Gaussians are explicit and discrete, editing the scene, for example moving or deleting an object, is more direct than in a continuous NeRF. §14.3.2 · p. 411

2. One loss for tracking and mapping

As with neural fields (Day 3), the appeal for SLAM is end-to-end optimization through the renderer: localization and mapping share one objective, there is no chain of hand-designed stages, and the map can represent complex light, including transparent objects. On top of that the book names two advantages specific to 3DGS. §14.3.3 · pp. 411–412

  1. Fast rendering. Rasterization is much faster than ray marching, which matters for anything that must interact with the map in real time, such as robot navigation.
  2. Flexible primitives. Discrete, point-like Gaussians can be allocated and updated freely. That fits SLAM, where the scene's boundary grows and loop closures move parts of the map. This is the "dynamic allocation" row of Table 14.1.

How does a renderer estimate a pose? Camera poses are among the parameters in Figure 14.2. The pose enters equation (14.5) through \(\mathbf{W}\) and through the camera-frame mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c\), so the rendered image is a differentiable function of the pose, and the loss has a gradient with respect to it.

Filling the gap: the gradient of a pixel with respect to the camera position

Use the 2D world of Day 4: a Gaussian at \((x, z)\), a camera at sideways position \(c_x\) looking along \(z\). The footprint centre is

\[ \mu' = \frac{x - c_x}{z} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial \mu'}{\partial c_x} = -\frac{1}{z} . \]

Moving the camera to the right slides every footprint to the left, near ones more than far ones. That is parallax. Ignoring the smaller change in footprint width, the opacity at pixel \(u\) is \(\alpha = o\,\exp\bigl(-(u - \mu')^2 / 2\sigma'^2\bigr)\), so by the chain rule

\[ \frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial c_x} = \frac{\partial \alpha}{\partial \mu'}\,\frac{\partial \mu'}{\partial c_x} = \alpha\,\frac{u - \mu'}{\sigma'^2}\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{1}{z}\Bigr) . \]

Numbers: \(o = 0.8\), \(\sigma' = 0.25\), \(z = 2\). At a pixel one \(\sigma'\) from the centre, \(u - \mu' = 0.25\): \(\alpha = 0.8\,e^{-0.5} = 0.485\) and \(\partial\alpha/\partial c_x = 0.485 \times 0.25 / 0.0625 \times (-0.5) = -0.97\). At a pixel four \(\sigma'\) away, \(u - \mu' = 1\): \(\alpha = 0.8\,e^{-8} = 0.0003\) and the derivative is \(-0.002\).

The second number is the catch. A pixel tells the camera where to move only if a footprint already reaches it. If the rendered image and the recorded image do not overlap, the gradient carries almost no information, and the estimate can settle somewhere wrong.

Try it: the three Gaussians of Day 4 are now a finished map. The camera recorded one image at x = 0. Press "Run". Then set "Starting guess x" to −2.0 and run again. Then raise "Image noise".

Notice that from the default guess of 0.8 the estimate reaches 0.000 in about ten steps. From −2.0 it slides the wrong way and stops near −2.82, in a local minimum where the rendered image shares little with the recorded one. From +2.0 it still converges: the loss curve is not symmetric. With image noise at 0.30 the minimum moves to 0.015 and the loss can no longer reach zero (0.051).

3. The systems the book describes

ApproachWhat the book says
RGB-D (several early systems)A depth camera supplies dense depth that initializes the Gaussians' positions and regularizes them through a depth rendering loss. Tracking is accurate and rendering is fast, but extra hardware is needed.
Monocular (MonoGS)Colour images only. The Gaussians start at random positions and are refined by multi-view optimization, with no depth supervision.
Loop closure (LoopSplat)Discrete Gaussians act as features that can be registered across times and viewpoints, so segments of the scene can be matched and aligned.
Physical imaging (I²-SLAM)Adds motion blur and tone mapping to the rendering model, and reconstructs a high-dynamic-range map while tracking the camera.
Hybrid with keypoints or ICPPhoto-SLAM pairs ORB-SLAM's sparse tracking with a 3DGS map. GS-ICP SLAM and RTG-SLAM track with ICP, and RTG-SLAM adds a back-end optimization based on ORB keypoints.
LiDAR + 3DGSHybrid frameworks use the Gaussians as one map shared by several sensor types.

4. What is still hard

The chapter ends with four limits of NeRF- and 3DGS-based SLAM. §14.4 · pp. 414–416

ChallengeThe problemDirections the book proposes
Real-time performanceEnd-to-end systems are very slow. The book contrasts ElasticFusion, which tracked, mapped, and rendered in real time on a 2015 GPU, with most NeRF and 3DGS SLAM methods, which struggle to exceed 5 frames per second on modern GPUs. Rendering is fast; optimizing is the bottleneck.Fewer Gaussians (compact representations). A higher-order optimizer in place of first-order gradient descent. A network that predicts poses and Gaussians in one forward pass.
Tracking accuracyOften behind classical sparse SLAM. These methods assume photometric consistency across frames, which holds on synthetic data such as Replica and breaks in real scenes through unmodelled effects such as sensor noise and rolling shutter.Model the light field and the imaging process more precisely. Match in a domain other than colour. Use a robust feed-forward prediction model.
4D scenesMost methods assume a static world, and commonly mask out possibly moving objects.Build on the success of 3DGS in non-rigid and 4D reconstruction and point tracking, toward a full 4D SLAM.
Large and outdoor scenesMost methods are limited to room-scale sequences. Outdoors, illumination changes with weather and time of day.Efficient map updates such as adaptive resolution, and drift correction over long distances.

On the feed-forward direction the book is specific. PixelSplat and MVSplat predict Gaussian parameters from posed images without any optimization at test time. DUSt3R (Week 19 · Day 5) showed that a network can predict pointmaps without pose input, and NoPoSplat extends that to predicting Gaussians from two unposed views. Using such predictors in an incremental, multi-view SLAM setting is left as future work. §14.4 · pp. 415–416 · Fig. 14.21

Intuition

"Higher-order optimizer" is a pointer back to Week 2 · Day 4. Gradient descent knows only the slope, so on a loss that is steep in some directions and flat in others it takes many small steps, as you saw the widget do from a distant start. Gauss-Newton also uses curvature and can cover the same ground in a few steps. The price is building and solving a linear system over a very large number of Gaussian parameters.

Common mistakes

  • "3DGS renders in real time, so 3DGS SLAM runs in real time." Rendering one image is fast. SLAM must run many optimization steps per frame, and that is the slow part. p. 415
  • "A photorealistic map means accurate geometry." 3DGS optimizes appearance. Accurate surfaces need extra regularization, as in SuGAR or 2DGS. p. 411
  • "Rendering-based tracking has replaced feature-based tracking." The book says its accuracy often falls behind classical sparse SLAM, and several practical systems still track with ORB features or ICP. pp. 414–415

Check yourself

Q1. In the 2D world, the camera moves 0.2 to the right. A Gaussian at depth z = 4 stays where it is. How does its footprint centre move on the image line z = 1?

∂μ′/∂cₓ = −1/z = −0.25, so the centre moves by −0.25 × 0.2 = −0.05. A Gaussian at z = 1 would move four times as far: parallax.

Q2. In the widget, why does tracking fail from a starting guess of −2.0?

A pixel gives a useful gradient only where a footprint already has some opacity. Far from the truth the loss curve has a hump, and descent goes down its wrong side.

Q3. What lets MonoGS do monocular SLAM with no depth sensor, according to the book?

The flexibility of where Gaussians are allocated lets the positions be estimated incrementally from colour images alone.

Q4. Why can 3DGS be combined so directly with ORB-SLAM or ICP?

The Gaussian means are 3D points. Classical tracking supplies the pose, and the Gaussians supply the dense, renderable map.

Q5. Which reason does the book give for tracking accuracy falling behind classical sparse SLAM on real data?

The loss assumes a point looks the same in every frame. That holds on synthetic sequences and fails in real ones unless the imaging process is modelled.

Recap

  1. 3DGS has been extended to accurate surfaces (SuGAR, 2DGS) and to semantic maps with foundation-model features (LangSplat, FMGS).
  2. For SLAM, a splat map offers end-to-end optimization like a neural field, plus fast rendering and freely allocated primitives.
  3. Tracking is gradient descent on the pose through the renderer. It needs the rendered and recorded images to overlap.
  4. Systems range from end-to-end (RGB-D, monocular MonoGS) to hybrids that track with ORB features, ICP, or LiDAR.
  5. Open problems: optimization speed, tracking accuracy on real data, dynamic scenes, and large outdoor scenes.
