When the world moves: short-term, long-term, and deformable scenes

After today you can classify a changing scene as short-term dynamic, long-term dynamic, or deformable from how fast it changes relative to how often the robot observes it, and place a method on the chapter's three design axes.

Today's big idea

Every method so far assumed that only the robot moves. Real scenes change, and the kind of change is defined by what the robot sees. If the scene changes little between two observations, the change looks smooth and can be tracked (short-term dynamics). If it changes a lot, it looks like a jump and must be re-identified (long-term dynamics). When no part of the scene is static, the problem is deformable SLAM.

1. What the static-world assumption bought us

The static-world assumption says the scene does not change and only the robot moves. Two results rest on it. A feature that is detected again constrains the robot pose, because the feature is taken to be where it was (Chapter 1, Week 2 · Day 1). A dense map can be built by fusing many posed observations, because they show the same surfaces (Chapter 5, Week 8 · Day 1). The chapter opens with three scenes where it fails: a car in city traffic, a home robot whose users rearrange the room, and a surgical robot inside the body, where there is no static background and everything deforms. Ch. 15 opening · p. 417

The oldest remedy lets outlier rejection (RANSAC, Week 6 · Day 2) separate measurements that move consistently with the sensor from those that move on their own, as the stars move together while the planets wander.

Filling the gap: how much damage does one moving object do?

A robot drives forward 1.00 m between two frames and tracks 10 features. In its own frame each static feature appears to shift by \(-1.00\) m. Three features sit on a truck that drove forward 0.60 m in the same time, so they shift by only \(-0.40\) m. Least squares picks the shift \(s\) that minimizes \(\sum_i (s_i - s)^2\), the mean:

\[ \hat{s} = \frac{7 \times (-1.00) + 3 \times (-0.40)}{10} = -0.82 \ \text{m}. \]

The robot concludes it moved 0.82 m, an 18 cm error from one frame pair, repeated for as long as the truck is in view. The median of the ten shifts is \(-1.00\) m, the right answer: the robust-estimation idea of Week 6 · Day 1.

Now let the truck cover six of the ten features. The mean is \(-0.64\) m and the median is \(-0.40\) m. The consensus is now the truck. Rejection works only while the static scene holds the majority.

2. Short-term and long-term: a ratio, not a duration

The book classifies dynamics by how they appear in the observations. Short-term dynamics give observations that change gradually (transient characteristics). Long-term dynamics give observations that change abruptly. Almost every physical process is continuous if sampled often enough, so the class does not depend on how long the process takes. It depends on the relation between the rate of change of the scene and the rate of observation of the robot. §15.1.1 · p. 419

Filling the gap: put a number on the ratio

An object moving at speed \(v\), observed \(f\) times per second, moves

\[ d = \frac{v}{f} \]

between two observations. Compare \(d\) with the distance to the nearest thing the object could be confused with. If \(d\) is much smaller, each detection is closest to its own previous position and the object is easy to follow. If \(d\) is larger, the nearest detection is often something else, and the object must be recognized again from its appearance or shape.

Example D of Figure 15.2: a tennis ball at 250 km/h is \(69.4\) m/s. At 340 Hz it moves \(69.4/340 = 0.20\) m between frames, so a very fast object still gives a smooth track. A person walking at 1.5 m/s seen by a 10 Hz LiDAR moves 0.15 m per scan; seen once a minute, up to 90 m.

The other examples of Figure 15.2 follow the same logic. Plant growth measured daily (A) is short-term although the rate is very low, because plants grow little in a day. A building patrolled daily (B) is long-term, because parts of it change completely between patrols, and so are objects rearranged while the robot was gone for a minute (C). Figure 15.2 · p. 420

In most robotics the two rates are of the same order: objects move at about \(10^{-1}\) to \(10^{2}\) m/s and sensors run at about \(10^{0}\) to \(10^{3}\) Hz. The sensor then sees motion as continuous whenever the object is in view, and the distinction becomes simple: short-term is motion within view, long-term is change that happened out of view. §15.1.1 · p. 419

Try it: eight identical objects, one observer. Lower the observation rate step by step.

Notice: at 1.5 m/s and 10 Hz each object moves 0.15 m between observations, about an eighth of the distance to its nearest neighbour, and 99% of the nearest-neighbour matches are right. At 0.5 Hz it moves 3 m, twice that distance, and 15% are right, close to the 12.5% of a blind guess among eight. Then set 3 m/s and 20 Hz: the picture is the same as at the start, because only \(v/f\) matters.

3. The observer decides

Since the class depends on the observations, it is not a property of the object or of the environment. The same physical motion can be short-term for one robot and long-term for another. §15.1.1 · pp. 419–421, Figure 15.3

The book's example is a bottle that a person picks up and puts down next to where it stood: a robot that sees the table only before and after sees a bottle that jumped. Mapping a place in several sessions is the extreme case, with the scene static during each visit and the changes piling up in between. §15.1.1 · p. 419 Both classes assume some static background. In endoscopic surgery the whole scene moves and deforms around the sensor; this setting with no static part is deformable SLAM (Day 5). §15.1.1 · p. 420

Papers use other words. Dynamic or high-dynamic means short-term. Semi-static, quasi-static, or low-dynamic means long-term: objects that could move but stand still while observed, or changes between visits. Spatio-temporal or 4D models keep the history of the scene, 3D plus time (not radar's 4D, whose fourth dimension is velocity). Lifelong, persistent, or continuous systems cope with a varying environment over long time spans. §15.1.1.1 · pp. 420–421

The classes also mix: one rigid moving object, a person who moves and deforms, a building that changes only between patrols, a home with all of these. Estimating every effect jointly gets, in the book's words, exponentially more complex, so knowing which effects to expect allows a narrower system that works better. §15.1.1.2 · pp. 421–422

4. Three axes for placing a method

Figure 15.1 organizes the chapter along three questions. §15.1 · pp. 418–419

AxisThe questionValues
1. Observation characteristicsWhich dynamic effects appear in the observations?Short-term, long-term
2. Level of reconstructionHow much must the robot understand for its task?Pose → scene geometry → temporal scene understanding
3. Time criticalityWhen, and how fast, must the answer be ready?Online, offline

Ordinary pose-graph SLAM sits at one corner: pose only, online, moving parts rejected as outliers. Multi-session change detection sits far from it: a detailed account of how a scene evolved, from a few sessions. Figure 15.1 · p. 418

Axis 2. If the application needs only the robot state, it is often adequate to treat dynamic observations as outliers and localize against the static background. If a dense map is wanted, the robot must detect and represent the motion and change. §15.1.2 · p. 422

Axis 3. Robots mostly must act at once, so the chapter concentrates on real-time methods. Offline processing has more computation and all the data: to decide whether something in frame \(F_t\) moved, it can use the later frames \(F_{t+1}, \dots, F_T\), which an online method has not seen yet. §15.1.3 · pp. 422–423

Common mistakes

  • "Long-term means slow, or a long time ago." A bottle moved during the minute the robot looked away is long-term. Plant growth measured daily is short-term. Only the ratio of the two rates counts.
  • "Robust estimation always finds the static scene." It finds the largest consistent group. When a moving object fills most of the view, that group is the object.

Check yourself

Q1. A 10 Hz LiDAR watches a cyclist riding at 6 m/s. How far does the cyclist move between two scans?

d = v / f = 6 / 10 = 0.6 m, small next to the spacing of road users, so the cyclist can be followed from scan to scan.

Q2. A camera photographs a glacier once a day. Between photos the ice moves 30 cm, and its distinctive features are about 20 m apart. How do these dynamics appear to the camera?

The change between observations (0.3 m) is small compared with the feature spacing (20 m), as in example A. Neither the low rate nor the duration decides it.

Q3. A robot tracks 10 features. Eight are static and appear to shift by −0.50 m. Two are on a moving cart and appear to shift by −0.10 m. What forward motion does plain least squares report?

The mean shift is (8 × (−0.50) + 2 × (−0.10)) / 10 = −0.42 m, so the robot reports 0.42 m instead of 0.50 m.

Q4. What can an offline method use to decide whether a point in frame t belongs to a moving object, that an online method cannot?

Offline, frames t+1 … T are already recorded. Online methods cannot use the future.

Recap

  1. The static-world assumption lets a re-detected feature constrain the pose and posed observations be fused. Moving things break both.
  2. Short-term dynamics look smooth to the robot, long-term dynamics look like jumps. The class is set by rate of change against rate of observation: \(d = v/f\) against the spacing of confusable things.
  3. In practice short-term means motion in view and long-term means change out of view; the same motion can be either. Deformable SLAM is the case with no static part.
  4. A method is placed by three axes: which dynamics it sees, how much it reconstructs, and whether it runs online or offline.
