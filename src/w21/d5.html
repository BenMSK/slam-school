Deformable SLAM: when nothing in the scene stands still

After today you can explain why reconstructing a deforming scene from one camera needs priors, evaluate an as-rigid-as-possible regularizer on a small deformation graph, and describe the tracking and mapping threads of a deformable SLAM system.

Today's big idea

If every point may move freely in every frame, one camera does not provide enough equations to locate the points, let alone the camera. Deformable SLAM becomes solvable by assuming how surfaces deform: smoothly in time, like their neighbours, and without stretching. The rigid part of what the camera sees is then assigned to the camera's own motion and the rest to the scene.

1. The problem, and why it is under-constrained

If only part of the scene deforms, the robot localizes against the static background as on Day 2, and what remains is reconstruction of a deforming object with known sensor poses. Deformable SLAM is the general case with no visible rigid part. The motivating application is minimally invasive surgery, where a monocular camera sees only deforming organs. The related vision problem is non-rigid structure from motion (NRSfM): a point has a different 3D position in every frame, so the map stores a 3D trajectory per point. §15.4 · p. 446

The book names temporal smoothness (a point's trajectory is smooth over time) and spatial smoothness (a point deforms like its neighbours). §15.4.1.1 · p. 446 With depth the counts already balance and the priors over-constrain the problem, which is why the book treats that case first. §15.4.2 · p. 449

2. From NRSfM to deformable SLAM

Classical NRSfM assumes a fixed camera and a moving object with no static background: a hand, a waving flag, a jumping person. Most frames show the whole object. Each point's motion mixes a rigid motion of the object with a local deformation; the rigid part is never computed separately, but it supplies parallax, which makes the estimation well conditioned. §15.4.1.1 · pp. 446–447

Shape from template (SfT) adds a strong prior: the textured 3D shape of the object at rest, the template, is known, and only the deformation is recovered. One model is isometric deformation: the geodesic distance between surface points, the shortest path within the surface, is preserved. Paper bends this way; rubber does not. It is well-posed and led to stable real-time solutions; the alternative jointly minimizes a deformation energy and the reprojection error. Without a template, early NRSfM assumed an orthographic camera, valid only for distant objects, and a low-dimensional basis for the trajectories. Close-ups need a perspective camera, where isometry has also worked well. §15.4.1.1 · pp. 447–448

Separating the rigid from the non-rigid component is ill-posed in general, which the book calls the floating map ambiguity; priors on the displacements of the deformable object make the separation possible. §15.4.1.2 · p. 448

3. With depth: a canonical map and a deformation graph

RGB-D and stereo cameras measure depth. DynamicFusion, which the book calls the seminal work on deformable 3D SLAM from RGB-D data, builds a canonical map, the scene's shape at rest, and deforms it to explain the current depth image, combining a form of embedded deformation (ED) graph with an as-rigid-as-possible regularizer. An ED model discretizes the space of deformations as a graph, which speeds up dense reconstruction. §15.4.2 · p. 449

Filling the gap: what a deformation graph and "as rigid as possible" compute

A deformation graph is a sparse set of nodes spread over the surface. Node \(i\) has a rest position \(\mathbf{q}_i\), a current position \(\mathbf{p}_i\), and a rotation \(\mathbf{R}_i\); edges join nearby nodes. Each dense surface point follows a blend of the transforms of the nodes near it, so a few hundred node transforms stand in for millions of points.

The as-rigid-as-possible energy asks every node's neighbourhood to move as a rigid piece:

\[ E_{\text{ARAP}} = \sum_i \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \norm{ (\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j) - \mathbf{R}_i\, (\mathbf{q}_i - \mathbf{q}_j) }^2 . \]

The energy is zero exactly when every neighbourhood has only rotated and translated, so bending is cheap and stretching or shearing is expensive.

One node by hand, in the plane. Rest edges \(\mathbf{e}_1 = (1, 0)\), \(\mathbf{e}_2 = (0, 1)\); current edges \(\mathbf{e}'_1 = (0.8, 0.6)\), \(\mathbf{e}'_2 = (-0.6, 0.8)\). With \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} = a_x b_y - a_y b_x\), the best rotation angle is

\[ \theta_i = \operatorname{atan2}\Big( \sum_j \mathbf{e}_j \times \mathbf{e}'_j,\ \sum_j \mathbf{e}_j \cdot \mathbf{e}'_j \Big) = \operatorname{atan2}(0.6 + 0.6,\ 0.8 + 0.8) = 36.9^\circ . \]

Rotating the rest edges by \(36.9^\circ\) gives exactly the current edges, so this node contributes 0. The simpler prior "neighbours move alike", \(\sum \norm{(\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j) - (\mathbf{q}_i - \mathbf{q}_j)}^2\), gives \(\norm{(-0.2, 0.6)}^2 + \norm{(-0.6, -0.2)}^2 = 0.8\): it punishes a pure rotation that deforms nothing.

Minimizing \(E_{\text{ARAP}}\) alternates two easy steps. With positions fixed, each \(\mathbf{R}_i\) has the closed form above (in 3D, an SVD as in Week 13 · Day 2). With rotations fixed, the energy is quadratic in the positions, and its gradient vanishes when \(\sum_{j}(\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_j) = \sum_{j} \tfrac12(\mathbf{R}_i + \mathbf{R}_j)(\mathbf{q}_i - \mathbf{q}_j)\) at every free node, a linear system.

Try it: drag the round handle, and switch the regularizer.

Notice: at the start the handle has been pulled down and to the left. With no regularizer only the handle moves, and the strip's departure from rigid motion is 89%. "Neighbours move alike" spreads the displacement but shears every cell: 25%. As-rigid-as-possible lets each piece rotate, and the strip bends like a ruler: 6%. Now drag the handle far beyond the strip's length. No rigid solution exists, and the regularizer spreads the stretch over all edges.

After DynamicFusion, VolumeDeform added photometric information, and KillingFusion enforced smooth, nearly isometric deformations (Figure 15.16). All three store the map as an SDF, which scales poorly and limits exploration; SurfelWarp uses surfels instead. For stereo in surgery, MIS-SLAM combines as-rigid-as-possible deformation with the ED model. §15.4.2 · p. 449

4. One camera: the tracking and mapping threads

A monocular deformable SLAM system keeps the two threads of visual SLAM (Week 12 · Day 1): tracking at frame rate, mapping at keyframe rate. Tracking resembles shape from template: a shape at rest is assumed available for the area being explored, and each frame estimates the camera pose and the deformation of that local template. Mapping takes point tracks and recomputes the templates. §15.4.3–15.4.4 · pp. 450–451

5. Where the field stands

§15.5 closes with open problems. §15.5 · pp. 451–453 Tracking objects helps state estimation under large occlusions and many moving objects, at higher cost. NeRF and 3D Gaussian splatting (Week 20) show promise for dynamic scenes, with real-time cost and map management unsolved. Data association is still hard, and the trend is toward learned priors. Lifelong operation needs memory management: what to keep, how to summarize or marginalize, when to forget. For deformable SLAM the basic questions are open: under what conditions can camera trajectory and deformation both be estimated, can the object's rigid motion be told from the camera's, are there local minima? The stated goal is spatio-temporal AI, a world model that gives a consistent history and predicts what comes next.

Common mistakes

  • "As rigid as possible means the object stays rigid." Each small neighbourhood should move rigidly, each with its own rotation. The whole can bend a great deal.

Check yourself

Q1. A fixed monocular camera observes 50 points on a deforming surface in 20 frames. With no priors, how many unknowns and equations are there?

Every point has its own 3D position in every frame: 3 × 50 × 20 = 3000. Each observation is a pixel: 2 × 50 × 20 = 2000. One depth per observation is undetermined.

Q2. A node has rest edges (1, 0) and (0, 1) and current edges (0, 1) and (−1, 0). What are its as-rigid-as-possible energy and its "neighbours move alike" energy?

The current edges are the rest edges rotated by 90°, so the best rotation fits exactly: ARAP energy 0. Without a rotation the differences are (−1, 1) and (−1, −1), with squared lengths 2 + 2 = 4.

Q3. In deformable SLAM, part of the observed motion could be explained by a rigid transformation. Where does the working hypothesis put it?

Whatever a rigid transformation can explain is included in the camera trajectory.

Q4. Why did Lucas-Kanade optical flow replace ORB matching for frame-to-frame tracking in medical scenes?

The book credits LK's better performance there to its invariance to affine lighting changes.

Recap

  1. A deforming scene seen by one camera has \(3NF\) unknowns and \(2NF\) equations. Temporal and spatial priors, or a depth sensor, supply the rest.
  2. Shape from template assumes a known rest shape; isometry, preserving distances within the surface, is the prior that works under perspective.
  3. Deformable SLAM moves the camera too, sees the scene in close-up pieces, runs causally, and assigns the rigid part of the motion to the camera.
  4. With depth, a canonical map is warped by a deformation graph regularized to be as rigid as possible: neighbourhoods may rotate, not stretch.
