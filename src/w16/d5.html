Bias updates, the IMU factor, and better integration

After today you can correct preintegrated measurements for a changed bias estimate without re-integrating, write the residuals of the IMU factor and the bias factor, and say what the rectangle rule costs in accuracy.

Today's big idea

Yesterday's increments were free of the pose and velocity at keyframe \(i\) but still depended on the bias estimate. A first-order correction, with Jacobians accumulated during the one integration, removes that last reason to re-integrate. The corrected increments then enter the factor graph as one 9-dimensional residual per pair of keyframes, joined by a small factor that says the bias changes slowly.

1. When the bias estimate changes

The increments \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}, \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}, \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}\) were computed by subtracting a bias estimate \(\bar{\mathbf{b}}_i = \{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g_i, \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a_i\}\) from every sample. The biases are unknowns of the optimization, so the solver will revise them: \(\mathbf{b} \leftarrow \bar{\mathbf{b}} + \delta\mathbf{b}\). Re-integrating would bring back the cost preintegration was meant to remove. The book instead expands to first order in \(\delta\mathbf{b}\): §11.2.2.3 · pp. 314–315

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}(\mathbf{b}^g_i) &\simeq \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g_i)\,\Exp\!\Big(\frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^g}\,\delta\mathbf{b}^g\Big) \\ \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}(\mathbf{b}^g_i, \mathbf{b}^a_i) &\simeq \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g_i, \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a_i) + \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^g}\,\delta\mathbf{b}^g_i + \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^a}\,\delta\mathbf{b}^a_i \\ \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}(\mathbf{b}^g_i, \mathbf{b}^a_i) &\simeq \Delta\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g_i, \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a_i) + \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^g}\,\delta\mathbf{b}^g_i + \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^a}\,\delta\mathbf{b}^a_i \end{aligned} \tag{11.30} \]

The vectors are corrected by adding. The rotation is corrected by multiplying with a small rotation on the right, because rotations do not form a vector space (Week 4 · Day 3). The five Jacobians are evaluated at \(\bar{\mathbf{b}}_i\), computed once during preintegration, and then stay constant. The book gives no formulas for them and refers to the original paper. §11.2.2.3 · p. 315

Filling the gap: what the Jacobians look like

Accelerometer bias. Look at the recipe \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{ij} = \sum_k\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \mathbf{b}^a)\Delta t\). It is linear in \(\mathbf{b}^a\), so the derivative can be read off and the correction is exact:

\[ \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^a} = -\sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\,\Delta t, \qquad \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^a} = \sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\Big[\frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ik}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^a}\Delta t - \tfrac12\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\Delta t^2\Big]. \]

If the body does not rotate these are \(-\Delta t_{ij}\mathbf{I}\) and \(-\tfrac12\Delta t_{ij}^2\mathbf{I}\): a bias of \(\delta b\) removes \(\delta b\,\Delta t_{ij}\) of velocity and \(\tfrac12\delta b\,\Delta t_{ij}^2\) of position, as for any constant acceleration.

Gyroscope bias, in the plane. The angle increment is \(\Delta\tilde\theta_{ij} = \sum_k(\tilde\omega_k - b^g)\Delta t\), so \(\partial\Delta\theta_{ij}/\partial b^g = -\Delta t_{ij}\), again exact. But a changed gyroscope bias also changes every \(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}\) that rotates an accelerometer sample. Since \(\tfrac{d}{d\theta}\mathbf{R}(\theta) = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{R}(\theta)\) with \(\mathbf{J}\) the 90° rotation,

\[ \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}}{\partial b^g} = \sum_{k=i}^{j-1}\mathbf{J}\,\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a)\,\underbrace{(-\Delta t_{ik})}_{\partial\Delta\theta_{ik}/\partial b^g}\,\Delta t . \]

This one is a true first-order approximation, since \(\mathbf{R}(\theta)\) is not linear in \(\theta\). The neglected part is proportional to \((\delta b^g)^2\).

In 3-D (Forster et al.) the structure is the same: \(\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}/\partial\mathbf{b}^g = -\sum_k\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{k+1\,j}\T\mathbf{J}^k_r\Delta t\), which is (11.28) with the bias error playing the role of a noise that is the same in every sample, and \(\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}/\partial\mathbf{b}^g = -\sum_k\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a)^\wedge\,\tfrac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ik}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^g}\Delta t\). All are running sums, updated with each incoming sample.

Try it: the one-second increments of Day 4 were integrated with bias estimate zero. Move the gyroscope bias update and compare three ways of responding.

Notice that for a 1°/s update, ignoring it leaves Δp wrong by 31.5 mm, while the first-order correction is within 0.14 mm of a full re-integration and reads no IMU samples. The remaining error grows like the square of the update: at 5°/s it is 3.4 mm, against 157 mm uncorrected. Set the gyroscope update to zero and move the accelerometer slider: the corrected error stays at 0.00 mm, because the increments are exactly linear in the accelerometer bias.

2. The preintegrated IMU factor

Yesterday's model (11.23) says "computed increment = function of states, plus Gaussian noise with covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}\)". As in Week 2 · Day 2, the residual is the difference between the two sides, with the bias-corrected increments of (11.30) in place of the raw ones: §11.2.2.4 · p. 315

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{r}_{\Delta\mathbf{R}_{ij}} &\doteq \Log\!\Big(\Big(\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g_i)\,\Exp\!\Big(\frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^g}\delta\mathbf{b}^g\Big)\Big)\T\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_j\Big) \\ \mathbf{r}_{\Delta\mathbf{v}_{ij}} &\doteq \mathbf{R}_i\T\big(\mathbf{v}_j - \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}\Delta t_{ij}\big) - \Big[\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g_i, \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a_i) + \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^g}\delta\mathbf{b}^g + \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^a}\delta\mathbf{b}^a\Big] \\ \mathbf{r}_{\Delta\mathbf{p}_{ij}} &\doteq \mathbf{R}_i\T\big(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{v}_i\Delta t_{ij} - \tfrac12\mathbf{g}\Delta t_{ij}^2\big) - \Big[\Delta\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}(\bar{\mathbf{b}}^g_i, \bar{\mathbf{b}}^a_i) + \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^g}\delta\mathbf{b}^g + \frac{\partial\Delta\bar{\mathbf{p}}_{ij}}{\partial\mathbf{b}^a}\delta\mathbf{b}^a\Big] \end{aligned} \tag{11.31} \]

Stacked, they form \(\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{I}_{ij}} \in \R^9\), and the factor adds \(\norm{\mathbf{r}_{\mathcal{I}_{ij}}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}} = \mathbf{r}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}^{-1}\mathbf{r}\) to the least-squares objective. The rotation residual is the rotation vector of "measured increment inverse times predicted increment": zero when the states reproduce the measurement. The other two are plain differences. The factor touches the rotation, position, and velocity at both keyframes and the bias at \(i\).

A planar number: Day 4's widget stored \(\Delta\tilde\theta_{ij} = 19.90^\circ\) with \(\sigma = 0.115^\circ\). If the current states say the robot turned \(\theta_j - \theta_i = 20.50^\circ\), the residual is 0.60°, which is 5.2σ, and it adds \(5.2^2 \approx 27\) to the cost. The optimizer will pull the two orientations toward a 19.9° difference, or shift the gyroscope bias to explain the gap.

3. A model for the bias itself

Inside one interval the bias was treated as constant (11.15). Across intervals it is allowed to move, slowly. The book models it as integrated white noise: §11.2.2.4 · pp. 315–316

\[ \dot{\mathbf{b}}^g(t) = \boldsymbol{\eta}^{bg}, \quad \dot{\mathbf{b}}^a(t) = \boldsymbol{\eta}^{ba} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{b}^g_j = \mathbf{b}^g_i + \boldsymbol{\eta}^{bgd}, \quad \mathbf{b}^a_j = \mathbf{b}^a_i + \boldsymbol{\eta}^{bad}, \tag{11.32, 11.33} \]

where the discrete noises have covariances \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{bgd} = \Delta t_{ij}\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{bg})\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{bad} = \Delta t_{ij}\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{ba})\). This is the random walk of Day 2: the variance of the change grows in proportion to the elapsed time. Compare with the sample noise, whose covariance is divided by \(\Delta t\). The corresponding factor penalizes bias changes between consecutive keyframes:

\[ \norm{\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{b}_{ij}}}^2 \doteq \norm{\mathbf{b}^g_j - \mathbf{b}^g_i}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{bgd}} + \norm{\mathbf{b}^a_j - \mathbf{b}^a_i}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{bad}} . \tag{11.34} \]

Example: a gyroscope bias random walk of \(2\times10^{-5}\) rad/s²/√Hz and keyframes 0.25 s apart give a standard deviation of \(2\times10^{-5}\times\sqrt{0.25} = 1\times10^{-5}\) rad/s for the change between keyframes. A proposed jump of \(10^{-4}\) rad/s would be a 10σ event and cost 100. Without this factor each keyframe's bias could absorb any disagreement between IMU and camera. With it, the bias has to be a slowly varying curve, which is what makes it estimable.

4. Better integration

Everything so far used the scheme (11.12), which holds each sample constant until the next one arrives: the rectangle rule, or Euler method. §11.2.3 looks at what this costs and at alternatives. §11.2.3.1 · p. 316 The book's Figures 11.2 and 11.3 integrate one example acceleration signal over 2 s in four ways and report the errors:

Filling the gap: first order against second order

Over one step the rectangle rule replaces \(a(t)\) by \(a(t_k)\). By Taylor expansion the true integral is \(a(t_k)\Delta t + \tfrac12\dot a(t_k)\Delta t^2 + \dots\), so each step is wrong by about \(\tfrac12\dot a\,\Delta t^2\). There are \(T/\Delta t\) steps, so the accumulated error is of order \(\Delta t\): halve the step, halve the error. The trapezoid rule, which is what integrating a piecewise-linear signal amounts to, cancels that term and leaves a per-step error of order \(\Delta t^3\), hence an accumulated error of order \(\Delta t^2\): halve the step, quarter the error.

The methods the book surveys, in brief: §11.2.3 · pp. 316–319

  • Upsampling. Fit a Gaussian process (Week 5 · Day 3) to the samples and query it at a higher rate, then integrate with the rectangle rule as before.
  • Closed-form integration under a motion assumption. Standard preintegration assumes the acceleration is constant in the world frame during a step. Assuming it constant in the body frame ("local" acceleration) matches real motion better; the book quotes about 5% better visual-inertial odometry accuracy on the EuRoC dataset.
  • Continuous acceleration. Represent the rotation-corrected samples \(\hat{\mathbf{a}}_k = \Delta\mathbf{R}_{ik}\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k\) by a continuous function and integrate it analytically: piecewise-linear gives the trapezoid rule, and a Gaussian process with a squared-exponential kernel can be integrated exactly because integration is a linear operation.
  • Continuous rotation. The hard part. The exact solution of \(\dot{\mathbf{R}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\omega}^\wedge\) is an infinite product of small rotations (11.35), with no general closed form because rotations do not commute. One approach works on the rotation vector \(\mathbf{r}(t)\), with \(\mathbf{R} = \Exp(\mathbf{r})\), whose dynamics are \(\dot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{J}_r(\mathbf{r})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\omega}^b_b\) (11.36), and fits a Gaussian process to \(\dot{\mathbf{r}}\) using the gyroscope readings. The book reports accuracy gains of at least an order of magnitude over the standard discrete scheme. §11.2.3.3 · p. 319

Rotation errors matter more than they look, for the reason found on Day 3. Go back to the dead-reckoning widget, set every bias and noise slider to zero, and switch the gyroscope output to "instantaneous sample": the error after 20 s jumps from 6 cm to 2.8 m. Nothing is wrong with the sensor. The rectangle rule on the pitch rate leaves a tilt error of about a tenth of a degree while the vehicle is on the hills, and gravity does the rest.

Common mistakes

  • "After a bias update the preintegration must be redone." For small updates the stored Jacobians correct it to first order. A full re-integration is only worth its cost once the bias has moved so far from \(\bar{\mathbf{b}}_i\) that the neglected second-order term matters.
  • "The bias covariance shrinks with longer intervals, like averaging." It grows: \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{bgd} = \Delta t_{ij}\operatorname{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{bg})\). A random walk wanders further given more time.
  • "The IMU factor constrains absolute position." It constrains how the state at \(j\) relates to the state at \(i\). Absolute position and yaw must come from elsewhere, which is next week's topic.

Check yourself

Q1. In (11.30) the velocity and position increments are corrected by adding a term, but the rotation increment by multiplying with Exp(·). Why?

Adding a matrix to a rotation matrix does not give a rotation. On SO(3) a perturbation is a small rotation multiplied on, here on the right, as in Week 4.

Q2. Planar case. With bias estimate zero, the preintegrated angle over Δt_ij = 1 s is 20.0°. The gyroscope bias estimate is updated to +0.5°/s. The corrected angle increment is

The Jacobian is −Δt_ij = −1 s, so the correction is −1 × 0.5 = −0.5°. A positive bias means the gyroscope over-reported the rate.

Q3. The time between two keyframes doubles. The standard deviation that the bias factor (11.34) allows for the bias change

The covariance is Δt_ij times the continuous one, so it doubles, and the standard deviation grows by √2.

Q4. The sampling rate of a smooth acceleration signal is raised four-fold. By roughly what factor does the error of piecewise-linear (trapezoid) integration fall?

The trapezoid rule is second order: error proportional to Δt². A step four times smaller gives an error 16 times smaller. The rectangle rule would improve only four-fold.

Q5. Which unknowns does the preintegrated IMU factor (11.31) between keyframes i and j involve?

The residuals contain R, p, v at both ends and, through δb, the bias at i. The bias at j enters through the separate bias factor (11.34).

Recap

  1. A changed bias estimate is absorbed by a first-order update of the increments, equation (11.30), using Jacobians accumulated during preintegration. No samples are re-read.
  2. The update is exact in the accelerometer bias and approximate, with an error quadratic in the update, in the gyroscope bias.
  3. The IMU factor is the 9-dimensional residual (11.31) weighted by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}\), linking rotation, position, velocity at two keyframes and the bias at the first.
  4. The bias is a random walk between keyframes, with covariance proportional to \(\Delta t_{ij}\), equations (11.32)–(11.34).
  5. Standard preintegration uses the rectangle rule. Piecewise-linear and Gaussian-process models of the signal integrate more accurately, and rotation is the hard part.
