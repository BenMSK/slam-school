Week 4 review: Lie groups and optimization on manifolds

After today you can move between a pose and its tangent vector, run one Gauss-Newton step on a group by hand, state what a covariance of a pose means, and use the adjoint to change the side of a perturbation.

About 60 min · No new pages

The week in one paragraph

Rotations and poses are matrices tied by constraints, so they form curved manifolds, \(\SO(d)\) and \(\SE(d)\), not vector spaces. They are also groups, and each has a flat companion, its Lie algebra, with one coordinate per degree of freedom. The exponential map turns a vector of the Lie algebra into a group member, and the logarithm goes back. That is enough to rebuild everything Week 2 did with vectors: a perturbation \(\mathbf{T}^0\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) replaces "guess plus step" in Gauss-Newton, \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\) with Gaussian \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) replaces "mean plus noise", and \(\Log\) of a relative transformation replaces subtraction. Because order matters in a group, two more tools are needed: the adjoint, which moves a perturbation across a transformation exactly, and the Jacobian, which combines two exponentials approximately.

2. Mixed quiz

Check yourself

Q1. How many degrees of freedom does a planar pose in \(\SE(2)\) have?

Two for translation and one for the heading: \(3(d-1)\) with \(d=2\). The 3 × 3 matrix has nine entries, but most are fixed.

Q2. You know \(\mathbf{T}^b_a\) and \(\mathbf{T}^c_b\). Which product converts coordinates from frame \(a\) to frame \(c\)?

The matrix nearest the point acts first, and adjacent indices must match: \(\mathbf{T}^c_a = \mathbf{T}^c_b\mathbf{T}^b_a\). The other order does not have matching indices, and poses do not commute.

Q3. What is \(\Exp(\pi)\) in \(\SO(2)\)?

\(\cos\pi = -1\) and \(\sin\pi = 0\), so the matrix is \(-\mathbf{I}\): a half turn. Its determinant is \((-1)^2 = 1\), so it is a proper rotation.

Q4. Which description of \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) for a 3D pose matches the book?

Equation (2.7b): \(\boldsymbol{\xi} = (\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\theta})\in\R^6\). \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\) is not the position, which is \(\mathbf{J}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\rho}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) is an axis times an angle, not Euler angles.

Q5. In Gauss-Newton on \(\SE(3)\), what is the approximation \(\mathbf{T}^0(\mathbf{I}+\boldsymbol{\xi}^\wedge)\) used for?

The first-order form makes the model linear in \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) so that \(\mathbf{H}_i\) can be read off. The residual uses the exact current pose, and the update (2.19) uses the exact exponential, which keeps the pose in the group.

Q6. A point is at \(\boldsymbol{\ell} = (0,0,2)\). The perturbation is \(\boldsymbol{\rho} = (1,0,0)\), \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = (0,0.5,0)\). What are the top three entries of \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\odot}\boldsymbol{\xi}\)?

\(\boldsymbol{\rho}+\boldsymbol{\theta}\times\boldsymbol{\ell} = (1,0,0) + (0,0.5,0)\times(0,0,2) = (1,0,0)+(1,0,0) = (2,0,0)\). A turn about y pushes a point on the z-axis in the x direction, and the translation adds to it.

Q7. One-step model of Day 4 with \(L = 10\) m and heading noise \(\sigma = 0.1\) rad. What is the standard deviation of the sideways tangent coordinate \(\rho_2\)?

The covariance is \(\sigma^2(0,L,1)\T(0,L,1)\). Its middle diagonal entry is \(\sigma^2L^2 = 1\ \text{m}^2\), so the standard deviation is \(L\sigma = 1\) m.

Q8. Which of these identities is exact for every \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\), not only for small ones?

The adjoint identity (2.31) follows from \(\mathbf{T}\exp(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{T}^{-1} = \exp(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}^{-1})\) with no terms dropped. The first is false unless the wedge matrices commute, and the other two are first-order approximations.

3. Pen-and-paper problems

  1. A planar transformation \(\mathbf{T}^b_a\) has \(\theta = 90^\circ\) and \(\mathbf{t}^b_a = (1, 2)\). (a) Write \(\mathbf{T}^b_a\) as a 3 × 3 matrix. (b) Convert the landmark \(\boldsymbol{\ell}^a = (3, 0)\) to frame \(b\). (c) Write \((\mathbf{T}^b_a)^{-1}\) and use it to convert your answer back.

    Solution

    (a) \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\), \(\sin 90^\circ = 1\):

    \[ \mathbf{T}^b_a = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

    (b) \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\ell}^a = (0, 3)\). Adding \(\mathbf{t}\) gives \(\boldsymbol{\ell}^b = (1, 5)\).

    (c) \(\mathbf{R}\T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{t} = (2, -1)\) and the translation of the inverse is \(-\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{t} = (-2, 1)\):

    \[ (\mathbf{T}^b_a)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & -2 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

    Back: \(\mathbf{R}\T(1,5) = (5,-1)\), and \((5,-1) + (-2,1) = (3, 0)\). This is \(\boldsymbol{\ell}^a\) again.

  2. Use Rodrigues' formula to compute \(\mathbf{R} = \Exp(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) for \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = (0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}, 0)\). Check your answer in two ways: apply it to \((1,0,0)\), and recover the angle from the trace.

    Solution

    The axis is \(\mathbf{a} = (0,1,0)\) and the angle \(\phi = \pi/2\), so \(\sin\phi = 1\) and \(1-\cos\phi = 1\). From (2.6b),

    \[ \mathbf{a}^\wedge = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{a}^\wedge\mathbf{a}^\wedge = \mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}\T - \mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}. \] \[ \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I} + \mathbf{a}^\wedge + \mathbf{a}^\wedge\mathbf{a}^\wedge = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \]

    Check 1: \(\mathbf{R}(1,0,0) = (0,0,-1)\). A quarter turn about y by the right-hand rule takes the x-axis to minus z. Check 2: \(\tr\mathbf{R} = 1 = 1 + 2\cos\phi\), so \(\cos\phi = 0\) and \(\phi = 90^\circ\).

  3. Verify the adjoint identity (2.31) in the plane. Let \(\mathbf{T}\) be the pose at \(\mathbf{t} = (2, 0)\) with heading 90°, and \(\boldsymbol{\xi} = (\rho_1,\rho_2,\theta) = (0, 0, \tfrac{\pi}{2})\), a quarter turn on the spot. Compute \(\mathbf{T}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) directly. Then compute \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \Ad(\mathbf{T})\boldsymbol{\xi}\) with the planar adjoint of Day 5, and \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\,\mathbf{T}\).

    Solution

    Left side. \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) is a pure rotation by 90° with no translation. Composing it on the right keeps the position and adds the headings: \(\mathbf{T}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})\) is at \((2,0)\) with heading 180°.

    Adjoint. \(\Ad(\mathbf{T})\boldsymbol{\xi} = (\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\rho} - \theta\,\mathbf{S}\mathbf{t},\ \theta)\). Here \(\boldsymbol{\rho} = \mathbf{0}\) and \(-\mathbf{S}\mathbf{t} = (t_2, -t_1) = (0, -2)\), so \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = (0, -\pi, \tfrac{\pi}{2})\).

    Right side. \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\) has rotation 90° and translation \(\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\rho}_\epsilon\) with both coefficients of \(\mathbf{V}\) equal to \(2/\pi\) (Day 2):

    \[ \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\rho}_\epsilon = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -\pi \end{bmatrix} + \tfrac{2}{\pi}\,\mathbf{S}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -\pi \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

    Now compose: the position of \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\,\mathbf{T}\) is \(\mathbf{R}(90^\circ)(2,0) + (2,-2) = (0,2) + (2,-2) = (2, 0)\), and the heading is \(90^\circ + 90^\circ = 180^\circ\). Both sides agree. Seen from the global frame, turning on the spot at \((2,0)\) is a quarter turn about the origin combined with exactly the translation that puts the robot back where it stood.

  4. Gauss-Newton on \(\SO(2)\), by hand. A robot with unknown heading \(\mathbf{R}\in\SO(2)\) observes one known landmark \(\boldsymbol{\ell} = (1,0)\) and measures \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\ell} + \boldsymbol{\eta} = (0.6, 0.8)\), with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{I}\). Start from \(\mathbf{R}^0 = \mathbf{I}\) and perturb on the right, \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}^0\Exp(\theta)\). (a) Find the Jacobian of \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\ell}\) with respect to \(\theta\). (b) Do two Gauss-Newton iterations. (c) Compare with the exact answer.

    Solution

    (a) To first order \(\mathbf{R}^0\Exp(\theta)\boldsymbol{\ell}\approx\mathbf{R}^0(\mathbf{I}+\theta\mathbf{S})\boldsymbol{\ell} = \mathbf{R}^0\boldsymbol{\ell} + \mathbf{R}^0\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\ell}\,\theta\). So \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{R}^0\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\ell}\), a 2 × 1 matrix, with \(\mathbf{S}\boldsymbol{\ell} = (0,1)\). It is a unit vector for every \(\mathbf{R}^0\), so \(\mathbf{H}\T\mathbf{H} = 1\) and the normal equations reduce to \(\theta^* = \mathbf{H}\T\mathbf{r}\).

    (b) Iteration 1. \(\mathbf{R}^0 = \mathbf{I}\): prediction \((1,0)\), residual \(\mathbf{r} = (0.6,0.8)-(1,0) = (-0.4, 0.8)\), \(\mathbf{H} = (0,1)\), so \(\theta^* = 0.8\). Update: \(\mathbf{R}^0\leftarrow\Exp(0.8)\), a rotation by 0.8 rad.

    Iteration 2. Prediction \(\mathbf{R}^0\boldsymbol{\ell} = (\cos 0.8, \sin 0.8) = (0.6967, 0.7174)\). Residual \(\mathbf{r} = (-0.0967, 0.0826)\). \(\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{R}^0(0,1) = (-0.7174, 0.6967)\). Then \(\theta^* = (-0.7174)(-0.0967) + (0.6967)(0.0826) = 0.0694 + 0.0576 = 0.1269\). Update: \(\mathbf{R}^0\leftarrow\mathbf{R}^0\Exp(0.1269)\), a rotation by \(0.9269\) rad in total.

    (c) The measurement is a unit vector, so the exact answer is the heading with \(\cos\theta = 0.6\), \(\sin\theta = 0.8\): \(\theta = 0.9273\) rad (53.13°). Two iterations are within 0.0004 rad. At every iteration the estimate was a rotation matrix. An additive update of the four matrix entries would have left the circle after the first step.
