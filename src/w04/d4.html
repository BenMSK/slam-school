Uncertainty on Lie groups and the banana distribution

After today you can write a Gaussian over rotations or poses as a mean on the group plus noise in the tangent space, and explain why accumulated heading noise produces a banana-shaped cloud that such a Gaussian can follow.

About 60 min · Read §2.1.4, pp. 59–60 (1 page) · Needs Week 1 Day 2 and Week 4 Day 2

Read first: §2.1.4, pp. 59–60

The section is short. While you read, look for:

  • Equation (2.21) on p. 60: a random vector written as a mean plus zero-mean noise.
  • The reason the book gives for not adding noise to a rotation matrix.
  • Equations (2.22) and (2.23): which object is the mean, where the noise \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) lives, and how the two are combined.
  • The closing remark that this is an induced distribution, and that Chapter 6 uses a different kind.

Today's big idea

A Gaussian needs a mean and a way to add noise to it. For a pose the mean is a group member \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\), the noise is an ordinary Gaussian vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) in the tangent space, and "add" means \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\). Because Exp bends straight lines into arcs, this family can describe the curved clouds that real robot motion produces, which no Gaussian in \((x, y)\) can.

1. A Gaussian is a mean plus noise

For a vector, the book writes a Gaussian random variable as §2.1.4 · p. 60, eq. (2.21)

\[ \mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\delta}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\delta}\sim\N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}) . \]

This is the Gaussian of Week 1 · Day 2, split into two ingredients: a centre \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and a zero-mean spread \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) with covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). The split matters because the "+" is the part that fails for rotations. Adding a noise matrix to \(\mathbf{R}\) breaks \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\), exactly as averaging did on Day 1.

2. Replace + by ⊕

The fix is the perturbation from yesterday. Keep the noise a plain Gaussian vector, but apply it through the exponential map: §2.1.4 · p. 60, eqs. (2.22)–(2.23)

\[ \mathbf{R} = \bar{\mathbf{R}}\oplus\boldsymbol{\delta} = \bar{\mathbf{R}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta}), \qquad \mathbf{T} = \bar{\mathbf{T}}\oplus\boldsymbol{\delta} = \bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta}), \qquad \boldsymbol{\delta}\sim\N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}) . \]

Closure guarantees that every sample is a valid rotation or pose. The mean and the covariance now live in different places:

GroupMeanNoise \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\)Covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\)
\(\SO(2)\)2 × 2 rotationone angle1 × 1 (a variance)
\(\SO(3)\)3 × 3 rotation\(\R^3\)3 × 3
\(\SE(2)\)3 × 3 transformation\(\R^3\): \((\rho_1,\rho_2,\theta)\)3 × 3
\(\SE(3)\)4 × 4 transformation\(\R^6\): \((\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\theta})\)6 × 6

To draw a sample: draw \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) as for any Gaussian vector, then form \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\). The simplest case is a heading. With \(\bar{\mathbf{R}}\) a rotation by 30° and a standard deviation of 5°, a draw of \(\delta = -10^\circ\) gives the rotation by 20°. In \(\SO(2)\) this is nothing more than a Gaussian on the angle, because planar rotations commute.

Because \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) multiplies on the right, it is expressed in the frame attached to the mean pose. For a robot pose, \(\rho_1\) is error along the robot's own forward direction, whichever way the robot faces in the world.

Filling the gap: what "surjective-only" means, and when it matters

The book calls the exponential a "surjective-only mapping". Surjective means every group member is reached. "Only" means it is not one-to-one: angles \(\delta\) and \(\delta + 2\pi\) give the same rotation (Day 2). A Gaussian has tails that run to infinity, so some of its probability lands on \(|\delta| \gt \pi\) and wraps around onto rotations that smaller angles also reach.

How much? With a standard deviation of 10°, the wrap point 180° is 18 standard deviations away, and the wrapped probability is far too small to matter. With a standard deviation of 90° it would matter, and this way of describing uncertainty stops being a good model. The representation is meant for concentrated uncertainty, which is the usual case for a tracked robot.

3. The banana

Let a robot drive straight ahead in \(K\) steps of one unit. Each step is a pose increment whose tangent vector is the command \(\mathbf{u} = (1, 0, 0)\) plus a little Gaussian noise, mostly in heading: \(\mathbf{T}_{k+1} = \mathbf{T}_k\,\Exp(\mathbf{u} + \boldsymbol{\delta}_k)\). Where can the robot be after \(K\) steps? Simulate it 500 times.

Notice that the cloud is curved, and that the pink ellipse, the best Gaussian in (x, y), covers empty ground ahead of the cloud and misses its curled-back ends, while the green contour bends with the cloud. With the default settings (15 steps, 6° of heading noise per step) the mean of (x, y) is at about (14.4, −0.1), 0.6 behind the noise-free end point (15, 0), and the tangent-space mean maps to (15.0, −0.1). The x-coordinate has skewness about −2.2, and a Gaussian has none.

The green contour is computed as the book's definition suggests. Take \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) to be the noise-free end pose, convert every sample to a tangent vector \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_j = \Log(\bar{\mathbf{T}}^{-1}\mathbf{T}_j)\), fit an ordinary mean and covariance to those vectors, and push the two-sigma ellipse of that fit back through \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\cdot)\). (The fit is three-dimensional, and the widget draws the ellipse spanned by its two largest principal directions.) An ellipse in the tangent space becomes a banana on the ground.

In plain words

If you walk twenty paces with your eyes closed, you know quite well how far you walked and rather badly in which direction. The places you might be form a piece of a ring around the starting point, not a blob. A description that says "about twenty paces, direction uncertain" fits that ring. A description in east and north coordinates does not.

4. Why a banana, and why the tangent space straightens it

Filling the gap: a one-step model you can solve by hand

Strip the simulation to its core. The robot first turns by an uncertain angle \(\delta_\theta\sim\N(0,\sigma^2)\) and then drives a known distance \(L\). It ends at

\[ x = L\cos\delta_\theta \approx L\left(1 - \tfrac12\delta_\theta^2\right), \qquad y = L\sin\delta_\theta \approx L\,\delta_\theta , \qquad \text{heading } \delta_\theta . \]

In (x, y). The sideways coordinate is linear in the noise, so it is close to Gaussian with standard deviation \(L\sigma\). The forward coordinate is quadratic in the noise: whichever way the robot turns, it falls short of \(L\), never beyond. That one-sided shortfall is the curl of the banana. Its average is \(\E[x] \approx L(1-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\). For \(L = 10\) and \(\sigma = 0.3\) rad (17°) the mean is at \(x \approx 9.55\), a point inside the bend where the robot almost never is. A Gaussian puts its highest density at its mean.

In the tangent space. Take the mean pose \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) at \((L, 0)\) with heading 0 and try the tangent vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta} = \delta_\theta\,(0, L, 1)\). With \(\boldsymbol{\rho} = (0, L\delta_\theta)\) and \(\theta = \delta_\theta\), the matrix \(\mathbf{V}\) of Day 2 gives

\[ \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\rho} = \frac{\sin\delta_\theta}{\delta_\theta}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ L\delta_\theta \end{bmatrix} + \frac{1-\cos\delta_\theta}{\delta_\theta}\begin{bmatrix} -L\delta_\theta \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -L(1-\cos\delta_\theta) \\ L\sin\delta_\theta \end{bmatrix}. \]

\(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) only shifts this by \((L, 0)\), so \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\) is at \((L\cos\delta_\theta,\ L\sin\delta_\theta)\) with heading \(\delta_\theta\). That is the true end pose, exactly, with no approximation. The tangent vector is a fixed direction \((0, L, 1)\) times the Gaussian number \(\delta_\theta\), so it is exactly Gaussian, with covariance

\[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \sigma^2\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ L \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & L & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \sigma^2\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & L^2 & L \\ 0 & L & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The whole arc of possible end poses is the image under Exp of a straight line in the tangent space. The off-diagonal entry \(L\) says that sideways error and heading error go together: a robot that ended up to the left is also facing left.

The simulation in the widget is messier than this model, since noise enters at every step, and its tangent-space cloud is only approximately Gaussian. The picture still holds: the curvature comes from the group, and a description that respects the group absorbs it. The book points to Long et al. and to Barfoot and Furgale for the full treatment. §2.1.4 · p. 60

5. Where these covariances appear

  • Noise models of factors. An odometry or loop-closure measurement that is itself a pose carries a covariance of this kind, and its residual is formed in the Lie algebra, as in equation (2.20) from yesterday.
  • The uncertainty of an estimate. Yesterday's Gauss-Newton solved for \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\). The inverse of the final information matrix \(\sum_i\mathbf{H}_i\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i^{-1}\mathbf{H}_i\) is the approximate covariance of that \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\). So the covariance a solver reports for a pose is a tangent-space covariance about the estimate, in the sense of (2.23).
  • Left or right. The book's noise multiplies on the right. Putting it on the left, \(\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\,\bar{\mathbf{T}}\), is equally valid but gives a different \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) for the same cloud. Tomorrow's adjoint converts one into the other.

The book closes with a caveat: (2.22) induces a distribution on rotations by pushing a tangent-space Gaussian through Exp. One can also define distributions directly on the group, and Chapter 6 does so because it brings computational advantages there. §2.1.4 · p. 60

Common mistakes

  • "The covariance of a pose is the covariance of its matrix entries." It is the covariance of the tangent vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\): 6 × 6 for a 3D pose, not 16 × 16.
  • "The first block of Σ is the position uncertainty in world coordinates." With right-multiplied noise it is expressed in the frame of the mean pose, and \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\) is not the position error once rotation error is present.
  • "The mean pose is the average of the sampled matrices." Averaging matrices leaves the group (Day 1). The mean is the \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) about which the tangent vectors average to zero.
  • "A Gaussian in the tangent space is always a good model." Only while the spread is well inside a half turn. For very uncertain headings the tails wrap.

Check yourself

Q1. A 3D pose estimate \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\in\SE(3)\) comes with a covariance in the sense of equation (2.23). What is its size?

The covariance belongs to \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\in\R^6\), one entry per degree of freedom, so it is 6 × 6.

Q2. One-step model: a robot turns by \(\delta_\theta\sim\N(0,\sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma = 0.2\) rad, then drives \(L = 10\) m. Roughly where is the mean of its x-coordinate?

\(\E[x]\approx L(1-\tfrac12\sigma^2) = 10\,(1 - 0.02) = 9.8\) m. Every turn, left or right, shortens the forward progress, so the mean falls behind the noise-free end point.

Q3. Same model with \(L = 10\) m. The robot happened to turn by \(\delta_\theta = 0.1\) rad. Which tangent vector \(\boldsymbol{\delta} = (\rho_1,\rho_2,\theta)\) at \(\bar{\mathbf{T}}\) describes its end pose?

\(\boldsymbol{\delta} = \delta_\theta\,(0, L, 1) = (0, 1, 0.1)\). The option (−0.05, 1, 0.1) is roughly the position offset \((x - L,\ y)\) with the heading, but the tangent vector holds \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\), not the position: the forward shortfall is produced by Exp.

Q4. Why does a Gaussian in \((x, y)\) fit the end positions of the simulated robots poorly?

The noise in each step is Gaussian. But the position is a sine and cosine of the heading error, and a nonlinear function of a Gaussian is not Gaussian. In tangent coordinates at the mean pose the relation is close to linear, so the Gaussian shape is nearly kept.

Recap

  1. A Gaussian on a Lie group is \(\mathbf{T} = \bar{\mathbf{T}}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\delta})\) with \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\sim\N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\): the mean is on the group, the covariance in the tangent space.
  2. \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is 3 × 3 for \(\SO(3)\) and \(\SE(2)\), and 6 × 6 for \(\SE(3)\). With right-multiplied noise it is expressed in the frame of the mean.
  3. Heading error makes the forward coordinate fall short by about \(\tfrac12 L\delta_\theta^2\), always on one side. That bends the position cloud into a banana.
  4. In the one-step model the banana is exactly a Gaussian along the line \(\delta_\theta(0, L, 1)\) in the tangent space.
  5. The model assumes concentrated uncertainty, since Exp is onto but not one-to-one.
