Where outliers come from, and why least squares cannot cope

After today you can name the sources of outliers in SLAM, compute how far one bad measurement moves a least-squares estimate, and say what a breakdown point is.

Today's big idea

Least squares is the right estimator when every error is Gaussian. Real front-ends sometimes match the wrong things, and those measurements are wrong by meters, not by noise. Because the cost squares each residual, a few such outliers outvote all the good measurements and the estimate can be arbitrarily wrong.

1. What least squares silently assumes

In Week 2 · Day 2 you derived the back-end problem from a noise model. If measurement \(\mathbf{z}_i\) equals its prediction \(h_i(\mathbf{x}_i)\) plus zero-mean Gaussian noise with covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i\), then the maximum a posteriori estimate is the nonlinear least-squares problem

\[ \mathbf{x}_{\text{MAP}} = \argmin_{\mathbf{x}} \sum_i \norm{\mathbf{z}_i - h_i(\mathbf{x}_i)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i}, \tag{3.1} \]

where \(\mathbf{x}_i\) is the part of the state that measurement \(i\) involves and \(\norm{\mathbf{e}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \mathbf{e}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{e}\). Ch. 3 opening · p. 75 The chapter writes the size of one term as a scalar residual \(r_i(\mathbf{x}) = \norm{\mathbf{z}_i - h_i(\mathbf{x}_i)}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i}\), so the cost is \(\sum_i r_i(\mathbf{x})^2\). A residual is measured in standard deviations: \(r_i = 3\) means "this measurement disagrees with the estimate by 3σ". §3.1.2 · p. 77

The book calls the Gaussian light-tailed: it gives almost no probability to large errors. §3.1.2 · p. 77

Filling the gap: how light is "light-tailed"?

For a Gaussian error \(e\) with standard deviation \(\sigma\), the probability of landing further than \(k\sigma\) from the mean is \(2\,(1 - \Phi(k))\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution. From a table of \(\Phi\):

Error larger thanProbability under the Gaussian modelIn words
1σ0.32one measurement in three
3σ0.0027one in 370
5σ5.7 × 10⁻⁷one in 1.7 million
10σ1.5 × 10⁻²³never, for practical purposes

So a model with only Gaussian noise says that a 10σ error cannot happen. When one does happen, the estimator "believes" the measurement anyway, and the only way to make a 10σ error plausible is to move the estimate a long way toward it.

2. Where the bad measurements come from

The back-end never sees raw data. It sees what the front-end extracted (see Week 1 · Day 1), and the extraction involves a decision: which landmark is this, which earlier pose saw the same place? Deciding what a measurement refers to is the data association problem. A wrong decision gives a measurement that is precise but about the wrong thing. §3.1.1 · p. 76

CauseWhat goes wrongKind
Wrong landmark identityA detection is attributed to landmark \(\ell_j\) but really shows a different landmark, so \(\mathbf{z}_{ij}\) is far from \(h(\mathbf{p}_i, \ell_j)\).Data association
False loop closurePlace recognition links poses \(\mathbf{p}_i\) and \(\mathbf{p}_j\) that did not observe the same scene.Data association
Moving objectThe landmark is identified correctly, but the model assumes it is static and it has moved.Violated assumption
Sensor faultA faulty wheel encoder, dust on the lens.Failure or degradation

False loop closures often have a specific cause, perceptual aliasing: two different places look the same to the sensor, like two classrooms in one building or identical office cubicles. §3.1.1 · p. 76

A false loop closure is the worst kind of outlier. It is not a noisy version of the truth. It is a confident statement, with a small covariance, that two distant poses coincide.

3. Why squaring makes it worse

Evaluate the cost near the true state. Good measurements have residuals around 1 (they are in units of σ), so each contributes about 1 to \(\sum_i r_i^2\). An outlier that is off by 20σ contributes \(20^2 = 400\): as much as 400 good measurements. The optimizer reduces the cost fastest by shrinking that one term, and it does so by bending the estimate away from what the good measurements say. §3.1.2 · p. 77

Try it: the blue points lie near the line \(y = 0.5x + 1\). Drag the pink point around, then change the number of good points.

Notice: with 12 good points, one bad point at (8.5, 1.2) changes the fit from y = 0.52x + 0.82 to y = 0.36x + 1.35, and at the true line it alone carries 98% of the cost. Drag it further and the line follows without limit. With 40 good points the pull is weaker, but it never reaches zero.

Filling the gap: one outlier moves the estimate by error ÷ N

The simplest least-squares problem is estimating one number \(x\) from \(N\) direct measurements \(z_1, \dots, z_N\) with equal noise. The cost is \(\sum_i (z_i - x)^2\). Set its derivative to zero:

\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (z_i - x)^2 = -2\sum_{i=1}^{N}(z_i - x) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} z_i . \]

The least-squares estimate is the mean. Now corrupt one measurement by adding an error \(E\) to it. The sum grows by \(E\), so

\[ \hat{x}_{\text{corrupted}} = \hat{x} + \frac{E}{N}. \]

Two things follow. The shift is proportional to \(E\), so there is no bound on the damage: make \(E\) large enough and the estimate is anywhere you like. And the shift shrinks only like \(1/N\), so adding good data dilutes an outlier but never removes it. Example: nine measurements equal to 2.0 and one equal to 52.0 give \(\hat{x} = (9 \times 2 + 52)/10 = 7.0\), far from every single measurement.

In a line fit, or in SLAM, the same thing happens with one extra twist that the widget shows: a bad point far from the middle of the data has a long lever arm, so it changes the slope as well as the offset.

4. Breakdown point: a number for "how fragile"

Robust statistics measures fragility with the breakdown point of an estimator: the smallest fraction of the measurements that, if replaced by arbitrary values, can move the estimate arbitrarily far. This idea is not in today's pages, but it is the standard yardstick and you will meet it in the papers the chapter cites.

  • The mean (least squares) has breakdown point \(1/N\), which tends to 0: one bad measurement out of any number is enough, as Section 3 showed.
  • The median has breakdown point 50%. To drag the middle value arbitrarily far you must corrupt half of the data.

Example: the data \(\{0.8,\ 0.9,\ 1.0,\ 1.1,\ 1.2\}\) have mean 1.0 and median 1.0. Replace 1.2 by 100. The mean becomes \((0.8 + 0.9 + 1.0 + 1.1 + 100)/5 = 20.76\). The median, the middle of \(\{0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, 100\}\), is still 1.0.

No estimator can have a breakdown point above 50% without extra information: if most of the data are wrong and they agree with each other, nothing in the data says which group to trust. Remember this limit on Days 3 and 5, where methods tolerate far more than 50% outliers. They can because they are told something extra, for example that the odometry is trustworthy.

In plain words

Least squares runs a vote where each measurement's voice gets louder the more it disagrees with everyone else. One measurement that is wildly wrong shouts down a hundred that are quietly right.

Common mistakes

  • "An outlier is a very noisy measurement." Usually it is a precise measurement of the wrong thing. Inflating every covariance to cover it would throw away the accuracy of the good data.
  • "With enough good data the outliers average out." The shift is \(E/N\). It is small only if \(E\) is bounded, and a false loop closure can be wrong by the size of the building.
  • "Outliers are random, so they point in random directions and cancel." Perceptual aliasing produces many outliers that agree with each other, as in the SubT corridors.

Check yourself

Q1. Nineteen range measurements read 4.0 m and one reads 44.0 m. What is the least-squares estimate of the range?

The estimate is the mean: the outlier's error is 40 m and N = 20, so the shift is 40/20 = 2 m, giving 6.0 m. Check: (19 × 4 + 44)/20 = 120/20 = 6.

Q2. Evaluated at the true state, 200 good measurements each have residual 1 (in units of σ) and one outlier has residual 30. What fraction of the cost Σr² comes from the outlier?

The outlier contributes 30² = 900 and the good measurements 200 × 1 = 200, so the share is 900/1100 ≈ 0.82. One measurement in 201 owns most of the cost.

Q3. A camera tracks a parked car as a landmark. The car is identified correctly in every frame, then drives off while still in view. Why do its measurements become outliers?

The association is right, so this is not a data-association error. The measurement model assumes a static landmark, and a violated assumption produces large residuals just as a wrong match does (p. 76).

Q4. You have 11 measurements of one quantity. What is the smallest number of them an adversary must corrupt to move the median arbitrarily far?

The median of 11 values is the 6th smallest. With 5 corrupted values, the 6th smallest is still one of the 6 good values, or lies between good values. With 6 corrupted values sent to +∞, the 6th smallest is corrupted. The mean would need only 1.

Recap

  1. Least squares is MAP estimation under Gaussian noise, and a Gaussian says large errors essentially never happen.
  2. Outliers come mostly from wrong data association (wrong landmark, false loop closure, perceptual aliasing), and also from violated assumptions and sensor faults.
  3. Squaring gives an outlier with residual \(k\) the weight of \(k^2\) good measurements.
  4. One corrupted measurement shifts a mean by \(E/N\): unbounded in \(E\). The breakdown point of least squares is zero.
  5. The chapter's answer has two layers: reject outliers in the front-end, and make the back-end cost robust to the ones that remain.
