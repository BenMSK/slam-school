M-estimation: robust losses, IRLS, and Black-Rangarajan duality

After today you can compare robust losses by their influence functions, derive the IRLS weight and run IRLS by hand, and rewrite a robust loss as weighted least squares plus an outlier process.

Today's big idea

Replace the square in the cost by a function that grows more slowly for large residuals, so an outlier's pull is bounded or vanishes. The new problem is no longer least squares, but it can be solved as a sequence of weighted least-squares problems: weight each measurement by how believable its current residual is, solve, and repeat.

1. M-estimation and the influence function

Front-end rejection misses some outliers, so the back-end must tolerate them. §3.3 · p. 85 M-estimation ("maximum-likelihood-type estimation") keeps the residuals \(r_i(\mathbf{x})\) of Day 1 and changes only what is done to them:

\[ \mathbf{x}_{\text{MAP}} = \argmin_{\mathbf{x}} \sum_i r_i(\mathbf{x})^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{x}_{\text{MEST}} = \argmin_{\mathbf{x}} \sum_i \rho\big(r_i(\mathbf{x})\big). \tag{3.9} \]

The robust loss \(\rho\) must be non-negative and grow less than quadratically for large residuals. §3.3 · p. 86 To see why that is the right requirement, take the gradient of the cost \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i \rho(r_i(\mathbf{x}))\) with the chain rule:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial \mathbf{x}} = \sum_i \frac{\partial \rho(r_i)}{\partial r_i}\cdot\frac{\partial r_i(\mathbf{x})}{\partial \mathbf{x}}. \tag{3.10} \]

The second factor says in which direction measurement \(i\) wants to move the estimate. The first factor says how hard it pulls. That factor is the influence function \(\psi(r) := \partial\rho(r)/\partial r\). §3.3 · p. 86 For the quadratic loss \(\psi(r) = 2r\): the pull grows without bound, which is Day 1's problem restated. A robust loss has \(\psi(r) \ll 2r\) for large \(r\), and ideally \(\psi(r)\to 0\).

2. The menu of losses

Figure 3.5 shows the common choices as curves. §3.3 · pp. 86–87 Here they are as formulas, each with one parameter \(\beta\) that marks where inliers end. The book gives the formulas for the truncated quadratic (3.13) and Geman-McClure (3.17). Huber and Tukey are the standard definitions, scaled like the book's so that every loss equals \(r^2\) for small \(r\). The last column, \(w = \psi/(2r)\), is explained in Section 3.

Try it: pick a loss and move the residual slider. Watch the middle panel.

Notice: for Huber with β = 1, a residual of 2.5 costs 4.00 where the quadratic charges 6.25, and its influence is capped at 2.00 where the quadratic's is 5.00. Capped is not zero: an outlier keeps pulling. Switch to Geman-McClure and the influence rises, peaks, and falls back toward zero. For the truncated quadratic and Tukey it is exactly zero beyond β.

How to choose: §3.3 · pp. 86–87

  • Huber is convex, so the optimization behaves well. It is common in bundle adjustment. The price is the non-zero influence you just saw.
  • Losses that go flat (Geman-McClure, Tukey, truncated quadratic) give distant outliers little or no influence, but they are non-convex, so the optimizer can stop in a bad local minimum.
  • A hard cut-off (truncated quadratic) suits problems where the largest error an inlier can have is known.
  • The maximum consensus loss, 0 for small residuals and 1 for large ones, simply counts outliers. Minimizing it is consensus maximization (3.4) from Day 2. Its influence is zero everywhere, so gradients are useless and it is optimized by sampling: that is RANSAC.

Going deeper (optional): a robust loss is still MAP

Switching to \(\rho\) does not abandon probability. A loss is the negative log of a noise density, and losses that grow slowly correspond to heavy-tailed densities. §3.3 · pp. 88–89 Take the density proportional to \(\max\{e^{-r^2},\ e^{-\beta^2}\}\): Gaussian near zero, a constant floor (a uniform density over the sensor's range) far away. Its negative log is \(\min\{r^2, \beta^2\}\), the truncated quadratic. The floor says "with some small probability this measurement is unrelated to the state".

3. Iteratively reweighted least squares

Problem (3.9) has two costs. The fast solvers of Week 2 · Day 4, Gauss-Newton and Levenberg-Marquardt, need a sum of squares. And non-convex costs make any local solver depend on its starting point. §3.3.1 · p. 89 Plain gradient descent does work on some problems (Figure 3.6) but takes thousands of iterations. §3.3 · p. 88

Iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) gets the solvers back. At each iteration it solves a weighted least-squares problem whose weights come from the previous estimate:

\[ \mathbf{x}^{(t+1)} = \argmin_{\mathbf{x}} \sum_i w_i\big(\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\big)\, r_i^2(\mathbf{x}), \qquad w_i\big(\mathbf{x}^{(t)}\big) = \frac{\psi\big(r_i(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})\big)}{2\,r_i(\mathbf{x}^{(t)})}. \tag{3.11, 3.12} \]

§3.3.1 · p. 89

Filling the gap: why this weight, and why the iteration lands in the right place

Freeze the weights at \(w_i\) and differentiate the weighted cost \(\sum_i w_i\,r_i^2(\mathbf{x})\):

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{x}}\sum_i w_i\,r_i^2(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i 2\,w_i\,r_i(\mathbf{x})\,\frac{\partial r_i(\mathbf{x})}{\partial\mathbf{x}}. \]

Compare with (3.10), \(\sum_i \psi(r_i)\,\partial r_i/\partial\mathbf{x}\). The two sums agree term by term if \(2\,w_i\,r_i = \psi(r_i)\), that is \(w_i = \psi(r_i)/(2r_i)\). That is (3.12).

Now suppose the iteration has converged: \(\mathbf{x}^{(t+1)} = \mathbf{x}^{(t)} = \mathbf{x}^\star\). Then \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) minimizes the weighted problem built with its own weights, so the weighted gradient is zero at \(\mathbf{x}^\star\). By the matching, the gradient (3.10) of the robust cost is zero there too. A fixed point of IRLS is a stationary point of the M-estimation problem. For a non-convex \(\rho\) it may be a poor local minimum, which is tomorrow's topic.

By hand. Estimate one number \(x\) from \(z = \{1, 2, 3, 14\}\), with \(r_i = z_i - x\) and the Huber loss, \(\beta = 2\). A weighted least-squares fit of a constant is the weighted mean \(x = \sum w_i z_i / \sum w_i\).

In SLAM the "solve" inside each iteration is one Gauss-Newton or Levenberg-Marquardt step on the weighted problem. §3.3.1 · p. 89 Try it on the line fit of Day 2: 40% of the points are outliers, most of them on a second line.

Notice: with Huber (β = 0.5), IRLS moves from the least-squares line y = 0.12x + 2.81 to y = 0.37x + 1.44 in about 28 steps and stays there, still dragged by the outliers. Geman-McClure reaches y = 0.51x + 0.94, next to the true line, in about 12 steps. The truncated quadratic stops after 2 steps at y = 0.09x + 2.86: it kept the points that happened to lie near the bad starting line and gave all the others weight zero for good.

The book's experiments agree in outline (Figure 3.7): IRLS converges in tens of iterations and is much faster than gradient descent (under 1.5 s against about 5 s for the Huber loss on M3500), at a slight loss of accuracy, and with non-convex losses it can end in a wrong solution. §3.3.1 · pp. 89–91

4. Black-Rangarajan duality: weights as variables

Rule (3.12) was obtained by matching gradients, and it is undefined where \(\rho\) has a corner, such as \(|r| = \beta\) for the truncated quadratic. Black-Rangarajan (B-R) duality gives the same algorithm a cleaner footing by making the weights unknowns of the optimization. §3.3.2 · p. 90

Start with the truncated quadratic, \(\rho(r_i) = \min\{r_i^2, \beta_i^2\}\) (3.13). The book rewrites it as

\[ \rho\big(r_i(\mathbf{x})\big) = \min_{w_i\in[0,1]}\ w_i\,r_i^2(\mathbf{x}) + (1-w_i)\,\beta_i^2. \tag{3.14} \]

Filling the gap: checking (3.14), and the Geman-McClure case in full

Truncated quadratic. The expression in (3.14) is linear in \(w_i\): it equals \(\beta_i^2 + w_i\,(r_i^2 - \beta_i^2)\). A linear function on \([0,1]\) is smallest at an end. If \(r_i^2 \lt \beta_i^2\) the slope is negative, so \(w_i = 1\) and the value is \(r_i^2\). If \(r_i^2 \gt \beta_i^2\) the slope is positive, so \(w_i = 0\) and the value is \(\beta_i^2\). Either way the result is \(\min\{r_i^2, \beta_i^2\}\). Example: \(r = 3,\ \beta = 2\) gives \(4 + 5w\), minimized at \(w = 0\) with value 4.

Geman-McClure. Example 3.5 claims that \(\rho(r) = \beta^2 r^2/(\beta^2+r^2)\) (3.17) equals \(\min_{w\in[0,1]}\ w\,r^2 + \Phi_\rho(w)\) with \(\Phi_\rho(w) = \beta^2(\sqrt{w}-1)^2\) (3.18), and leaves the algebra out. Differentiate \(g(w) = w\,r^2 + \beta^2(\sqrt{w}-1)^2\):

\[ g'(w) = r^2 + \beta^2\cdot 2(\sqrt{w}-1)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{w}} = r^2 + \beta^2 - \frac{\beta^2}{\sqrt{w}}. \]

Setting \(g'(w) = 0\) gives \(\sqrt{w} = \beta^2/(r^2+\beta^2)\), so

\[ w^\star = \left(\frac{\beta^2}{r^2+\beta^2}\right)^{2}, \tag{3.20} \]

which lies in \([0,1]\), and is a minimum because \(g''(w) = \tfrac12\beta^2 w^{-3/2} \gt 0\). Substitute back, using \(\sqrt{w^\star} - 1 = -r^2/(r^2+\beta^2)\):

\[ g(w^\star) = \frac{\beta^4 r^2}{(r^2+\beta^2)^2} + \frac{\beta^2 r^4}{(r^2+\beta^2)^2} = \frac{\beta^2 r^2\,(\beta^2 + r^2)}{(r^2+\beta^2)^2} = \frac{\beta^2 r^2}{\beta^2+r^2}. \]

That is (3.17). Notice also that \(w^\star\) is the Geman-McClure weight in Section 2's table: B-R duality and rule (3.12) give the same number.

Substituting (3.14) for every measurement turns M-estimation into a joint problem over the state and one weight per measurement:

\[ \min_{\mathbf{x},\ w_i\in[0,1]}\ \sum_{i} \Big[\, w_i\,r_i^2(\mathbf{x}) + (1-w_i)\,\beta_i^2 \,\Big]. \tag{3.15} \]

The first term is weighted least squares. The second charges \(\beta_i^2\) for every measurement that is switched off, so the optimizer cannot simply discard everything. At the solution, \(w_i = 1\) marks an inlier and \(w_i = 0\) an outlier. §3.3.2 · p. 90

Theorem 3.4 extends this to a family of losses. Write \(\phi(z) := \rho(\sqrt{z})\), the loss as a function of the squared residual. If \(\phi'(z)\to1\) as \(z\to0\), \(\phi'(z)\to0\) as \(z\to\infty\), and \(\phi''(z)\lt0\), then M-estimation (3.9) is equivalent to

\[ \min_{\mathbf{x},\ w_i\in[0,1]}\ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Big[\, w_i\,r_i^2(\mathbf{x}) + \Phi_\rho(w_i) \,\Big], \tag{3.16} \]

where the penalty \(\Phi_\rho\), called the outlier process, depends on the loss. §3.3.2 · pp. 90–92 For Geman-McClure the conditions are easy to check: \(\phi(z) = \beta^2 z/(\beta^2+z)\), \(\phi'(z) = \beta^4/(\beta^2+z)^2\), which is 1 at \(z = 0\) and tends to 0, and \(\phi''(z) = -2\beta^4/(\beta^2+z)^3 \lt 0\). Tomorrow you solve (3.16) by alternating between \(\mathbf{x}\) and the weights.

Common mistakes

  • "A robust loss removes outliers." It reduces their influence. With Huber the influence never reaches zero, as the worked example shows.
  • "The IRLS weights are tuning parameters." They are computed from the residuals by \(w = \psi(r)/(2r)\). The only parameter is \(\beta\).
  • "IRLS finds the minimum of the robust cost." It finds a stationary point that depends on where it starts. The widget's truncated-quadratic run stops at a cost of 8.04, while the line next to the true one costs about 4.3 under the same loss.

Check yourself

Q1. With the Huber loss and β = 1, what IRLS weight does a measurement with residual r = 4 receive?

Beyond β the weight is β/|r| = 1/4. Check with w = ψ/(2r): ψ = 2β = 2, and 2/(2 × 4) = 0.25.

Q2. For the Geman-McClure loss with β = 2, what are the loss and the weight at r = 2?

ρ = β²r²/(β² + r²) = 16/8 = 2, half of the quadratic loss r² = 4. The weight is (β²/(β² + r²))² = (4/8)² = 0.25.

Q3. One IRLS iteration with the truncated quadratic loss, β = 3.5, on the scalar data z = {0, 1, 2, 9}, starting from the mean x = 3. What is the new estimate?

Residuals z − x are −3, −2, −1, 6. Those with |r| below 3.5 get weight 1 and the last gets 0. The weighted mean is (0 + 1 + 2)/3 = 1.0.

Q4. Which loss leaves an outlier with a pull that does not shrink as its residual grows?

Huber's influence is constant at ±2β beyond β. Geman-McClure's decays toward zero, and the other two are exactly zero beyond β. Huber accepts this in exchange for convexity.

Q5. In problem (3.15), why does the optimizer not set every weight to zero and make the first term vanish?

The outlier process (1 − w)β² charges β² for discarding a measurement. Discarding pays only when the residual squared exceeds β².

Recap

  1. M-estimation replaces \(r^2\) by a robust loss \(\rho(r)\) that grows sub-quadratically (3.9).
  2. The influence function \(\psi = \rho'\) is how hard a measurement pulls. Huber caps it. Geman-McClure, Tukey, and the truncated quadratic send it to zero and pay with non-convexity.
  3. IRLS solves weighted least squares repeatedly with \(w_i = \psi(r_i)/(2r_i)\) (3.12). Its fixed points are stationary points of the robust cost.
  4. B-R duality writes \(\rho(r) = \min_{w\in[0,1]} w\,r^2 + \Phi_\rho(w)\). For the truncated quadratic \(\Phi_\rho = (1-w)\beta^2\). For Geman-McClure \(\Phi_\rho = \beta^2(\sqrt{w}-1)^2\).
