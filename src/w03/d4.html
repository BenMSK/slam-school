The Bayes tree, and why a new measurement touches only part of it

After today you can group the conditionals of an eliminated factor graph into a Bayes tree, name the frontal and separator variables of each clique, and predict which cliques a new factor forces you to recompute.

Today's big idea

The conditionals of a Bayes net that came from elimination can be grouped into cliques, and those cliques form a tree. During elimination, information only travels from the leaves up to the root. A new factor can therefore change only the cliques that hold its variables and the cliques above them. Take that part of the tree apart, eliminate it again, and hang the untouched sub-trees back on.

1. The problem: measurements never stop

A robot wants a fresh estimate every time a measurement arrives. Refactoring \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) from scratch each time costs more and more as the trajectory grows, although one new measurement changes very little. For a linear problem there are classical ways to update an existing factorization (tomorrow shows one). But SLAM is nonlinear: when the estimate moves, old factors must be linearized again, old rows of \(\mathbf{A}\) change, and it is not clear how to do that without factoring everything again. The book's answer is to stop thinking of \(\mathbf{R}\) as a matrix and to store it as a graphical model that can be edited locally, the Bayes tree. §1.7 · p. 46

2. Elimination gives a chordal Bayes net, and chordal graphs have cliques that form a tree

A graph is chordal (or triangulated) if every cycle of more than three nodes has a chord, an edge joining two nodes that are not consecutive on the cycle. Ignoring arrow directions, the Bayes net produced by the elimination algorithm is always chordal. §1.7.1 · p. 46

Filling the gap: why elimination always produces a chordal graph

Take any cycle in the Bayes net and let \(v\) be the variable on it that was eliminated first. Its two neighbours on the cycle, \(u\) and \(w\), were eliminated later, so the edges \(v\)–\(u\) and \(v\)–\(w\) mean that both were in the separator of \(v\). Yesterday's rule says eliminating \(v\) joins all members of its separator, so \(u\) and \(w\) share a factor from then on, and whichever of the two is eliminated first gets the other in its separator. The Bayes net therefore has the edge \(u\)–\(w\). If the cycle has more than three nodes, \(u\) and \(w\) are not consecutive on it, and this edge is a chord.

The same argument shows that each variable together with its separator is a clique, a set of variables that are all joined pairwise.

The toy Bayes net (last panel of Figure 1.10) has the cycle \(\mathbf{p}_1\)–\(\mathbf{p}_2\)–\(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\), so at the level of single variables it is not a tree. §1.7.1 · p. 47 But its largest cliques are \(\{\boldsymbol{\ell}_1, \mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2\}\), \(\{\mathbf{p}_2, \mathbf{p}_3\}\) and \(\{\boldsymbol{\ell}_2, \mathbf{p}_3\}\), and these three overlap like a tree: the first and the third each share one variable with the middle one and nothing with each other. For chordal graphs this always works. The book states it without proof. The undirected version is known as a clique tree or junction tree.

3. The Bayes tree

A Bayes tree is a directed tree whose nodes are the cliques \(\mathbf{c}_k\) of the chordal Bayes net. For each clique: §1.7.1 · p. 47

  • the separator \(\mathbf{s}_k = \mathbf{c}_k \cap \boldsymbol{\varpi}_k\) is what the clique shares with its parent clique \(\boldsymbol{\varpi}_k\);
  • the frontal variables \(\mathbf{f}_k = \mathbf{c}_k \setminus \mathbf{s}_k\) are the rest;
  • the node stores one conditional \(p(\mathbf{f}_k \mid \mathbf{s}_k)\), and the clique is written \(\mathbf{f}_k : \mathbf{s}_k\).

The joint density is the product over the cliques, equation (1.55): \(p(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_k p(\mathbf{f}_k \mid \mathbf{s}_k)\). The root has an empty separator and stores a prior \(p(\mathbf{f}_r)\). Every variable is frontal in exactly one clique.

Filling the gap: building the tree from the Bayes net

The book says the cliques can be "identified". The procedure, from the iSAM2 paper, visits the conditionals in reverse elimination order. For \(p(\mathbf{x}_j \mid \mathbf{s}_j)\): if \(\mathbf{s}_j\) is empty, start a root clique. Otherwise find the clique in which the first-eliminated variable of \(\mathbf{s}_j\) is frontal. If that clique's variables are exactly \(\mathbf{s}_j\), add \(\mathbf{x}_j\) to it as another frontal variable. If not, start a new clique \(\mathbf{x}_j : \mathbf{s}_j\) as its child. For the toy example:

Each clique is a small dense piece of \(\mathbf{R}\): the rows of its frontal variables, with nonzeros only in the columns of the clique. The book calls the Bayes tree "a deeply meaningful sparse storage scheme" for the square-root information matrix. §1.7.2 · p. 48 Rows of different branches overlap only in columns that belong to a common ancestor: in Figure 1.13(c), the rows of the two child cliques meet only in the root's columns.

4. Updating: only the path to the root

Two facts about how the tree was made explain everything. §1.7.2 · p. 48

  1. Information flows upward only. A clique is computed from its own factors and from the new factors \(\tau\) passed up by its children. Nothing computed in a parent is ever used in a child.
  2. A factor enters at the first of its variables to be eliminated, that is, in the clique where that variable is frontal.

So a new factor \(\phi(\mathbf{x}_j, \mathbf{x}_{j'})\) changes the cliques where \(\mathbf{x}_j\) and \(\mathbf{x}_{j'}\) are frontal and every clique above them, up to the root. Sub-trees hanging below or beside these paths were computed from factors that have not changed. The update is an edit of the tree:

1. detach the affected cliques (the paths from the new factor's variables to the root)
2. turn each of them back into a factor on its variables f_k and s_k
3. add the new factor
4. eliminate this small factor graph, in any convenient order -> a new top of the tree
5. re-attach the untouched sub-trees ("orphans") below it

Figure 1.14 adds a factor between \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_3\). \(\mathbf{p}_1\) is frontal in the left clique and \(\mathbf{p}_3\) in the root, so those two are detached. They become a factor on \((\boldsymbol{\ell}_1, \mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2)\) and a factor on \((\mathbf{p}_2, \mathbf{p}_3)\). Add \(f(\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_3)\) and eliminate in the order \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1, \mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2, \mathbf{p}_3\):

  • \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\): separator \(\{\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2\}\), as before.
  • \(\mathbf{p}_1\): its factors are \(\tau(\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2)\) and the new \(f(\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_3)\), so its separator is now \(\{\mathbf{p}_2, \mathbf{p}_3\}\) and not just \(\{\mathbf{p}_2\}\).
  • \(\mathbf{p}_2\): separator \(\{\mathbf{p}_3\}\). Then \(\mathbf{p}_3\), with none.

Run the tree-building procedure on these four conditionals and you get a root \(\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2, \mathbf{p}_3\) with a child \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1 : \mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2\). The clique \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2 : \mathbf{p}_3\) is hung back under the root unchanged. §1.7.2 · p. 49

Intuition

Why is \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2 : \mathbf{p}_3\) untouched although the new factor involves \(\mathbf{p}_3\)? Because \(\mathbf{p}_3\) is only its separator. The clique stores "how to place \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\) once \(\mathbf{p}_3\) is known", and that rule was built from \(\phi_5\) and \(\phi_9\) alone. The new measurement changes what we believe about \(\mathbf{p}_3\), which is stored in the root. The value of \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\) will still move when back-substitution runs again; its conditional does not.

Common mistakes

  • "The root is where the robot started." The root holds the variables eliminated last. A good incremental ordering puts the most recent poses there, because that is where the next factors will arrive.
  • "The Bayes tree is a different, approximate model." It is the same density and the same \(\mathbf{R}\) as the Bayes net, grouped by clique.
  • "A factor between two variables changes two cliques." It changes their cliques and all the ancestors of both. If one of the variables is deep in the tree, that can be most of it.

Check yourself

Q1. In the clique written ℓ₁, p₁ : p₂, which variables are frontal?

Frontal variables are written before the colon and the separator after it. The clique stores p(ℓ₁, p₁ | p₂).

Q2. A cycle a – b – c – d – a has no other edges. Is the graph chordal?

Chordal means every cycle longer than three has a chord. Elimination would add one: whichever node goes first, its two neighbours are joined.

Q3. In the toy Bayes tree a second prior factor on ℓ₂ alone arrives. Which cliques must be recomputed?

The factor enters where ℓ₂ is frontal, and the new factor that clique passes upward changes its parent, the root. The clique ℓ₁, p₁ : p₂ is on no path from ℓ₂ to the root.

Q4. With ordering ℓ₁, ℓ₂, p₁, p₂, p₃, which rows of R belong to the root clique?

The root's frontal variables are p₂ and p₃, the last two eliminated, so its conditionals are the last two rows. Row 3, p(p₁ | p₂), went into the left child.

Q5. Why can the sub-trees that are not on a path to the root be reused as they are?

Information flows upward during elimination, so a clique depends only on its own factors and its descendants. The estimates of its variables may still change, through the separator, when back-substitution is run.

Recap

  1. Batch refactoring wastes work, and plain incremental matrix updates do not handle relinearization. The Bayes tree is \(\mathbf{R}\) stored so it can be edited.
  2. Elimination yields a chordal Bayes net. Its cliques form a tree.
  3. Each clique \(\mathbf{f}_k : \mathbf{s}_k\) stores \(p(\mathbf{f}_k \mid \mathbf{s}_k)\); the separator is what it shares with its parent; \(p(\mathbf{x}) = \prod_k p(\mathbf{f}_k \mid \mathbf{s}_k)\).
  4. Information flows from leaves to root, and a factor enters at its first eliminated variable.
  5. A new factor affects only the cliques of its variables and their ancestors. Detach them, add the factor, re-eliminate, re-attach the orphans.
