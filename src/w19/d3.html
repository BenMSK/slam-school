Today's big idea

Asking a network for the pose makes it relearn geometry. Asking it for matches lets it do what networks are good at, recognizing that two image patches show the same thing, and leaves the geometry to the factor graph. RAFT is the cleanest example: it compares every pixel of one image with every pixel of the other once, stores the result, and then refines its flow estimate by looking up that table again and again.

1. Predict the measurements, not the state

In the notation of Part I, SLAM estimates the state \(\mathbf{x}\) (poses, depths) from measurements \(\mathbf{z}\) (for example feature matches) by solving for \(\mathbf{x}^{\text{MAP}}\). Yesterday's networks went from images straight to \(\mathbf{x}\) in one forward pass. To do that well, a network has to learn multi-view geometry from scratch: how depth, pose, and intrinsics relate. The alternative is to let the network predict \(\mathbf{z}\) and keep the optimization. §13.2 · p. 374

In plain words

Finding the same corner in two photos is a recognition problem. Working out how the camera moved from a set of matched corners is a geometry problem with a known solution. Give each job to whoever is good at it.

2. Learned keypoints, descriptors, and matchers

Indirect SLAM (Week 11 · Day 3) first finds keypoints, salient image locations, then computes a descriptor vector from the neighbourhood of each, and matches keypoints by comparing descriptors. Each step can be learned. §13.2.1–13.2.2 · pp. 374–375

SystemWhat is learnedHow it works, per the book

Descriptor networks are typically trained with a contrastive loss: descriptors of matching points should be similar, and descriptors of non-matching points far apart. §13.2.1 · p. 374

Filling the gap: a contrastive loss you can compute

The book describes the loss in words. One standard form, for a pair of descriptors at distance \(D\), with a margin \(m \gt 0\):

\[ \ell = \begin{cases} D^2 & \text{if the pair is a true match,} \\ \max(0,\, m - D)^2 & \text{if it is not.} \end{cases} \]

Matching pairs are pulled together. Non-matching pairs are pushed apart, but only until they are \(m\) apart; after that they cost nothing. With \(m = 1\): a true match at \(D = 0.3\) costs \(0.09\); a non-match at \(D = 0.4\) costs \((1 - 0.4)^2 = 0.36\); a non-match at \(D = 1.2\) costs 0.

SuperGlue's output. For \(N\) keypoints in image 1 and \(M\) in image 2, the network outputs an assignment matrix: for each keypoint of image 1, a likelihood for each keypoint of image 2, plus one extra column meaning "no match". §13.2.2 · p. 374 A small made-up example with three keypoints on each side:

Image 1 keypoint→ B1→ B2→ B3→ unmatched
A10.020.930.010.04
A20.900.030.020.05
A30.050.020.080.85

A1 matches B2, A2 matches B1, and A3 is most likely occluded or out of view in image 2. The extra column is what lets the network decline to match, which is the outlier rejection of Week 6 · Day 1 done inside the matcher. The attention layers are the ones from Day 1: self-attention lets a keypoint consult the other keypoints of its own image, and cross-attention lets it consult the candidates in the other image.

SuperGlue and LightGlue work on a sparse set of keypoints, which is cheap but ignores most of the image and yields few good matches where texture is poor. LoFTR matches densely to avoid this. All of them plug into an indirect SLAM system as ordinary visual measurements with their factors. p. 375

3. Optical flow, and RAFT's correlation volume

Optical flow is the per-pixel motion between two frames: a vector \(\mathbf{f}(u, v)\) for every pixel, saying where that pixel went. It is dense matching, and the flow field can serve as measurements in a factor graph. Classical methods computed it by minimizing a data term (matched regions should look alike) plus a regularization term (the motion field should be plausible). §13.2.3 · pp. 375–376

RAFT (Recurrent All-Pairs Field Transforms) is the learned method the book describes, because DROID-SLAM is built on it. It has three parts. §13.2.3 · pp. 376–378 · Fig. 13.7

Feature extraction. Two encoders map an image of size \((h_{in}, w_{in}, 3)\) to a feature map of size \((h, w, c)\): an appearance encoder \(g_\theta\) applied to both images, and a context encoder \(c_\theta\). By default \(h = h_{in}/8\) and \(w = w_{in}/8\). p. 376

Correlation volume. Take the inner product of every feature vector of image 1 with every feature vector of image 2: eq. (13.4) · p. 377

\[ \mathbf{C}_{ijkl}(I_1, I_2) = \langle g_\theta(I_1)_{ij},\, g_\theta(I_2)_{kl} \rangle = \sum_m g_\theta(I_1)_{ijm} \cdot g_\theta(I_2)_{klm}. \tag{13.4} \]

\(\mathbf{C}\) has four indices and shape \(h \times w \times h \times w\). Fix a pixel \((i, j)\) of image 1, and \(\mathbf{C}_{ij\cdot\cdot}\) is a whole image: a map of how well each pixel of image 2 resembles it.

Filling the gap: (13.4) as one matrix product, and how big it is

Number the \(hw\) pixels of each feature map \(1, \dots, hw\) and stack the feature vectors as rows of two matrices \(\mathbf{F}_1, \mathbf{F}_2 \in \R^{hw \times c}\). Then

\[ \mathbf{C} = \mathbf{F}_1 \mathbf{F}_2\T \in \R^{hw \times hw}, \qquad \mathbf{C}_{pq} = \sum_m (\mathbf{F}_1)_{pm} (\mathbf{F}_2)_{qm}, \]

which is (13.4) with \(p\) standing for \((i, j)\) and \(q\) for \((k, l)\). (Which factor carries the transpose depends on whether you stack features as rows or as columns.) A tiny case with two pixels and \(c = 2\): rows \((1, 0)\) and \((0.6, 0.8)\) in \(\mathbf{F}_1\), rows \((0.8, 0.6)\) and \((0, 1)\) in \(\mathbf{F}_2\) give

\[ \mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0 \\ 0.96 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}. \]

Pixel 2 of image 1 resembles pixel 1 of image 2 most (0.96).

Size: a \(384 \times 512\) image gives a \(48 \times 64\) feature map, which is 3072 pixels. The volume has \(3072^2 \approx 9.4\) million entries. The correlation pyramid adds three coarser copies \(\mathbf{C}^1, \mathbf{C}^2, \mathbf{C}^3\), made by average-pooling the last two dimensions, so \(\mathbf{C}^l\) has shape \(h \times w \times h/2^l \times w/2^l\). Here that is \(3072 \times (3072 + 768 + 192 + 48) \approx 12.5\) million numbers in total. Only image 2's axes shrink: every pixel of image 1 keeps its own row at full resolution. p. 377

4. Lookup and update: the iteration

RAFT keeps a flow estimate \(\mathbf{f}\), starting from zero. One iteration does two things. §13.2.3 · pp. 377–378 · Fig. 13.8

Lookup. For pixel \((u, v)\) of image 1, the current guess of its match in image 2 is \((u', v') = \mathbf{f}(u, v) + (u, v)\). RAFT reads the correlation volume on a small grid around that guess,

\[ N(u', v') = \{ (u' + \Delta u,\, v' + \Delta v) \mid \Delta u, \Delta v \in \{-r, \dots, r\} \}, \tag{13.5} \]

with bilinear interpolation, since \((u', v')\) is not a whole pixel. It does the same at each coarser level around the rescaled position \((u'/2^l, v'/2^l)\), and concatenates everything into one feature vector. The fine level tells it about small displacements, and the coarse levels about large ones.

In one dimension: pixel \(u = 10\) with current flow \(3.4\) has \(u' = 13.4\). With \(r = 2\) the lookup reads positions \(11.4, 12.4, 13.4, 14.4, 15.4\) of row 10 of \(\mathbf{C}^0\); for instance the middle one is \(0.6\,\mathbf{C}^0[10][13] + 0.4\,\mathbf{C}^0[10][14]\). At level 1 it reads five positions around \(13.4/2 = 6.7\), which together span about 10 pixels of the original row. With \(r = 3\) in two dimensions, a lookup reads \(7 \times 7 = 49\) values per level, 196 in total, out of the millions stored.

Update. A convolutional GRU (a gated recurrent unit whose linear layers are replaced by convolutions) takes four inputs, each a 2-D feature map: its hidden state from the previous iteration, the context features, the current flow, and the correlation features just looked up. It outputs a new hidden state and a flow update: p. 378

\[ \mathbf{f}^{k+1} = \boldsymbol{\delta}\mathbf{f}^k + \mathbf{f}^k . \]

The same weights are used in every iteration (weight tying). The book notes that tied weights and bounded activations encourage convergence to a fixed point, and that the convolutions pass information between neighbouring pixels. RAFT is trained on synthetic data with ground-truth flow and generalizes well to real images. pp. 376, 378

Try it: press Step a few times, or Play. Then set "Pyramid levels" to 1 and run again.

Notice that with the starting settings (true shift 5 px, radius 2, three levels) the mean flow error goes 5.00 → 0.89 → 0.10 px in two iterations and stays near 0.1. The finest window (pink) cannot reach a match 5 px away, but the coarse levels (amber) can, and they pull the estimate in until the fine level takes over. With one level, the error is still about 3 px after 12 iterations: most pixels never find their match. The update rule in this widget is a fixed formula (a soft-argmax of the values read, then smoothing over neighbouring pixels), where RAFT has a learned Conv-GRU. The correlation volume, pyramid, and lookup follow the equations above.

Intuition

RAFT behaves like an optimizer whose update rule was learned. Compare it with Gauss-Newton (Week 2 · Day 4): keep an estimate, look at the local evidence around it, compute a correction, repeat. The book puts it this way: RAFT combines deep learning with traditional first-order optimization. p. 376 That design is what allows DROID-SLAM, tomorrow, to put a real Gauss-Newton step inside the same loop.

Common mistakes

  • "The correlation volume is recomputed in each iteration." It is built once. Iterations only index into it, which is why a dozen of them are affordable.
  • "The pyramid shrinks both images." Only the last two dimensions, those of image 2, are pooled. Each pixel of image 1 keeps its own full-resolution entry.
  • "The lookup returns the best match." It returns raw correlation values around the current guess. Deciding which way to move is the update operator's job.
  • "Sparse matchers are obsolete." The book lists the trade: keypoints reduce computation; dense matching helps where texture is limited. p. 375

Check yourself

Q1. RAFT's feature maps are 40 × 50. How many entries does the level-0 correlation volume have?

There are 40 · 50 = 2000 pixels in each feature map, and the volume holds one number for every pair: 2000² = 4,000,000.

Q2. In a 1-D version, pixel u = 20 has current flow −2.5 and the lookup radius is r = 1. Which positions of image 2 are read at level 0?

The guess is u′ = 20 − 2.5 = 17.5, and the neighbourhood is u′ + Δu for Δu in {−1, 0, 1}. The values are read by interpolation.

Q3. What is the extra column in SuperGlue's assignment matrix for?

A keypoint may be occluded or outside the other image. The extra column gives that outcome its own likelihood (p. 374).

Q4. A true motion is 20 pixels at feature resolution and the lookup radius is 4. Why can RAFT still find it from a zero initial flow?

Level l is pooled by 2ˡ, so one cell at level 3 covers 8 pixels and a radius of 4 cells reaches 32 pixels. Coarse levels see large displacements, fine levels refine them.

Recap

  1. Predicting matches keeps the geometry in the factor graph and gives the network a recognition task.
  2. SuperPoint learns detection and description. SuperGlue and LightGlue match keypoints with attention and can answer "no match". LoFTR matches densely.
  3. RAFT builds the all-pairs correlation volume (13.4) once, as a matrix product of feature maps, and pools it into a four-level pyramid.
  4. Each iteration looks up a \((2r+1)^2\) neighbourhood (13.5) per level around the current match and lets a Conv-GRU output a flow update.
  5. Tied weights and repeated lookups make RAFT a learned iterative optimizer for flow.
