Today's big idea

Parts I and II built SLAM from models written by hand: a measurement model, a noise model, a solver. Part III asks what happens when some of those pieces are learned from data. Chapter 13 gives the first answer. A network can replace one component, add predicted depth or pose as extra factors, be trained through the optimizer itself, or regress 3D points directly from a pair of images. In most of these, the factor graph stays.

1. Is SLAM solved? What Part III is about

The Prelude opens with a question the field keeps asking. Its answer: yes, if you restrict yourself to small, static, indoor scenes with plenty of features. The uses people actually want are underwater, on other planets, in disaster zones, and in dense city traffic, and there the answer is no. Prelude III · p. 361

The editors call the direction of travel Spatial AI. Compared with the SLAM of Parts I and II it differs in two ways. First, it uses deep learning for the parts of the problem that are hard to model by hand, which makes systems more adaptive and robust. Second, it changes what a map is: maps that can render views nobody captured (novel view synthesis), maps that carry meaning (semantic information), and maps that cope with a scene that changes. The Prelude adds two cautions: use foundation models, and do not forget that the result has to run on a real device. §III.1 · pp. 361–362

Why not skip the map and ask a large image-and-text model about the scene? The Prelude's answer is that such models give a holistic description of an image and often struggle with spatial questions. Many tasks need a persistent 3D representation that is kept up to date, a spatio-temporal memory, and building one is what SLAM does. §III.2 · p. 362

3. Four ways to put a network into SLAM

Chapter 13 starts from the pipeline you know from Weeks 11 and 12: detect features, match them, reject outliers, then optimize a factor graph. A deep network is a function with many parameters that is fitted to examples. The chapter sorts the ways of using one by how much of the pipeline it takes over. Ch 13 intro · pp. 366–368

  1. Replace one component. Train a network to detect features, describe them, match them, or reject outliers, then plug it back into the usual pipeline. Such networks can often beat their hand-designed counterparts. p. 366
  2. Predict 3D properties and add them as factors. A network estimates depth from one image, or relative pose from two, and the prediction enters the factor graph as an extra term. p. 366
  3. Train through the optimizer. The weakness of the modular approach is that errors made by one component feed the next and compound. The alternative is to make more of the pipeline one trainable module, with bundle adjustment as a layer of the network. This is the differentiable optimization of Week 7 · Day 1, put to work. p. 366
  4. Replace the stack. DUSt3R predicts a pointmap, one 3D point for every pixel, directly from uncalibrated images. p. 366

The chapter's own summary is that these methods combine learning with geometry: some feed network predictions into an optimization-based pipeline, some join learned components to factor graphs, and some estimate motion and structure with a network alone. pp. 366–368

4. The network vocabulary you need this week

The chapter uses a handful of network terms without defining them. Here they are, at the depth this week needs.

Filling the gap: networks, losses, and training in five lines

A neural network is a function \(f_\theta\) built by composing simple differentiable steps called layers. The vector \(\theta\) holds all its adjustable numbers, the parameters or weights. A loss \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\) measures how far the outputs are from what we want on a set of training examples. Training lowers the loss by gradient descent:

\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta), \]

with a small step size \(\eta\). This is the steepest descent of Week 2 · Day 4, applied to the weights instead of the robot's state. Example with one parameter: \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = (\theta - 3)^2\), start at \(\theta = 0\), \(\eta = 0.25\). The gradient is \(2(\theta - 3) = -6\), so \(\theta\) becomes \(0 - 0.25 \cdot (-6) = 1.5\). The next gradient is \(-3\), giving \(\theta = 2.25\). Each step halves the distance to the minimum at 3.

The gradient is computed by reverse-mode automatic differentiation (Week 7 · Day 1), which is why every layer must be differentiable. If the labels come from a sensor or a simulator (for example, true depth), the training is supervised. If the loss is built from the input data alone, it is self-supervised; you will meet one tomorrow.

Two kinds of layer appear all week.

A convolutional layer slides one small filter over the image and applies it at every position. With the 1-D filter \((-1, 0, 1)\) and the signal \((2, 2, 5, 9, 9)\), the three interior outputs are \(5 - 2 = 3\), \(9 - 2 = 7\), and \(9 - 5 = 4\): the filter responds where brightness changes. A convolutional neural network (CNN) stacks many such layers with learned filters, usually shrinking the image grid as it goes, so the output is a coarse grid of feature vectors. RAFT's encoders output features at 1/8 of the input resolution. §13.2.3 · p. 376

An attention layer lets every feature vector look at all the others, however far apart they are in the image. A transformer is a network made mostly of attention layers, and a vision transformer (ViT) is a transformer whose inputs are small image patches turned into vectors called tokens. Figure 13.6 draws attention as three boxes: MatMul, SoftMax, MatMul. Fig. 13.6 · p. 375

Filling the gap: what MatMul → SoftMax → MatMul computes

Take one query vector \(\mathbf{q}\), and a set of tokens that each offer a key \(\mathbf{k}_j\) and a value \(\mathbf{v}_j\).

\[ s_j = \mathbf{q}\T \mathbf{k}_j, \qquad a_j = \frac{e^{s_j}}{\sum_m e^{s_m}}, \qquad \text{output} = \sum_j a_j \mathbf{v}_j . \]

The first matrix product computes all scores \(s_j\) at once. The softmax turns scores into positive weights that sum to 1. The second matrix product averages the values with those weights. (Transformers also divide the scores by \(\sqrt{d}\), the square root of the vector length, and learn the linear maps that produce queries, keys, and values.)

Example: scores \((2, 1, 0)\) give \(e^2 = 7.39\), \(e^1 = 2.72\), \(e^0 = 1\), which sum to 11.11. The weights are \(0.665, 0.245, 0.090\). If the values are the numbers \(10, 20, 30\), the output is \(6.65 + 4.90 + 2.70 = 14.25\).

In self-attention the queries and the keys come from the same image. In cross-attention the queries come from one image and the keys and values from the other, which is how a network compares two views.

Try it: turn the query, then raise the sharpness β.

Notice that at the start the query points almost at key k2, which gets weight 0.73, and the output 2.15 is pulled toward the value 2. At β = 0 every key gets 1/6 and the output is the plain mean 3.5. At β = 20 the layer picks k2 alone. Attention is a soft, differentiable way of saying "find the best match".

Common mistakes

  • "Part III replaces Parts I and II." The systems in this chapter still solve least-squares problems on factor graphs. What changes is where the residuals and their weights come from.
  • "A big image-language model makes the map unnecessary." The Prelude says these models often struggle with spatial understanding and that many tasks need a persistent 3D representation. §III.2 · p. 362
  • "Learned components always help." Plugging separately trained modules together lets their errors compound. That observation is the reason for end-to-end training. p. 366

Check yourself

Q1. According to the Prelude, in which setting could SLAM be called solved?

Page 361 limits "solved" to small, static, indoor, feature-rich environments, and lists the others as the open cases.

Q2. A system trains a depth network separately and adds its output to the factor graph as an extra term. Which approach of section 3 is this?

The network predicts a 3D property (depth) and the prediction becomes a factor. The optimizer is not part of the training, so it is not end to end.

Q3. Attention scores for three keys are (1, 1, 0). What weight does the third key get? Use e = 2.72.

The exponentials are 2.72, 2.72, 1, which sum to 6.44. The third weight is 1/6.44 = 0.155, about 0.16. A softmax weight is never exactly zero.

Q4. With loss (θ − 3)², θ = 1, and step size η = 0.1, what is θ after one gradient-descent step?

The gradient is 2(1 − 3) = −4. The update is 1 − 0.1·(−4) = 1.4.

Recap

  1. SLAM is solved only for small, static, feature-rich indoor scenes. Part III is about the rest, under the name Spatial AI.
  2. Spatial AI adds learning for what is hard to model, and richer maps: renderable, semantic, and able to change.
  3. A network can replace a component, supply factors, be trained through the optimizer, or regress pointmaps directly.
  4. Training is gradient descent on a loss over the network's weights.
  5. Attention scores every key against a query, applies a softmax, and returns the weighted average of the values.
