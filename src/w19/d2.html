Learning depth and camera pose, with and without labels

Today's big idea

A network can be trained to output depth from one image or relative pose from two. Used alone, such predictions are less accurate than geometry. Used as extra factors in a factor graph, they fix what a single camera cannot: scale, robustness, and density. And the network does not need 3D labels: if depth and pose are right, one image can be re-drawn from the other, and the mismatch is a loss you can differentiate.

1. Depth from one image, and what SLAM gains from it

Give a network many pairs of an image and its depth map, from depth sensors, LiDARs, or rendering engines, and it learns to predict depth from a single image. The book describes the current recipe as a pre-trained vision transformer followed by a convolutional decoder that brings the coarse features back to image resolution, trained on a mix of more than 20 real and synthetic datasets so that it generalizes. §13.1.1 · p. 369

A monocular SLAM system gains three things from such a network. §13.1.1 · p. 369

  • Scale. One moving camera recovers the scene only up to a scale factor, and that factor drifts over time (scale drift). Depth predicted in meters pins it down.
  • Robustness. Predicted depth helps in cases that break monocular geometry: pure rotation, changing camera parameters, moving objects.
  • Density. Feature-based SLAM tracks a sparse set of points, and textureless areas barely appear in the map. A depth map has a value at every pixel.

Filling the gap: why one camera cannot see scale

A pinhole camera with focal length \(f\) and principal point \((c_x, c_y)\) projects a point \((X, Y, Z)\) in its own frame to the pixel

\[ \pi_c(X, Y, Z) = \left( f\,\frac{X}{Z} + c_x,\; f\,\frac{Y}{Z} + c_y \right). \]

Multiply the whole world by \(s\): every point and every camera translation. A point's coordinates in any camera frame become \((sX, sY, sZ)\), and the ratios \(sX/sZ = X/Z\) do not change. Every image is identical, so no amount of image data can tell \(s\). A network that has seen many scenes with metric depth knows roughly how large cars and doorways are, and that is information the geometry does not contain. (Camera model: Week 11 · Day 3.)

From depth maps to a dense map. Lifting every pixel into 3D with its predicted depth and the SLAM pose, and accumulating the points, gives a dense reconstruction. The book names two problems with doing only that, and two systems that go further. §13.1.1 · pp. 369–370

ProblemSystemWhat it does
Depth maps of neighbouring frames disagree with each otherMonoRecFeeds the depth network a cost volume: for each pixel and each of a set of candidate depths, how well the brightness agrees with the neighbouring frames
Moving objects leave trails in a static mapMonoRecA second network predicts a moving-object mask from the cost volume, and masked pixels are not lifted into 3D
Depth maps contain only visible surfacesBehind the Scenes (BTS)Predicts a volumetric density field \(\phi(\mathbf{x}): \R^3 \to \R^+\) for the whole camera frustum, including occluded regions, which can be rendered from new viewpoints (next week's topic)

MonoRec is trained with the sparse 3D points of the SLAM system as targets. Its depth and mask modules help each other, so they are trained jointly. p. 370

2. Pose from two images, and why it is not enough

The same idea works for motion. A network \(f_\theta\) takes images \(I_1\) and \(I_2\) and outputs the relative pose \(\mathbf{T}^2_1 \in \SE(3)\). With ground-truth pose \(\mathbf{T}^{2*}_1\), the book's training loss is §13.1.2 · eq. (13.1) · p. 371

\[ \mathcal{L}_{pose} = \left\| \left(\mathbf{T}^2_1\right)^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{T}^{2*}_1 \right\|, \qquad \mathbf{T}^2_1 = f_\theta(I_1, I_2). \tag{13.1} \]

Filling the gap: reading equation (13.1)

If the prediction is exact, \((\mathbf{T}^2_1)^{-1}\mathbf{T}^{2*}_1\) is the identity. Otherwise it is the leftover transformation between prediction and truth. The book does not say which norm measures its size. A standard choice, in the language of Week 4 · Day 2, is the length of its tangent vector, \(\norm{\Log\!\big((\mathbf{T}^2_1)^{-1}\mathbf{T}^{2*}_1\big)}\), which is zero exactly at the identity.

Example: the network predicts a rotation of 10° about the vertical axis, the truth is 12° about the same axis, and the translations agree. The leftover is a rotation of 2°, and its tangent vector has length \(2 \cdot \pi / 180 = 0.035\) rad. The book mentions alternatives: penalize the rotation angle, or the distance between quaternions.

Early networks stacked the two frames and regressed axis-angle or Euler-angle parameters. Chaining relative poses gives a trajectory, and chained errors compound, as with any odometry (Week 1 · Day 1). DeepVO addressed this with a recurrent network (an LSTM) that reads a sequence of feature maps and outputs a sequence of poses. TartanVO improved generalization by giving the network predicted optical flow and the camera intrinsics as inputs. §13.1.2 · p. 371

The book's verdict is plain: predicting pose directly is often less accurate than classical SfM or SLAM, where the geometry is built into the system, and these networks tend to overfit to their training data. p. 371 That is the motivation for the rest of the chapter.

3. Training without 3D labels: view synthesis

Ground-truth depth and pose are expensive. View synthesis supplies a training signal from images alone. If you know the depth of every pixel of \(I_1\) and the relative pose, you know where each pixel of \(I_1\) appears in \(I_2\), so you can rebuild \(I_1\) out of colours taken from \(I_2\). If depth and pose are right, the rebuilt image matches \(I_1\). §13.1.3 · p. 371

Let \(\pi_c^{-1}(\mathbf{x}, d)\) be the inverse projection, which returns the 3D point at depth \(d\) along the ray of pixel \(\mathbf{x}\). A pixel \(\mathbf{x}^1\) with depth \(d^1\) in \(I_1\) lands in \(I_2\) at

\[ \mathbf{x}^2 = \pi_c\!\left( \mathbf{T}^2_1\, \pi_c^{-1}(\mathbf{x}^1, d^1) \right) = \omega_{1\to2}(\mathbf{x}^1, d^1), \tag{13.2} \]

ignoring occlusions. eq. (13.2) · p. 371 The synthesized image samples \(I_2\) at those locations:

\[ \tilde{I}_2(\mathbf{x}) = I_2\big(\omega_{1\to2}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}(\mathbf{x}))\big). \tag{13.3} \]

(As printed on p. 372, the right-hand side of (13.3) reads \(I_1(\cdot)\). The sentences around it describe warping \(I_2\) into the viewpoint of \(I_1\), using pixels sampled from \(I_2\), which is what is written here.) The loss compares \(\tilde{I}_2\) with \(I_1\) pixel by pixel. This is the photometric error of direct methods from Week 12 · Day 2, now used to train a network instead of to estimate a pose.

Filling the gap: (13.2) with numbers, and why interpolation makes it differentiable

Take \(f = 500\) px, \(c_x = 320\), and a pixel at \(x^1 = 420\) on the middle row with depth \(d^1 = 4\) m. Back-project: \(X = d\,(x^1 - c_x)/f = 4 \cdot 100/500 = 0.8\) m, so the point is \((0.8, 0, 4)\). Camera 2 sits \(b = 0.2\) m to the right with the same orientation, so in its frame the point is \((0.6, 0, 4)\). Project: \(x^2 = 500 \cdot 0.6/4 + 320 = 395\). The pixel moved by \(-25\) px. For this sideways motion the general rule is

\[ x^2 = x^1 - \frac{f\,b}{d}, \]

and \(500 \cdot 0.2 / 4 = 25\) checks. Had the network said \(d = 5\) m, the point would be \((1.0, 0, 5)\), then \((0.8, 0, 5)\), and \(x^2 = 400\): five pixels off.

The location \(x^2\) is rarely a whole number, so \(I_2\) is read by bilinear interpolation. In one dimension, at position \(p = a + t\) with integer \(a\) and \(0 \le t \lt 1\),

\[ I_2(p) = (1 - t)\, I_2[a] + t\, I_2[a+1], \qquad \frac{\partial I_2(p)}{\partial p} = I_2[a+1] - I_2[a]. \]

With \(I_2[395] = 0.40\), \(I_2[396] = 0.60\), and \(p = 395.25\), the value is \(0.45\) and the slope is \(0.20\) per pixel. The chain rule then reaches the depth: \(\partial x^2/\partial d = f b/d^2 = 100/16 = 6.25\) px per meter, so \(\partial \tilde{I}_2/\partial d = 0.20 \cdot 6.25 = 1.25\) per meter. Because that derivative exists, the gradient of the loss flows back into whatever produced \(d\), here a depth network, and likewise into a pose network. p. 372

Try it: move the guessed depth and watch the difference row, then press "Step downhill".

Notice that the loss has its minimum, zero, exactly at the true depth of 4 m, where the shift used for the warp equals the true shift of 6 px. From the starting guess of 2.5 m, two presses of "Step downhill" reach 4.00 m. Each press is one Gauss-Newton step on 1/d that uses the interpolation slope from the note above. The curve over candidate depths is also what one slice of a MonoRec-style cost volume holds.

The book credits Godard et al. with showing that this loss can train a depth network from stereo pairs, and later work with training a depth network and a pose network together from video. p. 372

4. DVSO and D3VO: predictions as factors

DVSO (Deep Virtual Stereo Odometry) takes a classical direct system, DSO, and adds a factor to it: a term that pulls the depth computed by DSO toward the depth map predicted by a network. That network was trained with the view-synthesis loss on stereo pairs, where the distance between the two cameras is known in meters, so its depths are metric. The result behaves as if a second camera were present, hence "virtual stereo": better reconstruction, more accurate and robust poses, and correct scale from one camera. §13.1.3 · p. 372

D3VO adds two more predictions. A pose network supplies relative motion between consecutive frames. And a network predicts uncertainty: where brightness is likely to stay constant between frames and where it is not. Direct methods assume a surface point keeps its brightness, which fails on shiny, metallic, or glass surfaces. D3VO down-weights the photometric residuals at those pixels. Depth and pose networks are trained self-supervised with view-synthesis losses, and their outputs enter the factor graph as additional terms. §13.1.3 · pp. 372–373 · Fig. 13.5

Intuition

A prediction used as a factor is advice, and a prediction used as the answer is an order. The factor graph can overrule advice when the images disagree with it. That is why "network plus factor graph" ends up more accurate than the network alone and more robust than the geometry alone.

Common mistakes

  • "Self-supervised means no geometry is used." The view-synthesis loss is built from projection, the relative pose, and depth. The geometry is in the loss, not in the labels.
  • "View synthesis from monocular video gives metric depth." The loss is unchanged if all depths and translations are scaled together. DVSO's network is metric because its training pairs came from a stereo rig with a known baseline. p. 372
  • "A pose network replaces the back-end." The book reports that direct pose prediction is often less accurate than classical methods and overfits. p. 371

Check yourself

Q1. A camera has f = 600 px. Camera 2 is 0.5 m to the right of camera 1 with the same orientation. A pixel has depth 10 m. By how many pixels does it shift between the images?

The shift is f·b/d = 600 · 0.5 / 10 = 30 px.

Q2. Image samples are I[7] = 0.2 and I[8] = 0.6. What does linear interpolation give at p = 7.75, and what is the derivative with respect to p?

0.25 · 0.2 + 0.75 · 0.6 = 0.05 + 0.45 = 0.5. The slope is I[8] − I[7] = 0.4 per pixel.

Q3. Why does MonoRec need a moving-object mask in addition to its cost volume?

Photometric consistency across frames only indicates depth if the point did not move. The mask removes moving points before they are lifted into the map (p. 370).

Q4. In D3VO, what is the predicted uncertainty used for?

The network predicts where colour is likely preserved across frames, and residuals elsewhere count less (p. 373). Scale comes from the depth predictions.

Recap

  1. Depth networks give monocular SLAM metric scale, robustness in degenerate cases, and dense maps.
  2. MonoRec adds a cost volume for consistency and a mask for moving objects. BTS predicts a density field that includes occluded space.
  3. Pose networks trained with (13.1) work, but are less accurate than geometry and overfit.
  4. View synthesis, (13.2) and (13.3), turns "depth and pose are right" into "the warped image matches", a loss that needs no 3D labels and is differentiable through bilinear interpolation.
  5. DVSO and D3VO add network predictions of depth, pose, and uncertainty to a direct SLAM system as factors and weights.
