Week 13 review: LiDAR SLAM from pulse to pose graph

After today you can go from a time-of-flight measurement to a registered scan, a place descriptor, and a loop-closed pose graph, and do the small calculations at each step by hand.

About 60 min · No new pages

The week in one paragraph

A LiDAR times laser pulses to get ranges and pairs them with beam angles to get a point cloud, dense nearby and sparse far away, gathered over a sweep of about 0.1 s. Scan registration finds the rigid motion between two clouds: in closed form by SVD when the pairs are known, and by ICP, alternating nearest-neighbour matching with alignment, when they are not. Point-to-plane, GICP, and NDT change what "distance" means. A LiDAR odometry system wraps registration with motion compensation, feature selection or fast search, and a local map, and drifts by roughly 0.1% of the distance travelled. Place recognition describes whole scans so that revisits can be detected without trusting the drifted pose. Verified loop closures enter a pose graph, whose optimization redistributes the error over the trajectory, after which the map is updated to match.

2. Mixed quiz

Check yourself

Q1. A surface is 45 m from a time-of-flight LiDAR. How long after emission does the echo arrive?

Δt = 2d/c = 90/(3×10⁸) = 3×10⁻⁷ s = 300 ns.

Q2. A wall facing a 3D LiDAR is moved from 10 m to 20 m. What happens to the number of points per square metre on it?

The gap between neighbours is rΔθ in both the horizontal and the vertical direction. Doubling r doubles both gaps, so the density falls by a factor of four.

Q3. In the closed-form alignment you find det(VUᵀ) = −1. What does that mean, and what do you do?

An orthogonal matrix with determinant −1 mirrors the cloud. Flipping the sign of the direction with the smallest singular value gives the best proper rotation.

Q4. Which statement about point-to-point ICP is true?

Matching picks the closest partner for the current estimate and alignment is an exact minimization, so the cost is monotone. Convergence is to a local minimum that depends on the starting guess.

Q5. A sensor turns at 20°/s without translating; one sweep takes 0.1 s. Uncompensated, about how far sideways is a point at 20 m range displaced if it was measured at the very end of the sweep?

The sensor has turned 2° = 0.0349 rad by the end of the sweep, and 20 m × 0.0349 = 0.70 m. Rotation errors grow with range.

Q6. A neighbourhood covariance has eigenvalues 0.9, 0.002, and 0.001. What kind of point is this, and which eigenvector is useful?

One dominant eigenvalue means the points lie along a line. Its eigenvector is the line direction.

Q7. A polar descriptor has 24 sectors. The robot revisits a place with its heading changed by 135°. Which column shift should give the best match?

One sector is 360°/24 = 15°, and 135°/15° = 9 columns (or 24 − 9 = 15 counted the other way round).

Q8. Four odometry steps have equal variance. An exact loop closure shows the total is 1.2 m too long. By how much does the pose reached after the second step move?

Each step is shortened by 1.2/4 = 0.3 m. The pose after two steps has accumulated two corrections: 0.6 m.

3. Pen-and-paper problems

  1. A 2D LiDAR has an angular step of 0.25° and spins at 10 Hz on a vehicle driving at 12 m/s. (a) How many points does one scan contain? (b) What is the echo delay for a wall at 60 m? (c) How far apart are neighbouring points on that wall if it faces the sensor? (d) How far does the vehicle move during one scan?

    Solution

    (a) 360/0.25 = 1440 points. (b) \(\Delta t = 2d/c = 120/(3\times10^8) = 4\times10^{-7}\) s = 400 ns. (c) \(s \approx r\Delta\theta = 60 \times 0.25 \times \pi/180 = 0.262\) m. (d) One scan takes 0.1 s, so 12 × 0.1 = 1.2 m. That is more than four times the point spacing at 60 m, which is why the scan has to be deskewed.

  2. Three pairs are given: \(\mathbf{q}_1 = (1, 0)\), \(\mathbf{q}_2 = (-1, 0)\), \(\mathbf{q}_3 = (0, 3)\) and \(\mathbf{p}_1 = (2, 0)\), \(\mathbf{p}_2 = (2, 2)\), \(\mathbf{p}_3 = (5, 1)\). Find the rotation angle \(\theta\) and translation \(\mathbf{t}\) with \(\mathbf{p}_i = \mathbf{R}(\theta)\mathbf{q}_i + \mathbf{t}\).

    Solution

    Centroids: \(\bar{\mathbf{q}} = (0, 1)\), \(\bar{\mathbf{p}} = (3, 1)\). Centred points: \(\mathbf{q}' = (1,-1),\ (-1,-1),\ (0, 2)\) and \(\mathbf{p}' = (-1,-1),\ (-1, 1),\ (2, 0)\).

    \(A = \sum \mathbf{q}'\cdot\mathbf{p}' = (-1+1) + (1-1) + (0+0) = 0\).

    \(B = \sum (q_x'p_y' - q_y'p_x') = (-1-1) + (-1-1) + (0-4) = -8\).

    \(\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(-8, 0) = -90^\circ\). A rotation by −90° sends \((x, y)\) to \((y, -x)\), so \(\mathbf{R}\bar{\mathbf{q}} = (1, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{t} = \bar{\mathbf{p}} - \mathbf{R}\bar{\mathbf{q}} = (2, 1)\). Check: \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{q}_3 + \mathbf{t} = (3, 0) + (2, 1) = (5, 1) = \mathbf{p}_3\).

  3. A 2D scan is matched to two walls with point-to-plane ICP. The current estimate is \(\mathbf{R}^0 = \mathbf{I}\), \(\mathbf{t}^0 = \mathbf{0}\), and the rotation is known to be correct, so only the translation update \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\) is solved for. Three pairs lie on a wall with normal \((1, 0)\) and have residual \(r^0 = 0.2\) m each. Two pairs lie on a wall with normal \((0, 1)\) and have \(r^0 = -0.1\) m each. (a) Find \(\boldsymbol{\rho}\). (b) What changes if the first wall is not visible?

    Solution

    (a) With \(\mathbf{R}^0 = \mathbf{I}\), \(\mathbf{m}_i = \mathbf{n}_i\) and the translation part of the Jacobian row is \(\mathbf{J}_i = -\mathbf{n}_i\T\). The normal equations are \(\big(\sum_i \mathbf{n}_i\mathbf{n}_i\T\big)\boldsymbol{\rho} = \sum_i \mathbf{n}_i r_i^0\):

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\rho} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \times 0.2 \\ 2 \times (-0.1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 \\ -0.2 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol{\rho} = (0.2, -0.1). \]

    Check: the new residuals are \(r_i^0 - \mathbf{n}_i\T\boldsymbol{\rho} = 0.2 - 0.2 = 0\) and \(-0.1 + 0.1 = 0\).

    (b) The matrix becomes \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), which is singular. The second component is still \(-0.1\), but the first is undetermined: sliding along the remaining wall changes no residual. This is the corridor degeneracy, and no number of extra points on that wall cures it.

  4. A robot makes four odometry steps \(u = (5, 5, 5, 5)\) m with variances \((0.01, 0.01, 0.04, 0.04)\) m². A loop closure measures the displacement from the first pose to the last as \(z = 19.0\) m with variance \(\sigma_L^2 = 0.02\) m². (a) Find the corrected poses. (b) How much disagreement is left on the loop-closure edge, and why is it not zero? (c) What happens as \(\sigma_L^2 \to \infty\)?

    Solution

    (a) \(e = z - \sum u_k = 19 - 20 = -1\). The denominator is \(0.01 + 0.01 + 0.04 + 0.04 + 0.02 = 0.12\), so

    \[ \delta = -1 \times \frac{(0.01, 0.01, 0.04, 0.04)}{0.12} = (-0.083, -0.083, -0.333, -0.333). \]

    The corrected steps are \(4.917, 4.917, 4.667, 4.667\), and the poses are \(0,\ 4.917,\ 9.833,\ 14.5,\ 19.167\).

    (b) The corrected total is 19.167 m, so the loop-closure edge keeps a residual of \(19.0 - 19.167 = -0.167\) m \(= e\,\sigma_L^2/0.12\). The loop closure is itself noisy, so it takes a share of the disagreement in proportion to its own variance, like every other edge.

    (c) All \(\delta_k \to 0\): a measurement with unbounded variance carries no information, and the odometry-only solution is kept. In the other limit, \(\sigma_L^2 \to 0\), the corrections sum to exactly \(e\).
