Today's big idea

LiDAR odometry is yesterday's ICP surrounded by three practical decisions: correct each point for the motion of the sensor during the sweep, decide which points to match and how to find their partners quickly, and register against a local map built from many recent scans instead of the previous scan alone. Done well, this drifts by about one metre per kilometre.

1. The pipeline

The goal of LiDAR odometry is the incremental motion of the sensor, in real time, from the current scan and past observations. §8.2 · p. 227 Figure 8.4 splits it into motion compensation, correspondence search, and relative pose estimation, with the last two iterating. §8.2.2 · p. 231 The diagram below adds the local map that the later sections introduce.

2. Motion compensation

A scan is gathered over a sweep of about 0.1 s while the sensor moves, so its points are measured from slightly different positions. Left alone, this gives a distorted cloud; the same effect in cameras is called rolling shutter. §8.2.2.1 · pp. 231–232 The fix is called motion compensation, undistortion, or deskewing.

Filling the gap: the deskewing formula

Let point \(k\) of the sweep be measured at time \(\tau_k \in [0, T]\) after the sweep started, with sensor-frame coordinates \(\mathbf{p}_k\). Let \(\mathbf{T}(\tau)\) be the sensor pose at that time. The point's world position is \(\mathbf{T}(\tau_k)\mathbf{p}_k\). To express every point in one frame, say the frame at the start of the sweep, apply

\[ \mathbf{p}_k^{\text{start}} = \mathbf{T}(0)^{-1}\,\mathbf{T}(\tau_k)\,\mathbf{p}_k . \]

Everything now depends on knowing the motion inside the sweep, \(\mathbf{T}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{T}(\tau)\). The constant-velocity model assumes the sensor keeps the velocity it had over the previous scan. If the last two estimated poses are \(\mathbf{T}_{j-1}\) and \(\mathbf{T}_j\), one scan period \(T\) apart, the body velocity (a twist, in the notation of Week 4) is

\[ \boldsymbol{\varpi} = \frac{1}{T}\,\Log\!\big(\mathbf{T}_{j-1}^{-1}\mathbf{T}_j\big), \qquad \mathbf{T}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{T}(\tau) = \Exp(\tau\,\boldsymbol{\varpi}), \qquad \mathbf{p}_k^{\text{start}} = \Exp(\tau_k\boldsymbol{\varpi})\,\mathbf{p}_k . \]

Numbers, in 2D. The sensor drives forward at \(v = 6\) m/s while turning at \(\omega = 90^\circ\)/s, and \(T = 0.1\) s. Take the point measured half-way, \(\tau_k = 0.05\) s, at range 5 m straight ahead: \(\mathbf{p}_k = (5, 0)\). By then the sensor has turned \(\theta = 4.5^\circ\) and moved along an arc of radius \(v/\omega = 3.82\) m to \(\big(3.82\sin\theta,\ 3.82(1-\cos\theta)\big) = (0.300, 0.012)\) m. Rotating \((5,0)\) by 4.5° gives \((4.985, 0.392)\); adding the position gives \(\mathbf{p}_k^{\text{start}} = (5.284, 0.404)\). Without compensation the point would sit at \((5, 0)\), wrong by 0.49 m.

Try it: the sensor of Day 1 now drives and turns. Press "Motion compensation", then make the velocity estimate wrong.

Notice that at 6 m/s and 90°/s the sensor moves 0.60 m and turns 9° during one sweep, and the raw points are 24 cm (RMS) away from the walls. With the correct velocity, compensation brings them back to under 1 cm, which is the range noise. With a velocity estimate that is 20% off, about 5 cm of distortion remains.

3. Features: keep the edges and the planes

A feature-based system does not match every point. It extracts a small number of informative ones and computes correspondences and residuals only for those, which cuts the cost. §8.2.2.2 · pp. 232–233 The usual features are points on edges and on planes. LOAM finds them from a curvature score computed from the differences between a point and its neighbours: high curvature marks an edge, low curvature a plane. §8.2.2.2 · p. 233 The second standard tool is principal component analysis of a point's neighbourhood. §8.2.2.2 · pp. 233–234

Filling the gap: what the eigenvalues say

Take the \(K\) points \(\mathbf{x}_j\) around a point, with mean \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}\), and form their covariance

\[ \mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K} (\mathbf{x}_j - \bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_j - \bar{\mathbf{x}})\T . \]

\(\mathbf{C}\) is symmetric, so it has orthogonal eigenvectors, and the eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of a unit eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}\) is the variance of the points along \(\mathbf{v}\), because \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v} = \frac1K\sum_j (\mathbf{v}\T(\mathbf{x}_j-\bar{\mathbf{x}}))^2\). Sort \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \lambda_3\) and read off the shape:

For a plane, the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_3\) is the surface normal: the direction in which the points vary least. That is where yesterday's \(\mathbf{n}_i\) comes from. The same ellipsoid, flattened into a disk, is a surfel. §8.2.2.2 · p. 234

Example in 2D. Five points on a wall: \((0, 0),\ (1, 0.02),\ (2, -0.02),\ (3, 0.01),\ (4, -0.01)\). The mean is \((2, 0)\). The variances are \(\tfrac15(4+1+0+1+4) = 2\) along \(x\) and \(\tfrac15(0 + 0.0004 + 0.0004 + 0.0001 + 0.0001) = 0.0002\) along \(y\), with covariance \(\tfrac15(-0.02 + 0.01 - 0.02) = -0.006\). The eigenvalues are close to 2 and 0.0002, a ratio of ten thousand: a clear line (a wall, in 2D), with normal almost exactly along \(y\).

The book also lists higher-level features: semantic labels that separate static from moving objects, surfels for point-to-plane distances, and intensity, which helps where the geometry has no structure. §8.2.2.2 · p. 234

4. Direct methods and fast search

Features have costs. They discard isolated points that are neither edge nor plane, which hurts among bushes and branches. Their detectors need retuning for each sensor. And the processing load still grows with the size of the scan, which becomes a problem for 64- or 128-beam sensors. §8.2.2.3 · p. 234 A direct method aligns the points themselves, ICP-style, as direct visual methods align pixels (Week 12). What held it back was the cost of the correspondence search. §8.2.2.3 · p. 235

Filling the gap: what the search costs

Equation (8.3) needs, for each of \(N\) scan points, the nearest of \(M\) map points. Checking all of them takes \(N \times M\) distance computations per ICP iteration: for \(N = 10^5\) and \(M = 10^6\) that is \(10^{11}\), far too many at 10 Hz. Two data structures fix this (see Week 8 · Day 3):

  • A k-d tree splits space recursively, so a query descends about \(\log_2 M \approx 20\) levels: roughly \(N\log_2 M = 2\times10^{6}\) steps instead of \(10^{11}\). The incremental k-d tree of Fast-LIO2 can add and remove points and rebalance itself, so the tree is not rebuilt for every scan. §8.2.2.3 · p. 235
  • A voxel grid stores points in a hash table keyed by the integer cell \(\lfloor \mathbf{x}/s \rfloor\) for voxel size \(s\). A query looks only in the cell of the point and its neighbours, a constant amount of work. It may miss the exact nearest neighbour, which the book accepts as an approximation. §8.2.1.2 · p. 230

5. Scan-to-scan or scan-to-map

The first systems registered each scan to the one before it. A single scan is sparse, so many points have no good partner in the previous scan, the registration errors add up, and the map becomes inconsistent. The modern alternative keeps a local map around the sensor and registers each scan to it. §8.2.2.4 · p. 235

AspectScan-to-scanScan-to-map
ReferenceThe previous scan onlyA local map accumulated from many scans
CorrespondencesMany points lack a suitable partnerThe map is much denser, so more pairs are true inliers
Error growthEvery registration adds its own errorEach scan is tied to a map that many scans agree on; lower drift

The starting guess for the fine registration is a motion prediction from scan-to-scan odometry or IMU pre-integration. LOAM interleaved fast scan-to-scan odometry with slower scan-to-map registration; newer systems use a single scan-to-map stage with direct point-wise correspondences in a voxelized local map. Learned LiDAR odometry exists too, with open questions about generalization. §8.2.2.4 · pp. 235–236

Put together, these systems reach drift rates on the order of 1 m per 1000 m travelled, that is 0.1%, depending on the environment and on how dynamic the scene and the sensor motion are. §8.2.3 · p. 236 After a 5 km drive that is still about 5 m of error, and it only grows. Removing it is the job of place recognition, tomorrow.

Common mistakes

  • "Deskewing needs the motion, and the motion is what we are estimating, so it is circular." It only needs a prediction of the motion inside one sweep, from the previous step or an IMU. The registration then refines the pose between sweeps.
  • "More points always give better odometry." Points that match badly, on moving objects or with no partner in the reference, hurt. Features, down-sampling, and a dense local map all aim at good pairs, not many pairs.

Check yourself

Q1. A vehicle drives straight at 10 m/s without turning. A point is measured 0.08 s after the sweep began. If the scan is not compensated, by how much is that point misplaced in the start-of-sweep frame?

The sensor has moved 10 × 0.08 = 0.8 m by then. With no rotation, the correction is exactly that translation.

Q2. The covariance of a point's neighbourhood has eigenvalues 0.50, 0.46, and 0.001 (in m²). What is the point, and what does the eigenvector of 0.001 give you?

Two similar large eigenvalues and one tiny one mean the points spread over a flat patch. They vary least along the normal, which is the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue.

Q3. Why does registering to a local map drift less than registering to the previous scan?

A single scan is sparse, so scan-to-scan pairs are often poor and each step's error accumulates. A local map supplies better inlier associations. It contains no loop closures and still needs an initial guess and deskewed input.

Q4. A handheld scanner is shaken while walking. Which motion-compensation method does the book recommend for such jerky motion, and what must be watched?

The constant-velocity assumption breaks for high-frequency motion. An IMU measures it directly at a high rate, but its noise, bias, and timing errors can make it worse than simpler methods if handled carelessly.

Recap

  1. A LiDAR odometry pipeline deskews the scan, searches correspondences, and estimates the pose, repeating the last two until convergence.
  2. Deskewing moves each point by the sensor motion at its own time stamp: p(start) = T(0)⁻¹ T(τ) p, with the in-sweep motion from a constant-velocity model, a continuous-time trajectory, or an IMU.
  3. Edges and planes are found from curvature or from the eigenvalues of the neighbourhood covariance; for a plane, the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue is the normal.
  4. Direct methods match all points and depend on fast search structures: k-d trees, incremental k-d trees, voxel grids.
  5. Scan-to-map registration against a dense local map gives drift of about 0.1% of distance travelled, which still grows without bound.
