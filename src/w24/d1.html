Today's big idea

The chapter treats Spatial AI as SLAM grown up: a device keeps a persistent, close-to-metric model of its surroundings and checks every new piece of sensor data against it. The hard part is doing this within the size, cost, and power budget of a real product.

1. The gap between the task and the budget

Chapter 18, by Andrew Davison, has no equations and argues for a position. Spatial AI is the capability of a device to build rich but efficient representations of its surroundings that let it act intelligently. The aim is the right representation for real-time interpretation and action; scene understanding in the abstract is not the goal. §18.1.1 · p. 521 A design is squeezed between what the task needs and what the device can carry. §18.1.1 · pp. 521–522

DeviceThe task requiresThe setting allows
Household robotClean and know when it is clean; recognize, move, and tidy objects; make way for peopleConsumer price, size, safety, limited power

A waking day of 16 hours at 1 W is \(16\ \text{Wh} = 57.6\ \text{kJ}\) for cameras, processing, and display together. Today's robot-vision processors are far from that, and robust performance is hard even with bulky sensors and unlimited computing. §18.1.1 · p. 522

2. Representation, also called a world model

A representation is the set of values in memory that stand for the state of the device and the world around it. It persists, it changes as information arrives or the world changes, and parts of it may be abstracted or forgotten to save resources. §18.1.2 · p. 522 Machine learning calls this a world model. The idea looks new there because most networks are stateless: input in, output out, nothing kept. Networks trained end to end from sensor to action do well on short tasks and struggle on long ones. A map is the oldest fix: a robot vacuum can guarantee full coverage because it remembers where it has been. §18.1.2 · pp. 522–523

Hypothesis 1. A device that must run for a long time, do many tasks (some unknown at design time), and communicate with people should keep a scene representation that is general, persistent, close to metric 3D geometry at least locally, and understandable by a human. This excludes single-task representations and opaque learned states. The reasons given are efficiency, composability, interpretability, and generality. §18.1.2 · p. 523

Hypothesis 2. The usefulness of such a system across many tasks is captured by a small number of performance measures, so a drone, a tidying robot, and an AR display would run largely the same system, tuned by a few numbers. §18.1.2 · pp. 523–524 Day 5 returns to this.

A Spatial AI system, for the chapter, is a module that builds and maintains such a representation incrementally, in real time, from mainly visual input, with quantifiable performance. §18.1.2 · p. 524

Real-time visual SLAM has grown richer in steps, and you have met each one. §18.1.3 · p. 524

A new representation is almost never bolted onto a working system. It changes the whole system: once a dense map exists the tracker can align whole images against it (Week 12 · Day 2), and labels can improve the reconstruction. §18.1.3 · pp. 524–525 Hence the chapter's working assumption: today's SLAM systems are the best guide to the structure of tomorrow's Spatial AI.

4. The overall computational structure

Section 18.2 lists six properties of any such system. §18.2 · p. 525

  1. Embedded: cameras and supporting sensors such as an IMU, with low-power processing, in a mobile body.
  2. Real-time model and pose, with geometry and semantics, mainly from on-board sensors.
  3. What is where: ideally the identity, position, shape, and motion of everything nearby.
  4. Close to metric, at least locally.
  5. Focused quality: full detail only where the device looks and will act; the rest at lower quality, upgradable on a revisit.
  6. Alert: every incoming piece of visual data is checked against a prediction from the model.

The quality the chapter singles out is the closed loop: the model is persistent, updated incrementally, and used inside the real-time loop for data association and tracking. The contrast is incremental estimation such as pure visual odometry, which estimates motion frame to frame and keeps no long-term structure. Closed loop does not mean one fixed rate for everything. It means each result is available when needed, so the whole never pauses. §18.2 · pp. 525–526

Filling the gap: why the stored map bounds the error

In the widget, a landmark at \(l_j\) seen from position \(x_k\) gives \(z_{kj} = l_j - x_k + n_{kj}\), with independent noise of variance \(\sigma^2\).

Odometry sums frame-to-frame motions, each the average change in \(z\) over the landmarks two frames share. While the shared set is unchanged the noise cancels in the sum. Each time a landmark enters or leaves the view, a term fails to cancel and a fresh error, a fraction of \(\sigma\) in size, is added for good. After \(h\) hand-overs the error variance has grown in proportion to \(h\): the random walk of Week 1 · Day 1.

The stored map holds landmark \(j\) as \(\hat{l}_j = l_j + e_j\), where \(e_j\) is the error at the moment it was stored. On any later frame with \(m\) landmarks in view,

\[ \hat{x}_k = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j}\bigl(\hat{l}_j - z_{kj}\bigr) = x_k + \frac{1}{m}\sum_j e_j - \frac{1}{m}\sum_j n_{kj}. \]

The first error term is frozen: it is the same on every return to those landmarks. The second is fresh noise of variance \(\sigma^2/m\). Neither depends on how long the system has run. With \(\sigma = 0.05\) and \(m \approx 6\) the fresh part is \(0.05/\sqrt{6} \approx 0.02\); the rest of the 0.04 is map error frozen in during the first sweep.

Section 18.2 ends with the route to efficiency: find the graphs of computation and data movement in the algorithms, and use hardware with the same graph so that data moves as little as possible. Three questions organize the rest of the week: what is stored where, what is processed where, and what is transmitted where and when. §18.2 · p. 526

Check yourself

Q1. Which system satisfies the chapter's first hypothesis about representation?

The hypothesis asks for a general, persistent representation, close to metric at least locally and understandable by a human. It excludes task-specific and opaque ones.

Q2. AR glasses must run 16 hours per charge at no more than 1 W. How much energy may the device use in a day?

16 h × 3600 s/h = 57 600 s at 1 J per second: 57.6 kJ, or 16 Wh.

Q3. A camera tracks against a stored map with 4 landmarks in view and measurement noise σ = 0.08. What is the standard deviation of the fresh, frame-to-frame part of the position error?

The fresh part averages 4 independent noises: σ/√m = 0.08/2 = 0.04.

Q4. A system estimates camera motion between consecutive frames very accurately and stores nothing else. What does it most clearly lack?

This is pure visual odometry. With no model to predict from, it cannot detect change or bound its drift.

Recap

  1. Spatial AI builds representations for real-time action, within a product's limits on size, cost, and power.
  2. Hypothesis 1: keep a general, persistent, close-to-metric, human-readable scene representation. Hypothesis 2: a few performance measures describe its usefulness.
  3. The defining structure is a closed loop: every measurement is compared with a prediction from a persistent model, which is then updated.
