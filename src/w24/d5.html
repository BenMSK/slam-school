Today's big idea

A Spatial AI system produces many outputs under several resource limits, so no single number ranks two systems. The chapter proposes a short list of metrics covering accuracy, robustness, latency, scene understanding, and resources, and it expects choices to be made by looking at trade-off curves. It also trusts a live demo more than any table.

1. What is measured today, and against what

Section 18.8 returns to the hypothesis of Day 1 that a few measures capture usefulness. Most evaluation today is of localization accuracy: a motion-capture system provides an external pose that is treated as ground truth, and the SLAM estimate is compared with it. §18.8 · p. 545 That is the absolute and relative trajectory error of Week 11 · Day 1. Everything else is harder to check.

2. Why the chapter distrusts benchmarks, and keeps them anyway

The criticism is blunt. Too much effort goes into squeezing a few percentage points out of a dataset. A SLAM benchmark fixes the scene type, the sensors and their placement, the frame rate, and the resolution, and then scores mostly accuracy, while efficiency and robustness, which matter at least as much, go unmeasured. Papers often report only the sequences on which their method basically works. §18.8 · p. 546

Against this the chapter sets the live demo. Playing with a visual SLAM system for a minute, watching its visualization and pushing it around, tells you more than its score. Progress is better read from the line of open real-time systems people could try for themselves: MonoSLAM, PTAM, KinectFusion, LSD-SLAM, ORB-SLAM, OKVIS, SVO, ElasticFusion, DROID-SLAM, Gaussian Splatting SLAM, MASt3R-SLAM. §18.8 · p. 546

Should we benchmark at all? The answer given is yes, with two changes: broaden what a benchmark measures, and trust it less. §18.8 · p. 546

3. Several objectives at once: the Pareto front

SLAMBench was a first step toward judging a whole system. It took one algorithm, KinectFusion, and measured both its accuracy and its computational cost across processor platforms and language implementations. The chapter wants benchmarks to move toward measures that predict performance on real tasks. That gives a set of objectives that pull against each other, and it says the analysis of Pareto fronts will let a designer choose for a particular application. §18.8 · p. 546

Filling the gap: what a Pareto front is and how to find it

The book uses the term without defining it. Score every candidate system on each metric, with lower being better. Candidate \(A\) dominates candidate \(B\) if \(A\) is at least as good as \(B\) on every metric and strictly better on at least one. Nobody should ever pick a dominated candidate. The Pareto front is the set of candidates that nothing dominates. Among those, improving one metric always costs you on another, so the choice depends on the application.

Five configurations, scored as (power in W, drift in %):

\[ A = (1,\ 3.0), \quad B = (2,\ 2.0), \quad C = (3,\ 2.5), \quad D = (5,\ 1.0), \quad E = (6,\ 1.5). \]

\(C\) is dominated by \(B\): \(B\) uses less power (2 against 3) and drifts less (2.0 against 2.5). \(E\) is dominated by \(D\) in the same way. \(A\), \(B\), and \(D\) survive: going from \(A\) to \(B\) to \(D\) buys accuracy with watts each time. The front is \(\{A, B, D\}\).

To choose, add a constraint from the product. With a budget of 4 W the candidates are \(A\) and \(B\), and \(B\) has the lower drift. For two metrics the front is found by sorting by power and keeping each candidate whose drift is lower than every cheaper one's.

Try it: forty synthetic configurations of one system, scored on power and drift. Move the power budget.

Notice that only 10 of the 40 configurations are on the front; each of the other 30 is beaten on both metrics by something else. At a 1 W budget the best choice uses 0.49 W and drifts 1.73%. Raise the budget to 2 W and it drops to 1.06%. From there the curve is nearly flat: at 20 W the best is 0.61%. Where the front bends tells you what a watt is worth.

The book names these without formulas. Three small calculations show what they would look like. The definitions are natural ones chosen for this lesson; the book does not fix them.

  • Drift rate. End-point error divided by distance travelled, with no loop closures. An error of 0.5 m after 100 m is 0.5%.
  • Tracking robustness. The share of frames on which the tracker held. Tracked on 5 820 of 6 000 frames is 97%.
  • Data movement. Bits sent times the distance they travel. A camera that sends a 1 Mbit frame along a 50 mm link moves \(10^6 \times 50 = 5\times10^7\) bit·mm per frame. If pixel-level processing lets it send 10 kbit of differences, that is \(5\times10^5\), a hundred times less.

5. Where the book says the field is going

Section 18.9 closes the chapter in two moves. First, capability has to rise while the resources it needs fall, and that takes joined-up thinking about algorithms, processors, and sensors: find the computational structure, design for sparse graph patterns in both algorithms and storage, and fit them to the new hardware. Second, hand-designed estimation and machine learning will both stay, and will converge. Estimation systems are gaining learned parts and learned networks are gaining structure and domain knowledge, and the chapter expects both routes to arrive at the same place with the same questions about computational structure. §18.9 · p. 547

Common mistakes

  • "The system with the lowest trajectory error is the best system." It may use ten times the power, fail on half the sequences nobody reported, or respond too late. Accuracy is one of twelve.
  • "A point on the Pareto front is the optimum." Every point on the front is optimal for some trade-off. The application's constraints pick one.

Check yourself

Q1. Four configurations are scored as (power, drift): P = (1 W, 4%), Q = (2 W, 2%), R = (3 W, 3%), S = (4 W, 1%). Which one is dominated?

Q uses less power than R and drifts less, so Q dominates R. P, Q, and S each beat the others on one metric and lose on the other, so they form the front.

Q2. With no loop closures, a visual odometry system is 1.2 m from the truth after travelling 300 m. What is its drift rate?

1.2 / 300 = 0.004, which is 0.4% of the distance travelled.

Q3. A sensor sends 500 kbit per frame over a 40 mm link. Near-sensor processing cuts that to 5 kbit. What is the data movement per frame afterwards?

5 000 bits × 40 mm = 2 × 10⁵ bit·mm. Before, it was 500 000 × 40 = 2 × 10⁷, a hundred times more.

Q4. Why does the chapter rate a one-minute live demo above a benchmark score?

Datasets capture limited aspects of performance, mostly accuracy, under fixed assumptions. Interacting with a running system shows how it behaves when pushed.

Recap

  1. Pose accuracy against motion capture is the usual benchmark. Ground truth for dense geometry and semantics is costly, so synthetic data and crowdsourcing fill in.
  2. Benchmarks fix too many assumptions and reward accuracy over robustness and efficiency. The chapter keeps them, broadened, and trusts a live demo more.
  3. With several objectives there is no single winner. The Pareto front is the set of undominated candidates, and the application's limits choose among them.
  4. The twelve proposed metrics span localization, robustness, latency, geometry, semantics, AR registration, change detection, power, and data movement in bits × millimetres.
  5. The book's closing view: design algorithms, processors, and sensors together around sparse graph structure, expect estimation and learning to converge, trust the math, and test on real systems.
