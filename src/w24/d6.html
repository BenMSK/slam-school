Week 24 review: the computational structure, Part III, and the whole course

After today you can work Gaussian belief propagation and the week's other calculations by hand, place every chapter of Part III in one table, and draw the path from a factor graph to a Spatial AI system from memory.

About 60 min · No new pages

The week in one paragraph

Chapter 18 argues that Spatial AI is SLAM continued: a device keeps a persistent, close-to-metric world model and checks every measurement against it in a closed loop. Because products limit size, cost, and power, and because single-core speed stopped rising when Dennard scaling failed, the computation has to be spread over many simple units with little data movement. The design gives each graph in the problem matching hardware: a self-maintaining map store, a real-time loop, and processing at the sensor. Gaussian belief propagation is the estimation algorithm that fits, since it needs only local messages and tolerates any schedule, and it can carry learning in the same factor graph. Success is to be judged on a dozen metrics at once, read as trade-offs.

2. Mixed quiz: Week 24

Check yourself

Q1. Which of these is not one of the six properties of a Spatial AI system in §18.2?

Property 5 says the opposite: full quality only where the device is looking and about to act, with the rest stored at lower quality levels that can be upgraded on a revisit.

Q2. A camera tracks against a stored map with 9 landmarks in view and measurement noise σ = 0.06. What is the standard deviation of the fresh noise in its position estimate?

Averaging 9 independent measurements divides σ by √9: 0.06/3 = 0.02.

Q3. An estimate is stored as (η, Λ) = (40, 20). A new measurement z = 5 with variance 0.2 arrives. What is the new mean?

The measurement adds Λ = 1/0.2 = 5 and η = 5/0.2 = 25. New pair: (65, 25), mean 65/25 = 2.6.

Q4. Under ideal Dennard scaling with k = 3, by what factor does the switching power of one transistor change?

C → C/3, V² → V²/9, f → 3f, so P → P × (1/3)(1/9)(3) = P/9. Nine times as many transistors fit per area, so power density is unchanged.

Q5. Two pixel processors are linked only to each other and hold 2 and 8. With the averaging rule of Day 3, what do they hold after one round?

Each has one neighbour, so the weight is 1/(1 + 1) = 1/2. The first becomes 2 + (8 − 2)/2 = 5 and the second 8 + (2 − 8)/2 = 5.

Q6. An odometry factor has w = 1/σ² = 3. The message arriving from one pose has Λ = 6. What is Λ of the message the factor sends to the other pose?

Λ = wΛin/(w + Λin) = 18/9 = 2. In variances: 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2.

Q7. In GBP Learning, what plays the part of a neural network's weights?

Parameters are variables like any others; inputs, outputs, and activations are variables copied per observation. Inference on the graph is the learning.

Q8. Four configurations, as (power, drift): A = (1 W, 3.0%), B = (2 W, 2.2%), C = (4 W, 1.4%), D = (8 W, 1.3%). With a 5 W budget, which do you pick for the lowest drift?

All four are on the Pareto front. D is over budget, and among A, B, C the lowest drift is C at 1.4%.

3. Pen-and-paper problems

  1. Three poses on a line. Prior: \(x_0 = 0\), variance 0.04. Odometry: \(x_1 - x_0 = 1.0\) and \(x_2 - x_1 = 1.2\), each with variance 0.09. Run GBP by hand and give the mean and variance of each belief. Why do the messages travelling back toward \(x_0\) carry nothing?

    Solution

    The prior gives \(x_0\): mean 0, variance 0.04. Through the first odometry factor the mean gains the step and the variances add: \(x_1\) has mean 1.0, variance \(0.04 + 0.09 = 0.13\). Through the second: \(x_2\) has mean 2.2, variance \(0.13 + 0.09 = 0.22\).

    The message from \(x_2\) to the second odometry factor is \(x_2\)'s belief minus what that factor sent, and \(x_2\) has no other factor, so it is zero. A factor that receives zero information sends zero onward (\(\Lambda = w\cdot 0/(w+0) = 0\)). With no measurement downstream there is nothing to report upstream.

  2. Add a position fix to Problem 1: \(x_2 = 2.0\) with variance 0.02. Compute the new beliefs of \(x_2\) and \(x_1\).

    Solution

    Belief of \(x_2\). Two incoming messages: from odometry (mean 2.2, variance 0.22), so \((\eta,\Lambda) = (10,\ 4.545)\); from the fix, \((100,\ 50)\). Sum: \((110,\ 54.545)\). Mean \(110/54.545 = 2.017\), variance \(1/54.545 = 0.0183\).

    Message back to \(x_1\). \(x_2\) sends the second odometry factor its belief minus that factor's message, which leaves the fix: mean 2.0, variance 0.02. The factor passes it on with the step subtracted and the variances added: mean \(2.0 - 1.2 = 0.8\), variance \(0.02 + 0.09 = 0.11\).

    Belief of \(x_1\). Forward message (mean 1.0, variance 0.13) is \((7.692,\ 7.692)\); backward message (mean 0.8, variance 0.11) is \((7.273,\ 9.091)\). Sum: \((14.965,\ 16.783)\). Mean \(0.892\), variance \(0.0596\). Solving the \(3\times3\) system \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\mu} = \boldsymbol{\eta}\) directly gives the same numbers, because the graph has no loop.

  3. Four pixel processors sit in a ring, each linked to its two neighbours, and hold 0, 4, 8, 4. Using the averaging rule with \(w_{ij} = 1/(1+\max(d_i,d_j))\), find the values after one round, and the number of rounds until every pixel is within 0.05 of the mean.

    Solution

    Every pixel has degree 2, so every weight is \(1/3\). The mean is \(16/4 = 4\).

    Round 1: pixel 1 becomes \(0 + \tfrac13(4-0) + \tfrac13(4-0) = 2.67\). Pixel 3 becomes \(8 + \tfrac13(4-8) + \tfrac13(4-8) = 5.33\). Pixels 2 and 4 see one neighbour 4 below and one 4 above and stay at 4. The values are 2.67, 4, 5.33, 4, and the sum is still 16.

    The deviations from the mean went from \((-4, 0, 4, 0)\) to \((-1.33, 0, 1.33, 0)\): each round multiplies them by \(1/3\). After \(t\) rounds the largest deviation is \(4/3^t\). It is \(4/27 = 0.148\) at \(t = 3\) and \(4/81 = 0.049\) at \(t = 4\). Four rounds.

  4. (Stretch.) Show with numbers that one forward step of GBP on a pose chain is one predict-and-update step of a Kalman filter. Prior: \(x_0\) has mean 0, variance 1. Odometry: \(x_1 - x_0 = 1\), variance 1. A position fix: \(x_1 = 3\), variance 1.

    Solution

    Kalman filter. Predict: mean \(0 + 1 = 1\), variance \(1 + 1 = 2\). Update with the fix: gain \(K = 2/(2+1) = 2/3\), mean \(1 + \tfrac23(3 - 1) = 2.33\), variance \((1 - \tfrac23)\cdot 2 = 0.667\).

    GBP. The odometry factor's message to \(x_1\) is the prediction: mean 1, variance 2, so \((\eta,\Lambda) = (0.5,\ 0.5)\). The fix sends \((3,\ 1)\). The belief is the sum, \((3.5,\ 1.5)\): mean \(3.5/1.5 = 2.33\), variance \(1/1.5 = 0.667\).

    The factor-to-variable message is the predict step, and adding the fix's message is the update step written in information form. Messages sent backward along the chain then turn the filter into a smoother. Compare Week 1 · Day 3.

5. The whole course on one page

Read the map as a sentence. A factor graph states what depends on what (Week 2); Gaussian noise turns it into least squares, and sparsity makes that solvable at scale (Week 3). Each sensor of Part II adds its own residual to the same graph, from reprojection error (Week 11) to preintegrated IMU increments (Week 16). Part III keeps the graph and changes what hangs on it. This week asks the graph to run itself, by passing messages.

6. Course quiz: Weeks 1–24

Check yourself: the whole course

Q1 (Week 2). With Gaussian noise on every factor, what is MAP inference on a factor graph equivalent to?

The negative log of a product of Gaussians is a sum of squared Mahalanobis distances, which is a nonlinear least-squares problem.

Q2 (Week 3). Why can a poor elimination order make sparse Cholesky slow?

Each elimination produces a factor on the variable's separator. A bad order makes separators large, so the triangular factor fills with nonzeros. The answer itself is the same in any order.

Q3 (Week 4). In the book's convention, how does Gauss-Newton apply a computed step ξ to a pose T?

Poses are not vectors. The step is computed in the tangent space and applied by the exponential map on the right, which keeps T a valid pose.

Q4 (Week 6). Half of the matches are inliers and a minimal sample needs 3 matches. About how many RANSAC iterations give a 99% chance of drawing at least one all-inlier sample?

An all-inlier sample has probability 0.5³ = 0.125. N = log(0.01)/log(1 − 0.125) = 4.605/0.1335 ≈ 34.5, so 35.

Q5 (Week 9). Shor's relaxation of a pose-graph problem returns the value d*. The true optimum is p*. What is guaranteed?

A relaxation enlarges the feasible set, so its minimum is a lower bound. If the relaxed solution is rank one it is feasible for the original problem, so it is the global optimum.

Q6 (Week 12). In bundle adjustment, why are the landmarks eliminated first with the Schur complement?

No factor joins two landmarks, so the landmark part of the information matrix is block diagonal. Eliminating it gives the reduced camera system.

Q7 (Week 13). What does one iteration of point-to-point ICP do?

ICP alternates data association by nearest neighbour with a closed-form alignment (by SVD), and repeats until the transform stops changing.

Q8 (Week 15). When does a pixel of an event camera emit an event?

Each pixel works independently and asynchronously and reports the time and sign of a brightness change. A static scene seen by a static camera produces almost no data.

Q9 (Week 17). How many directions of the state are unobservable for visual-inertial odometry in general motion?

The accelerometer sees gravity, which fixes roll and pitch, and makes scale observable. Global position and yaw are never measured and remain free.

Q10 (Week 20). In what order does 3D Gaussian splatting produce a pixel?

Splatting rasterizes: project, sort, blend. Sampling an MLP along each ray is NeRF's volume rendering.

Q11 (Week 23). What makes a map "open-vocabulary"?

A closed-set map holds labels from a list chosen at training time. An open-vocabulary map holds embeddings that can be compared with any text query later.

Q12 (Weeks 3 and 24). What do variable elimination and Gaussian belief propagation have in common?

The factor-to-variable message is a local elimination. Elimination applies it once in a global order and is exact; GBP applies it everywhere repeatedly, with exact means at convergence.

7. Where to go next

  1. Build one toy system end to end. This is the Epilogue's advice. A good first one: a 2D pose graph with simulated odometry and loop closures, solved by Gauss-Newton (Week 2 · Day 4), then given outliers and a robust loss (Week 6 · Day 4), then solved again by GBP (Day 4) and compared.
  2. Use a real library. The book points to GTSAM in Chapter 1, and to Theseus and PyPose for differentiable optimization in Chapter 4. Rebuild your toy system in one of them.
  3. Run a full system on a live camera. §18.8 lists open real-time systems, from ORB-SLAM to MASt3R-SLAM (p. 546). Push it until it fails and write down how it failed.
  4. Follow the pointers in the "Further Readings & Recent Trends" section that ends almost every chapter.
  5. Pick an open problem. The Part III prelude lists where SLAM is not solved: underwater, on other planets, in disaster zones, in dense city traffic (p. 361).
