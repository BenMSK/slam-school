Gaussian belief propagation: estimation by local messages, and learning in the same graph

After today you can derive the message rules of Gaussian belief propagation in information form, run them by hand on a small pose chain, relate them to variable elimination, and say what GBP Learning proposes.

Today's big idea

The book gives no equations for Gaussian belief propagation, so today supplies them. There are three rules, each using only what one node and its neighbours hold: a variable adds up the messages it receives, tells each factor everything it knows except what that factor told it, and a factor answers by eliminating its other variables. On a tree this is variable elimination done in parallel. On a graph with loops you repeat it, and the means converge to the least-squares solution.

1. What the chapter asks of an algorithm

If computing and storage are spread over many small units (Day 3), estimation must live there too. The chapter's brief: keep the factor graph itself as the master representation, and work on it in place by local message passing with no "god's eye view", letting messages keep "bubbling" as the graph grows. §18.6 · pp. 540–541

Belief propagation (BP) has this character and tolerates arbitrary, asynchronous schedules. Gaussian belief propagation (GBP) is BP when every factor and variable is Gaussian, which covers least-squares estimation (Week 2 · Day 2). The book adds that it converges well on loopy graphs, those with cycles. §18.6 · p. 541

2. Two operations on Gaussians in information form

Write a Gaussian as \(\exp(-\tfrac12\mathbf{x}\T\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\eta}\T\mathbf{x})\) up to a constant, with information matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) and information vector \(\boldsymbol{\eta} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\mu}\) (Week 1 · Day 3). Multiplying is adding: a product of Gaussians over the same variable has \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_1 + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_2\) and \(\boldsymbol{\eta} = \boldsymbol{\eta}_1 + \boldsymbol{\eta}_2\). Marginalizing is a Schur complement (Week 3 · Day 3): to keep part \(a\) and remove part \(b\),

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{a}^{\text{marg}} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{aa} - \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{ab}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{ba}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\eta}_{a}^{\text{marg}} = \boldsymbol{\eta}_{a} - \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{ab}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{bb}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{b}. \]

A factor is written the same way. A linear measurement \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{x} + \text{noise}\) with covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) contributes \(\exp(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{J}\mathbf{x})\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{J}\mathbf{x}))\). Expand the square and drop the term without \(\mathbf{x}\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_f = \mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\eta}_f = \mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{z}. \]

For a one-dimensional odometry factor \(x_j - x_i = z\) with variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\mathbf{J} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) on \((x_i, x_j)\). With \(w = 1/\sigma^2\):

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_f = w\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\eta}_f = wz\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

3. The three rules

Every edge of the factor graph carries two messages, one each way. A message is a Gaussian over the variable on that edge, stored as \((\boldsymbol{\eta}, \boldsymbol{\Lambda})\). All start at zero, meaning "no information".

Rule 1, belief. A variable's estimate is the product of its incoming messages, a sum in information form:

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{b(x)} = \sum_{f \ni x} \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{f\to x}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\eta}_{b(x)} = \sum_{f \ni x} \boldsymbol{\eta}_{f\to x}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\mu}_x = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{b(x)}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{b(x)}. \]

Rule 2, variable to factor. A variable tells factor \(f\) what it has heard from its other factors, which is its belief minus what \(f\) sent:

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{x\to f} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{b(x)} - \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{f\to x}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\eta}_{x\to f} = \boldsymbol{\eta}_{b(x)} - \boldsymbol{\eta}_{f\to x}. \]

Without the subtraction, a factor's own information would be echoed back to it and counted twice.

Rule 3, factor to variable. To send to \(x_i\), the factor takes its own \((\boldsymbol{\eta}_f, \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_f)\), adds the incoming message from every other variable \(x_j\) to that variable's diagonal block and to its part of \(\boldsymbol{\eta}\), and marginalizes all the \(x_j\) out by the Schur complement. A Gaussian over \(x_i\) alone is left.

Filling the gap: rule 3 worked out for an odometry factor

Let the message arriving from \(x_i\) be \((\eta_{\text{in}}, \Lambda_{\text{in}})\), and send to \(x_j\). Adding it to the \(x_i\) entries of the factor gives

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}' = \begin{bmatrix} w + \Lambda_{\text{in}} & -w \\ -w & w \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\eta}' = \begin{bmatrix} -wz + \eta_{\text{in}} \\ wz \end{bmatrix}. \]

Remove \(x_i\) (part \(b\)) and keep \(x_j\) (part \(a\)):

\[ \Lambda_{f\to x_j} = w - \frac{w^2}{w + \Lambda_{\text{in}}} = \frac{w\,\Lambda_{\text{in}}}{w + \Lambda_{\text{in}}}, \qquad \eta_{f\to x_j} = wz + \frac{w(\eta_{\text{in}} - wz)}{w + \Lambda_{\text{in}}} = \frac{w\,(z\Lambda_{\text{in}} + \eta_{\text{in}})}{w + \Lambda_{\text{in}}}. \]

In covariance form the variance is \(1/\Lambda_{f\to x_j} = 1/\Lambda_{\text{in}} + 1/w = \sigma_{\text{in}}^2 + \sigma^2\) and the mean is \(\eta/\Lambda = \mu_{\text{in}} + z\). The message says: "\(x_j\) is where \(x_i\) thinks it is, plus the measured step, and the uncertainties add." The other direction gives mean \(\mu_{\text{in}} - z\). If nothing has arrived, \(\Lambda_{\text{in}} = 0\) and the outgoing message is zero too.

4. A full example by hand

Two poses. A prior says \(x_0 = 2\) with variance \(\tfrac14\). Odometry says \(x_1 - x_0 = 3\) with variance \(\tfrac12\), so \(w = 2\). A position fix says \(x_1 = 6\) with variance \(\tfrac14\).

StepMessage or belief\((\eta, \Lambda)\)Mean, variance
1prior → \(x_0\)\((8,\ 4)\)2, 1/4
2fix → \(x_1\)\((24,\ 4)\)6, 1/4
3odometry → \(x_1\), using \(x_0\)'s message \((8, 4)\)\(\left(\tfrac{2(3\cdot 4 + 8)}{6},\ \tfrac{2\cdot 4}{6}\right) = \left(\tfrac{20}{3},\ \tfrac43\right)\)5, 3/4
4odometry → \(x_0\), using \(x_1\)'s message \((24, 4)\)\(\left(4,\ \tfrac43\right)\)3, 3/4
5belief of \(x_0\) = step 1 + step 4\(\left(12,\ \tfrac{16}{3}\right)\)2.25, 3/16
6belief of \(x_1\) = step 2 + step 3\(\left(\tfrac{92}{3},\ \tfrac{16}{3}\right)\)5.75, 3/16

In step 4, \(x_1\) sends the odometry factor its belief minus that factor's message, which leaves the fix, \((24, 4)\). The factor reports mean \(6 - 3 = 3\) and variance \(\tfrac14 + \tfrac12\). Check against the direct solution:

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & -2 \\ -2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{\eta} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 - 6 \\ 6 + 24 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 30 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1} = \frac{1}{32}\begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \]

so \(\boldsymbol{\mu} = (72/32,\ 184/32) = (2.25,\ 5.75)\) and both variances are \(6/32 = 3/16\). The messages reproduced the means and the marginal variances exactly.

5. How this relates to elimination

Rule 3 is one step of variable elimination (Week 3 · Day 2): absorb what is known about a factor's other variables, then eliminate them. On a graph without loops, messages sent from the leaves inward are elimination in some order, and messages sent back outward are back-substitution that also delivers every marginal, so the result is exact. Elimination needs a global ordering and creates fill-in. GBP needs neither and never changes the graph, but on a loopy graph it must iterate.

In a loop, a variable's own message comes back round as if it were independent evidence. For Gaussian models this double counting distorts the variances but cancels in the means: Weiss and Freeman proved that whenever GBP converges, its means are exact.

GBP also takes the nonlinear and robust factors of real problems. §18.6 · p. 541 Both are local changes to a factor: a nonlinear factor is re-linearized about the current belief means (Week 2 · Day 4), and a robust factor shrinks its own \(w\) when its residual is large. The widget uses the Huber weight of Week 6 · Day 4: with the residual in standard deviations \(m\), multiply \(w\) by \(\beta/m\) when \(m \gt \beta = 1.5\). Turn on the outlier, a false loop closure claiming \(x_6 - x_2 = 2.5\) when the truth is 0. Least squares, and GBP with it, drags \(x_6\) to 2.66. With robust factors the outlier's weight falls to 0.27 and \(x_6\) settles at 2.22.

Two systems show the scale: bundle adjustment as GBP on a Graphcore IPU, 30 times faster than a CPU on small real problems, and Robot Web, where many robots shared one localization graph and stayed accurate despite asynchronous and dropped messages. §18.6 · pp. 541–542

6. Learning inside the factor graph

Section 18.7 wants learning that runs alongside probabilistic inference, is mainly self-supervised, is continual with no divide between training and test time, and is interpretable at the level of modules. §18.7 · p. 543 The chapter's answer is Bayesian machine learning: estimate a model's parameters by the same laws as any other unknown. In its words, state estimation and model learning differ only in structure and scale. §18.7 · p. 544

A weight is then just another variable. For a model with one weight, each observed input-output pair is a linear measurement of that weight and adds one term to its information pair, exactly as a sensor reading would. Nothing separates training from use.

GBP Learning (Nabarro and colleagues) scales this up with a factor graph shaped like a neural network. The parameters are variables shared across all observations; inputs, outputs, and activations are variables copied per observation; nonlinear factors between layers switch softly, as neurons do. Learning is then inference by ordinary GBP; the book reports a version that learned image denoising and classification. §18.7 · pp. 544–545, Figure 18.4

Check yourself

Q1. A pose sends an odometry factor a message with mean 4 and variance 0.5. The factor measured a step of 2 with variance 0.25. What does the factor send to the next pose?

The mean is the incoming mean plus the step, 4 + 2 = 6, and the variances add, 0.5 + 0.25 = 0.75.

Q2. A variable's belief is (η, Λ) = (10, 5). Factor f sent it (4, 2). What does the variable send back to f?

Rule 2: the belief minus what f itself sent, (10 − 4, 5 − 2) = (6, 3).

Q3. A chain of nine poses has a prior on the first pose and odometry between neighbours. With a synchronous schedule, after which iteration does the last pose first hold its exact marginal?

Iteration 1 delivers the prior to the first pose, and each later iteration carries information one pose further. A chain has no loops, so the result at iteration 9 is exact.

Q4. GBP has converged on a pose graph with several loop closures. Which statement is correct?

For Gaussian models, convergence implies exact means (Weiss and Freeman). Marginal variances are exact only on graphs without loops.

Recap

  1. The chapter wants the factor graph as master representation, updated in place by local messages.
  2. GBP has three rules: belief = sum of incoming messages; variable to factor = belief minus that factor's message; factor to variable = add incoming messages, then Schur-complement away the other variables.
  3. On a tree GBP is elimination plus back-substitution and is exact. With loops it iterates: the means converge to the least-squares solution and the variances are approximate.
  4. GBP Learning treats network weights as variables in the same graph, so learning becomes continual inference.
