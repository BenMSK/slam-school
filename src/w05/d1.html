Why continuous time, and trajectories built from splines

After today you can say when a trajectory is better stored as a function of time than as a list of poses, build a B-spline curve from its basis functions, and name the coefficients that one measurement touches.

Today's big idea

Instead of estimating one pose per measurement, estimate a small set of coefficients that define a smooth function \(\mathbf{p}(t)\), and evaluate that function at whatever instant a sensor fired. If the function is a sum of basis functions that are each nonzero for only a short time, every measurement still touches only a handful of unknowns, so the factor graph stays sparse.

1. One pose per measurement does not scale

Until now every measurement was attached to a pose variable at its own time stamp. That works when a camera delivers a few frames per second. It breaks down in three situations the book lists. §2.2 · pp. 62–63

  • High-rate sensors. Suppose an inertial measurement unit reports 200 times a second. One minute of driving would need 12,000 pose variables for that sensor alone.
  • Asynchronous sensors. A camera and a lidar rarely fire at the same instants, so each would need its own poses.
  • Motion-distorted sensors. A spinning lidar, a radar, or a rolling-shutter camera collects one "frame" over a stretch of time while the robot moves. Every point or pixel row has its own time stamp, and strictly each one was taken from a different pose.

A continuous-time trajectory is a function \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) that returns the pose at any time \(t\). It is defined by far fewer variables than there are measurements. It also lets you ask for the pose at times when nothing was measured: between measurements (interpolation) or, with caution, after the last one (extrapolation). §2.2 · p. 63

2. A trajectory as a weighted sum of basis functions

The parametric way to build \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) is to fix \(K\) known functions of time, the temporal basis functions \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t)\), and add them up with unknown weights, the coefficients \(\mathbf{c}_k\):

\[ \mathbf{p}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t)\,\mathbf{c}_k . \tag{2.36} \]

The coefficients are the estimation variables. The basis functions are chosen once and never change. For now \(\mathbf{p}\) is an ordinary vector, such as a position. Poses on a Lie group come on Day 5. §2.2.1 · p. 63 In the simplest case every component of \(\mathbf{p}\) uses the same scalar function \(\psi_k(t)\), so \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t) = \psi_k(t)\,\mathbf{I}\). The book makes that simplification itself in equation (2.53).

Here is the smallest useful example. Put knots at \(t = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) seconds and let \(\psi_k\) be a "hat" that is 1 at knot \(k\), falls linearly to 0 at the two neighbouring knots, and is 0 everywhere else. At \(t = 2.25\) only two hats are nonzero: \(\psi_2 = 0.75\) and \(\psi_3 = 0.25\). With \(c_2 = 4\) and \(c_3 = 8\),

\[ p(2.25) = 0.75 \cdot 4 + 0.25 \cdot 8 = 5 . \]

That is linear interpolation between the coefficients. The curve is continuous but has corners at the knots, so its velocity jumps. Smoother basis functions fix that.

3. B-splines and local support

A spline is a function made of polynomial pieces joined at knots. A basis function has local support if it is zero outside a short interval. §2.2.1 · p. 63 The standard family with both properties is the B-spline. The book names it but does not construct it, so here is the construction.

Filling the gap: the B-spline recursion

Measure time in knot intervals, so the knots are the integers. The B-spline of degree 0 that starts at knot \(k\) is a box:

\[ B_{k,0}(t) = \begin{cases} 1 & k \le t \lt k+1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Each higher degree is a blend of two neighbours of the degree below, with weights that are linear in \(t\). This is the Cox–de Boor recursion, written here for evenly spaced knots:

\[ B_{k,d}(t) = \frac{t-k}{d}\,B_{k,d-1}(t) + \frac{k+d+1-t}{d}\,B_{k+1,d-1}(t) . \]

Do one step by hand with \(k=0\), \(d=1\): \(B_{0,1}(t) = t\,B_{0,0}(t) + (2-t)\,B_{1,0}(t)\), which equals \(t\) on \([0,1)\) and \(2-t\) on \([1,2)\). That is the hat from section 2. Each step makes the function one interval wider and one derivative smoother:

Degree dNonzero overFunctions nonzero at any timeWhat is continuous at the knots
01 interval1nothing (the curve jumps)
1 (linear)2 intervals2position
2 (quadratic)3 intervals3position, velocity
3 (cubic)4 intervals4position, velocity, acceleration

Two more facts hold for every degree: the functions are never negative, and at any time the nonzero ones add up to exactly 1. So \(\mathbf{p}(t)\) is always a weighted average of the nearby coefficients.

For the cubic case, write \(u \in [0,1)\) for the fraction of the current knot interval that has passed. Running the recursion three times gives the four weights, from the oldest active coefficient to the newest:

\[ w_0 = \tfrac{1}{6}(1-u)^3,\quad w_1 = \tfrac{1}{6}(3u^3-6u^2+4),\quad w_2 = \tfrac{1}{6}(-3u^3+3u^2+3u+1),\quad w_3 = \tfrac{1}{6}u^3 . \]

Worked example. Take four active coefficients \(0, 3, 6, 3\) and the middle of the interval, \(u = 0.5\). The weights are \(0.0208,\ 0.4792,\ 0.4792,\ 0.0208\) (they sum to 1), so

\[ p = 0.0208\cdot 0 + 0.4792\cdot 3 + 0.4792\cdot 6 + 0.0208\cdot 3 = 4.375 . \]

Try it: move the time slider and watch which basis functions light up. Then drag a coefficient up and down. Change the degree and repeat.

Notice that at degree 3 exactly four basis functions are nonzero at any time (0.057, 0.590, 0.348, 0.004 at the starting position) and they sum to 1. Dragging a coefficient changes the curve only inside the shaded band, four knot intervals wide. The widget shifts each function so that function \(k\) peaks at \(t = k\).

In plain words

Think of a row of people holding a long flexible rod. Each person can lift or lower only the part of the rod near them. The coefficients are how high each person holds their hands. The rod is the trajectory. No single person decides the shape anywhere, and no person affects the rod far away.

4. What one measurement touches

Suppose the robot observes landmark \(\boldsymbol{\ell}\) at time \(t_i\). The measurement model is the same as before, except that the pose is read off the trajectory at that instant: §2.2.1 · pp. 63–64

\[ \mathbf{z}_i = \mathbf{h}_i\big(\mathbf{p}(t_i), \boldsymbol{\ell}\big) + \boldsymbol{\eta}_i = \mathbf{h}_i\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K} \boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t_i)\,\mathbf{c}_k,\ \boldsymbol{\ell}\Big) + \boldsymbol{\eta}_i . \tag{2.37, 2.38} \]

The sum looks like it involves all \(K\) coefficients. It does not: with local support, \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t_i)\) is zero for all but a few \(k\). Collect those active coefficients and the landmark in \(\mathbf{x}_i\), and the model is again \(\mathbf{z}_i = \mathbf{h}_i(\mathbf{x}_i) + \boldsymbol{\eta}_i\), equation (2.39), which is the form every factor has had since Week 2 · Day 2. With cubic B-splines a landmark measurement becomes a factor on five variables: four coefficients and one landmark. The book calls it a quinary factor (Figure 2.3).

Filling the gap: the Jacobian with respect to a coefficient

To linearize you need the derivative of the residual with respect to each \(\mathbf{c}_k\). The chain rule gives it in one line. Let \(\mathbf{H}_i = \partial \mathbf{h}_i / \partial \mathbf{p}\) be the Jacobian you would have used for a pose variable. Since \(\mathbf{p}(t_i)\) is linear in the coefficients,

\[ \frac{\partial \mathbf{h}_i}{\partial \mathbf{c}_k} = \mathbf{H}_i\,\frac{\partial \mathbf{p}(t_i)}{\partial \mathbf{c}_k} = \mathbf{H}_i\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t_i) . \]

For a cubic B-spline this is \(\mathbf{H}_i\) times one of the four numbers \(w_0,\dots,w_3\), and zero for every other coefficient. Nothing else in the Gauss-Newton machinery changes.

5. Velocity and queries come for free

Because the coefficients do not depend on time, differentiating the trajectory only differentiates the basis functions: §2.2.1 · p. 64

\[ \dot{\mathbf{p}}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \dot{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_k(t)\,\mathbf{c}_k . \tag{2.40} \]

So a sensor that measures velocity, or acceleration, gives a factor on the same coefficients. For the cubic weights above, with knot spacing \(T\) seconds, \(\dot w_0 = -\tfrac{1}{2T}(1-u)^2\), \(\dot w_1 = \tfrac{1}{2T}(3u^2-4u)\), \(\dot w_2 = \tfrac{1}{2T}(-3u^2+2u+1)\), \(\dot w_3 = \tfrac{1}{2T}u^2\). In the worked example (\(T = 1\) s, \(u=0.5\)) these are \(-0.125, -0.625, 0.625, 0.125\), and the velocity is \(3\cdot(-0.625) + 6\cdot 0.625 + 3\cdot 0.125 = 2.25\) units per second.

After MAP inference you can evaluate (2.36) or (2.40) at any time. The uncertainty follows too. Stack the active weights at the query time in a row \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}(t)\) and let \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c\) be the marginal covariance of the active coefficients. Because the map from coefficients to pose is linear, the rule for a linear function of a Gaussian from Week 1 · Day 2 gives

\[ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{p}(t) = \boldsymbol{\Psi}(t)\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c\,\boldsymbol{\Psi}(t)\T . \]

Only the marginal over the few active coefficients is needed, not the whole covariance. §2.2.1 · p. 65 Example with the hats of section 2: at the midpoint the weights are \((0.5, 0.5)\). If the two coefficients each have variance 0.04 and covariance 0.01, the queried value has variance \(0.25\cdot0.04 + 0.25\cdot0.04 + 2\cdot0.25\cdot0.01 = 0.025\).

Common mistakes

  • "The coefficients are poses on the trajectory." Only for the linear hat functions. A cubic B-spline does not pass through its coefficients: at a knot (\(u = 0\)) the weights are \(\tfrac16, \tfrac46, \tfrac16\), so the curve is a blend of three of them.
  • "Continuous time means infinitely many unknowns." The unknowns are the \(K\) coefficients. The function is continuous; the list of variables is finite and usually shorter than in discrete time.
  • "Local support is only a convenience." It is what keeps each factor small. With basis functions that are nonzero everywhere, such as sines, every measurement would touch every coefficient and the sparsity of Week 3 · Day 1 would be lost.

Check yourself

Q1. A trajectory is a cubic B-spline. One landmark is observed at one time stamp. How many variables does the resulting factor connect?

Four basis functions are nonzero at any time, so four coefficients are active, plus the landmark: a five-way factor, as in Figure 2.3.

Q2. At a knot of a cubic B-spline (u = 0) the three active coefficients are 1, 4, and 7. What is the value of the curve there?

At u = 0 the weights are 1/6, 4/6, 1/6 (the fourth is 0). So p = (1 + 16 + 7)/6 = 4.

Q3. A cubic B-spline has knots every 0.5 s. You change one coefficient. Over how long a stretch of time can the trajectory change?

A cubic basis function is nonzero over four knot intervals: 4 × 0.5 s = 2 s. Outside that window its weight is zero.

Q4. A rolling-shutter camera exposes each image row at a slightly different time. What does a continuous-time trajectory let you do that one pose per image does not?

Each pixel's residual uses p(t) at that pixel's time. The unknowns are still the same few coefficients.

Q5. A wheel encoder measures speed. To use it with a spline trajectory you need

Equation (2.40): the velocity is the sum of the differentiated basis functions times the same coefficients.

Recap

  1. Continuous-time trajectories separate measurement times, estimation variables, and query times.
  2. Parametric form: \(\mathbf{p}(t) = \sum_k \boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t)\,\mathbf{c}_k\), equation (2.36). The coefficients are the unknowns.
  3. B-splines of degree \(d\) come from the Cox–de Boor recursion. Exactly \(d+1\) are nonzero at any time, and they sum to 1.
  4. Local support means a measurement at \(t_i\) is a factor on the few active coefficients; its Jacobian is \(\mathbf{H}_i\boldsymbol{\Psi}_k(t_i)\).
  5. Derivatives and queried covariances come from the same coefficients, through \(\dot{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_k\) and \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c\boldsymbol{\Psi}\T\).
