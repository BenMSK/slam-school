Splines and Gaussian processes on Lie groups

After today you can rewrite a spline in cumulative form, interpolate between two poses on SE(d) with Exp and Log, and write the random-walk motion-prior factor between two poses together with its linearization.

Today's big idea

Poses cannot be added or scaled, so a weighted sum of poses means nothing on SE(d). The difference between two neighbouring poses, however, can be turned into an ordinary vector with Log, scaled or given a Gaussian prior there, and turned back with Exp. Splines reach this through cumulative basis functions. Gaussian processes reach it through a local variable defined between control points. For the simplest case of each, the result is the same interpolation formula.

1. Why the sum fails on a group

Everything this week has used \(\mathbf{p}(t) = \sum_k \psi_k(t)\,\mathbf{p}_k\) with vectors \(\mathbf{p}_k\). Try it with rotations. The entrywise average of the planar rotations by 0° and by 90° is

\[ \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & -0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}, \]

whose determinant is 0.5, not 1. It is not a rotation: it also shrinks. This is the point of Week 4 · Day 1: \(\SO(d)\) and \(\SE(d)\) are not vector spaces. The sensible answer, a rotation by 45°, comes from taking the relative rotation (90°), halving its angle, and applying that to the first rotation. The rest of today generalizes this recipe. §2.2.4 · p. 69

2. The cumulative form of a spline

With the same scalar basis functions \(\psi_k(t)\) for every component, the spline is \(\mathbf{p}(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\psi_k(t)\,\mathbf{p}_k\), equation (2.53). The \(\mathbf{p}_k\) are now called control points. The book rewrites the sum so that only the first control point and differences of neighbours appear: §2.2.4.1 · p. 69

\[ \mathbf{p}(t) = \psi^c_1(t)\,\mathbf{p}_1 + \sum_{k=2}^{K} \psi^c_k(t)\,(\mathbf{p}_k - \mathbf{p}_{k-1}), \qquad \psi^c_k(t) = \sum_{\ell=k}^{K}\psi_\ell(t) . \tag{2.54, 2.55} \]

The \(\psi^c_k\) are the cumulative basis functions.

Filling the gap: deriving the cumulative form

Write each control point as the first one plus the steps that lead to it: \(\mathbf{p}_k = \mathbf{p}_1 + \sum_{j=2}^{k}(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_{j-1})\). Substitute into (2.53):

\[ \mathbf{p}(t) = \Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\psi_k\Big)\mathbf{p}_1 + \sum_{k=2}^{K}\psi_k \sum_{j=2}^{k}(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_{j-1}) . \]

In the double sum, the step \(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_{j-1}\) is multiplied by every \(\psi_k\) with \(k \ge j\). Collecting them gives \(\sum_{j=2}^{K}\big(\sum_{k=j}^{K}\psi_k\big)(\mathbf{p}_j - \mathbf{p}_{j-1})\), which is (2.54). The first bracket is \(\psi^c_1\), and it equals 1 wherever the basis functions sum to one.

For a linear spline with knots \(T\) seconds apart, each \(\psi^c_k\) is a ramp: 0 before its segment, rising linearly from 0 to 1 across it, and 1 afterwards (equations (2.58)–(2.59), lower half of Figure 2.5). Write \(\alpha_k(t)\) for the fraction of the segment that has passed. On the segment that ends at control point \(k\), every earlier step has weight 1, every later step has weight 0, and one step is in transition: §2.2.4.1 · p. 70

\[ \mathbf{p}(t) = \mathbf{p}_{k-1} + \alpha_k(t)\,(\mathbf{p}_k - \mathbf{p}_{k-1}) . \tag{2.60} \]

Numbers: control points 2, 5, 4, and a time 30% of the way from the second to the third. The cumulative weights are \(1, 1, 0.3\), so \(p = 2 + 1\cdot(5-2) + 0.3\cdot(4-5) = 4.7\), which is the linear interpolation \(5 + 0.3\cdot(4-5)\).

Reading note: an index shift on p. 70

Equations (2.56)–(2.57) place control point \(k\) at time \((k-1)T\) (check: \(\psi_1 = 1\) at \(t = 0\)). Equations (2.58)–(2.62) are written as if control point \(k\) sat at time \(kT\): in (2.60) the curve reaches \(\mathbf{p}_k\) as \(t \to kT\). The two halves of the page are one index apart. Nothing later depends on it. What matters is the pattern above: on each segment, \(\alpha\) runs from 0 to 1 while the curve goes from one control point to the next.

3. From the vector space to the group

The cumulative form uses only three operations, and each has a group counterpart from Week 4 · Day 2:

Vector spaceLie group
Difference \(\mathbf{p}_k - \mathbf{p}_{k-1}\)\(\Log\big(\mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{-1}\big)\), a vector
Scale the difference by \(\psi^c_k(t)\)Scale that vector by \(\psi^c_k(t)\)
Add the result to \(\mathbf{p}_{k-1}\)Apply \(\Exp(\cdot)\) and multiply onto \(\mathbf{T}_{k-1}\)

For the linear spline this turns (2.60) into §2.2.4.1 · p. 71

\[ \mathbf{T}(t) = \Exp\!\Big( \alpha_k(t)\,\Log\big(\mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{-1}\big) \Big)\,\mathbf{T}_{k-1} = \big(\mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{-1}\big)^{\alpha_k(t)}\,\mathbf{T}_{k-1} . \tag{2.61, 2.62} \]

The power in the second form is shorthand for the first: a matrix raised to a real power means Exp of the scaled Log. Check the ends. At \(\alpha = 0\), \(\Exp(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{I}\) and \(\mathbf{T}(t) = \mathbf{T}_{k-1}\). At \(\alpha = 1\), \(\mathbf{T}(t) = \mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{-1}\mathbf{T}_{k-1} = \mathbf{T}_k\). In between, the result is always a valid pose, because Exp always lands on the group.

Try it: drag the far pose, turn its heading, and slide α from 0 to 1.

Notice that the interpolated pose follows a circular arc while its heading turns at a steady rate. At the starting position, ξ = (15.01, −10.67, 2.09), ordered translation then rotation as in the book, and at α = 0.5 the pose is 2.45 units from the midpoint of the straight line. The first two numbers of ξ are not the displacement between the two positions. The last tile repeats section 1: blending the two rotation matrices entrywise gives determinant 0.25 at α = 0.5.

Two remarks. First, the book perturbs on the left here, where §2.1.3 perturbed on the right. Footnote 3 gives the reason: when the unknown pose maps world coordinates to sensor coordinates, splines are usually applied in the sensor frame, and the perturbation is placed there too. §2.2.4.1 · p. 71 Second, with a higher-order spline more than one step is in transition at a time (three for a cubic), so \(\mathbf{T}(t)\) is a product of several such Exp factors. The active set is still small.

4. Linearizing through the interpolation

The control poses are the estimation variables. Gauss-Newton perturbs each one, \(\mathbf{T}_k = \Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi}_k)\,\mathbf{T}_k^0\), and needs to know how the interpolated pose moves in response: \(\mathbf{T}(t) = \Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t))\,\mathbf{T}^0(t)\), equation (2.63). The book quotes the first-order answer: §2.2.4.1 · p. 71

\[ \boldsymbol{\xi}(t) \approx \big(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}(\alpha_k)\big)\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1} + \mathbf{A}(\alpha_k)\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_k, \qquad \mathbf{A}(\alpha) = \alpha\,\mathbf{J}\big(\alpha\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{rel}}\big)\,\mathbf{J}\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{rel}}\big)^{-1}, \tag{2.64, 2.65} \]

where \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{rel}} = \Log(\mathbf{T}_k^0\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{0\,-1})\) and \(\mathbf{J}\) is the left Jacobian of \(\SE(d)\) from Week 4 · Day 5. (The book writes the group element itself as the argument of \(\mathbf{J}\); it means the Jacobian evaluated at that element's Log.)

Filling the gap: three checks that make (2.65) believable

  • \(\alpha = 0\): \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{0}\), so \(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t) = \boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1}\). At the start of the segment the interpolated pose is control pose \(k-1\).
  • \(\alpha = 1\): \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{J}^{-1} = \mathbf{I}\), so \(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t) = \boldsymbol{\xi}_k\).
  • Control poses close together: \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathrm{rel}}\) is small, \(\mathbf{J} \approx \mathbf{I}\), and \(\mathbf{A} \approx \alpha\mathbf{I}\). Then \(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t) \approx (1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1} + \alpha\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_k\): the perturbation is interpolated the same way as the pose. The Jacobians correct this when the two control poses are far apart.

Now take any measurement at time \(t\) whose linearized error is \(\mathbf{e}_i(t) \approx \mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{h}_i(\cdot) - \mathbf{H}_i\,\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)\), equation (2.66), and substitute (2.64):

\[ \mathbf{e}_i(t) \approx \mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{h}_i\big(\mathbf{T}^0(t)\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\ell}}_i\big) - \mathbf{H}_i\big(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}(\alpha_k)\big)\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1} - \mathbf{H}_i\,\mathbf{A}(\alpha_k)\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_k . \tag{2.67} \]

This is the chain rule of Day 1 again. There a measurement's Jacobian was \(\mathbf{H}_i\) times a basis-function value; here it is \(\mathbf{H}_i\) times \(\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}\) for one control pose and \(\mathbf{H}_i\mathbf{A}\) for the other. Each measurement touches two control poses. §2.2.4.1 · p. 72

5. Gaussian processes on Lie groups

The GP needs a vector for its SDE to act on. The book defines one between each pair of control poses, the local variable §2.2.4.2 · pp. 72–73

\[ \boldsymbol{\xi}_k(t) = \Log\big(\mathbf{T}(t)\,\mathbf{T}_k^{-1}\big), \qquad t_k \le t \le t_{k+1} . \]

It is zero at \(t_k\) and equals \(\Log(\mathbf{T}_{k+1}\mathbf{T}_k^{-1})\) at \(t_{k+1}\) (Figure 2.7). The control poses \(\mathbf{T}_k\) are the global variables and remain the things we estimate. Put the simplest SDE on the local variable, a random walk:

\[ \dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_k(t) = \mathbf{w}(t), \quad \mathbf{w}(t) \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\,\delta(t-t')\big) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boldsymbol{\xi}_k(t) \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(\mathbf{0}, \min(t,t')\,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\big) , \tag{2.68, 2.70} \]

with time counted from \(t_k\). This is Day 3's result with \(\boldsymbol{\Phi} = \mathbf{I}\): over a segment of length \(T\) the local variable picks up covariance \(T\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\). The motion-prior factors are then, in terms of the control poses,

\[ \mathbf{e}_1 = \Log\big(\mathbf{T}_1\check{\mathbf{T}}_1^{-1}\big), \qquad \mathbf{e}_k = \Log\big(\mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{-1}\big) \ \ (k \gt 1), \tag{2.73} \]

the first weighted by the initial covariance \(\mathcal{K}(t_1,t_1)\) around a prior pose \(\check{\mathbf{T}}_1\), the others by \(T\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\). In words: neighbouring control poses should be close, and \(T\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\) says how close. It is the same chain as on Day 3 (Figure 2.6), with \(\Log(\mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{-1})\) in place of \(\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{x}_{k-1}\). Querying between control poses by GP interpolation gives equation (2.74), which is (2.62) again: here the linear interpolation is a consequence of choosing a random walk, not a separate choice. §2.2.4.2 · p. 73

Filling the gap: linearizing the prior factor, equation (2.75)

Write \(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}} = \mathbf{T}_k^0\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{0\,-1}\). Perturbing both poses on the left and using \((\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbf{T})^{-1} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\Exp(-\boldsymbol{\xi})\):

\[ \mathbf{e}_k = \Log\big( \Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi}_k)\,\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}}\,\Exp(-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1}) \big) . \]

Step 1. Move the right-hand exponential to the left with the adjoint identity \(\mathbf{T}\,\Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi}) = \Exp(\Ad(\mathbf{T})\,\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\mathbf{T}\) from Week 4 · Day 5: \(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}}\Exp(-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1}) = \Exp(-\Ad(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1})\,\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}}\).

Step 2. Two small exponentials next to each other combine by adding their arguments, to first order: \(\mathbf{e}_k \approx \Log\big( \Exp(\boldsymbol{\xi}_k - \Ad(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1})\,\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}} \big)\).

Step 3. A small left perturbation \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) changes the Log by \(\mathbf{J}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) (this is equation (2.34) on p. 62). When neighbouring control poses are close, \(\mathbf{J} \approx \mathbf{I}\), and

\[ \mathbf{e}_k \approx \Log(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}}) + \boldsymbol{\xi}_k - \Ad(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}})\,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1} . \tag{2.75} \]

So the factor's Jacobian is \(\mathbf{I}\) with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_k\) and \(-\Ad(\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{rel}})\) with respect to \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1}\). The printed (2.75) has no \(\mathbf{J}^{-1}\); reading it as the close-poses approximation is our interpretation.

If measurements fall between control poses, they are handled exactly as for the linear spline, with (2.67), because both methods interpolate linearly between \(\SE(d)\) control poses. The book's summary of the difference: the GP approach adds motion-prior factors that regularize the problem, and the spline approach does not. A footnote adds that such prior terms can also be introduced for splines. §2.2.4.2 · p. 74

The book points to Chirikjian for the theory of uncertainty on manifolds, including global treatments; to Boumal for optimization on manifolds; to Barfoot for estimation on Lie groups when the uncertainty is compact, and for the nonparametric continuous-time methods; and to the survey by Talbot et al. for continuous-time estimation, parametric and nonparametric. §2.3 · p. 74

Common mistakes

  • "The power in (2.62) is taken entry by entry." It is the group power: Log, scale by α, Exp.
  • "Interpolating position and angles separately is the same thing." On \(\SE(d)\) rotation and translation are coupled by Exp. The widget shows an arc where separate interpolation gives a straight line.
  • "The local variable is a new unknown." It is a device for writing the prior as a vector-space SDE. The estimation variables are still the control poses, plus derivatives if the chosen prior needs them.

Check yourself

Q1. Two planar rotations are by 30° and by 90°. What is the group interpolation between them at α = 0.25?

The relative rotation is 60°. A quarter of it is 15°, applied to the first: 30° + 15° = 45°.

Q2. A linear spline has control points p₁, p₂, p₃. At a time halfway between the second and the third, what are the cumulative weights on the steps (p₂ − p₁) and (p₃ − p₂)?

The first step is complete (weight 1) and the second is half done (weight 0.5): p = p₁ + (p₂ − p₁) + 0.5 (p₃ − p₂).

Q3. In equation (2.64), what is the perturbation of the interpolated pose when α = 1?

A(1) = J J⁻¹ = I, so the weight on control pose k − 1 is zero and the weight on control pose k is the identity. At α = 1 the interpolated pose is control pose k.

Q4. A random-walk prior on SE(d) has power spectral density 0.5 I and control poses every 0.1 s. What covariance weights the factor between consecutive control poses?

The local variable accumulates covariance T Q over one segment: 0.1 × 0.5 I = 0.05 I, as in equation (2.71).

Q5. According to the book, what is the main difference between the linear-spline and random-walk-GP formulations on SE(d)?

Both use the same linear interpolation and the same measurement linearization (2.67). The GP's motion-prior factors regularize the problem (p. 74).

Recap

  1. Cumulative form: \(\mathbf{p}(t) = \psi^c_1\mathbf{p}_1 + \sum_{k\ge2}\psi^c_k(\mathbf{p}_k - \mathbf{p}_{k-1})\) with \(\psi^c_k = \sum_{\ell\ge k}\psi_\ell\). Only differences of neighbours appear.
  2. On a group: \(\mathbf{T}(t) = \Exp\big(\alpha_k\Log(\mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{-1})\big)\mathbf{T}_{k-1}\), equations (2.61)–(2.62).
  3. Perturbations pass through the interpolation as \(\boldsymbol{\xi}(t) \approx (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k-1} + \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\xi}_k\), with \(\mathbf{A} \approx \alpha\mathbf{I}\) for nearby control poses.
  4. GP on a group: an SDE on the local variable \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_k(t) = \Log(\mathbf{T}(t)\mathbf{T}_k^{-1})\). The random walk gives factors \(\Log(\mathbf{T}_k\mathbf{T}_{k-1}^{-1})\) with covariance \(T\boldsymbol{\mathcal{Q}}\).
  5. Spline and GP share the interpolation. The GP adds the motion prior.
