Today's big idea

Two efforts pull in opposite directions. One connects language models to maps, so that their knowledge is tied to a real place. The other trains very large models on images or robot data in the hope of doing without an explicit map. This section is a snapshot of fast-moving work. Everything below is what the book reports as of its April 2026 edition; expect the details to date.

1. Handing a map to a language model

An LLM knows a great deal about the world but nothing about this room. Grounding means tying its reasoning to the physical scene. The book highlights two ways to do it with a map. §17.4.1 · pp. 514–515

# 1. Map prompting: the map is text inside the prompt
{"objects": [
  {"id": 3, "caption": "red soda can", "on": 7, "center": [2.1, 0.4, 0.9]},
  {"id": 7, "caption": "wooden dining table", "center": [2.0, 0.5, 0.4]}]}

# 2. Map API: the map stays outside; the LLM calls functions on it
exists("soda can")               → true
where_is("soda can")             → [2.1, 0.4, 0.9]
distance("soda can", "fridge")   → 3.2

Our illustration; the function names are the book's examples (p. 515), the contents are made up.

A system that passes a VLM-built map as text to an LLM is an instance of what the book calls a Socratic model: pretrained models composed as modules. With a map API, map calls can be interleaved with LLM calls and robot commands. This is the pattern of tool calling, in which an LLM is free to call external functions and use their outputs while it generates an answer. The book's example, NLMap, has an LLM break a user request into proposed objects and check that each exists, and where, by querying an open-vocabulary map. §17.4.1 · p. 515

2. Could a large model replace the map?

The book returns here to a question from its first pages: is an explicit map needed at all? §17.4.2 · p. 515

Evidence the book citesWhat was doneWhat was found
OpenEQA (embodied question answering)Compared ways of combining VLMs and scene graphsPrompting GPT-4V directly with 50 frames beat an explicit scene-graph prompt on that benchmark
Mobility VLA (navigation)Gave a VLM a full office tour of 948 frames and asked for the goal image matching a user requestWorked when the goal image was handed to a map-based navigation pipeline. A map-free variant, with the VLM outputting actions directly, was ineffective

So one benchmark favours raw frames, and one robot still needed a map to move. The book's reading, at present: whether an LLM- or VLM-based agent needs an explicit map appears to depend largely on the spatial and temporal horizons of its tasks, and the question is open. §17.4.2 · pp. 515–516 It adds that it is unclear how far long-context VLMs have a coherent understanding of the spatial structure behind the images.

Intuition

Fifty photos of a flat are enough to answer "what colour is the sofa?" Walking from a meeting room to the front door of an office you toured an hour ago is a different kind of problem: many steps, each depending on where the last one left you. The first has a short horizon, the second a long one.

3. A foundation model for robots needs robot data

Text and images had the internet. The book's example of the recipe working in a new domain is DUSt3R for 3D vision (Week 19 · Day 5). A robotics foundation model would be a base generalist policy: one model performing many skills on many robot bodies, representing the world only implicitly. Its training data are trajectories of sensor–action pairs \(\mathcal{D} = \{\mathbf{z}_t, \mathbf{u}_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\), which must be collected on robots or in simulation. §17.4.3 · pp. 516–517

DatasetSize the book gives
RT1 (Robotics Transformer)130k trajectories, a fleet of 13 robots, 700 tasks, collected over 17 months
BridgeData V2Over 50k demonstrations of 13 skills in 24 environments
DROIDA multi-institution dataset of diverse robot interaction, mainly for manipulation policies
Open-X-EmbodimentThe above and others combined: over 1M episodes, 22 robot embodiments, 527 skills, 34 laboratories

Compare the scale with Day 2: CLIP was trained on 400M image–caption pairs. Much of the effort therefore goes into making demonstration cheap: teleoperation with a VR headset (DROID), ALOHA's paired low-cost manipulators that mirror a human's motion, and the handheld gripper of the Universal Manipulation Interface, which matches a robot gripper and its eye-in-hand sensor. §17.4.3 · p. 517 These datasets mainly teach skills such as grasping; longer tasks are meant to come from longer demonstrations or by chaining short ones.

4. Policies that act without an explicit map

The simplest learner is behaviour cloning: supervised learning of the mapping from observation to action. The book calls it brittle, because the demonstrations contain little sub-optimal data and small deviations take the model out of distribution. §17.4.3 · p. 517

Filling the gap: why small errors compound

Suppose the cloned policy errs with probability \(\epsilon\) per step while it is in states like those in the demonstrations. An expert never shows how to recover from a mistake, so in the worst case one error puts the robot in unfamiliar states where it keeps erring for the rest of a \(T\)-step episode. A first error at step \(t\) then costs \(T - t + 1\) bad steps, and the expected number of bad steps is at most

\[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \epsilon\,(T - t + 1) = \epsilon\,\frac{T(T+1)}{2} \approx \frac{\epsilon T^2}{2} . \]

If errors did not compound it would be \(\epsilon T\). With \(\epsilon = 0.01\) and \(T = 100\): 1 bad step against a worst case of about 50. The bound grows with the square of the horizon, which is the same horizon the book pointed to in section 2. An explicit map and planner behave differently: after a slip the robot relocalizes and replans.

5. Where the chapter lands

The book does not pick a winner. Scaling robot datasets and models, combined with internet-scale VLMs and LLMs, could conceivably make explicit maps obsolete. Against that, it cites evidence that transformers struggle with compositional or chain-of-thought reasoning, which is why neural memory mechanisms with a notion of state (it names structured state space models) are re-emerging, with limits still unclear. It judges it equally likely, or more so, that generalization to compositional tasks in large, complex environments will come from explicit structure learned through a process such as SLAM. And it suggests the two are complementary: perceiving, representing, and understanding the world comes before acting, and an explicit world model should only help. §17.4.4 · pp. 519–520

Common mistakes

  • "The book says VLMs have made maps unnecessary." It reports one benchmark where frames beat a scene-graph prompt and one robot where the map-free variant failed, and concludes that it depends on the task's horizons.
  • "A VLA model contains no representation of the world." It has no explicit one that you can inspect or query. Whatever it knows is implicit in its weights and context.
  • "These model lists are the state of the art." The book itself says the section is not exhaustive and that new models appear very frequently.

Check yourself

Q1. A user asks, "How far is the mug from the sink, in metres?" The map is a dense metric map with object features. Which integration fits, according to §17.4.1?

Map prompting is restricted to sparse, text-compatible maps and conveys geometry poorly. A map API leaves the geometry in the map and returns only the answer.

Q2. What does the Mobility VLA result, as the book reports it, suggest?

The VLM chose the goal image from the tour. The map-free variant, in which it output actions directly, was found ineffective.

Q3. A cloned policy errs with probability 0.02 per step on a 50-step task. Using the worst-case argument, about how many bad steps should you expect, against how many if errors did not compound?

Worst case εT²/2 = 0.02 × 2500 / 2 = 25. Without compounding, εT = 0.02 × 50 = 1.

Q4. What makes a model a vision-language-action model in the book's description?

A VLA keeps the structure of the language and vision-language models of Day 2 and changes what the output tokens mean: robot actions.

Recap

  1. An LLM can be grounded by putting a text map in its prompt, or by letting it call functions on a map that stays outside.
  2. As of the book's April 2026 edition, large VLMs answer some questions from raw frames, but the robot example still needed a map to navigate. The need depends on spatial and temporal horizons.
  3. Robot foundation models learn policies from sensor–action trajectories, which are far scarcer than internet text and images.
  4. Behaviour cloning compounds errors; VLA models, action chunking, diffusion policies, and reinforcement learning are the current families.
  5. The book's closing view: explicit world modelling through SLAM and generalist policies are complementary.
