Reprojection error, bundle adjustment, and the geometry of two views

After today you can derive the reprojection cost from Gaussian pixel noise, state full bundle adjustment and the ways real-time systems cut it down, and derive the epipolar constraint that ties two views together.

Today's big idea

Feature-based visual SLAM is one least-squares problem: move the cameras and the 3D points until every point, pushed through the camera model, lands on the pixel where it was observed. The mismatch is the reprojection error. Everything else is about making that problem small enough to solve in real time and giving it a good enough starting point.

1. The reprojection error

For an observed image point \(\mathbf{z}_j \in \R^2\) and a 3D point \(\mathbf{x}^c_j\) in camera coordinates, the reprojection error is §7.3.3 · p. 204

\[ \mathbf{e}_{\text{reproj}} = \mathbf{z}_j - \pi(\mathbf{x}^c_j, \boldsymbol{\xi}). \]

It is a 2-vector in pixels: observed minus predicted. With the camera of Day 3 (\(f = 500\), principal point \((320, 240)\)) and an estimated point \(\mathbf{x}^c = (0.4, -0.2, 2)\), the prediction is \((420, 190)\). If the keypoint was detected at \((423, 186)\), then \(\mathbf{e} = (3, -4)\) and its length is 5 pixels.

Filling the gap: from Gaussian noise to equation (7.1)

The book goes from the likelihood to (7.1) in two lines. Here are the steps, which repeat Week 2 · Day 2 for a camera. Assume the detected pixel is the true projection plus Gaussian noise with covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j\):

\[ p(\mathbf{z}_j \mid \mathbf{x}^c_j, \boldsymbol{\xi}) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12\,\mathbf{e}_j\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j^{-1}\mathbf{e}_j\Big), \qquad \mathbf{e}_j = \mathbf{z}_j - \pi(\mathbf{x}^c_j, \boldsymbol{\xi}). \]

Independent observations multiply, so their logarithms add. Taking the negative logarithm,

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\sum_j \log p(\mathbf{z}_j \mid \mathbf{x}^c_j, \boldsymbol{\xi}) = \frac12\sum_j \mathbf{e}_j\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j^{-1}\mathbf{e}_j + \underbrace{\sum_j \log\!\big(2\pi\sqrt{\det\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j}\big)}_{\text{const.}} . \]

The last sum does not depend on the unknowns. Writing \(\norm{\mathbf{e}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \mathbf{e}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{e}\) for the squared Mahalanobis norm and dropping the constant gives the book's

\[ E_{\text{reproj}} = \frac12\sum_j \norm{\mathbf{z}_j - \pi(\mathbf{x}^c_j, \boldsymbol{\xi})}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j}. \tag{7.1} \]

Maximizing the likelihood is the same as minimizing this sum. In the simplest case every keypoint has the same isotropic noise, of standard deviation \(\sigma\) pixels in each direction, and each term is \(\norm{\mathbf{e}_j}^2/(2\sigma^2)\). (The book writes this case as \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j = \sigma\mathbf{I}_2\); its \(\sigma\) is then the variance.) For \(\mathbf{e} = (3, -4)\): with \(\sigma = 1\) the term is \(25/2 = 12.5\); with \(\sigma = 2\) it is \(25/8 \approx 3.1\). A keypoint detected on a coarse pyramid level is less precise, gets a larger \(\sigma\), and counts less.

Wrong matches produce huge residuals that a squared cost cannot absorb, so a robust kernel \(\rho\) such as Huber or Tukey (Week 6 · Day 4) is wrapped around each term: §7.3.3 · p. 204

\[ E_{\text{robust}} = \frac12\sum_j \rho\Big(\norm{\mathbf{z}_j - \pi(\mathbf{x}^c_j, \boldsymbol{\xi})}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j}\Big). \tag{7.2} \]

2. Full bundle adjustment

Now let the camera move. Points \(j \in \mathsf{P}\) have unknown world coordinates \(\mathbf{x}^w_j \in \R^3\); images \(i \in \mathsf{C}\) have unknown poses \(\mathbf{T}^i_w \in \SE(3)\), which take world coordinates to the coordinates of camera \(i\): \(\mathbf{x}^c = \mathbf{R}^i_w\mathbf{x}^w_j + \mathbf{t}^i_w\). The observation of point \(j\) in image \(i\) is the pixel \(\mathbf{z}_{ij}\). Minimizing over everything at once is full BA: §7.3.4 · p. 205

\[ \{\mathbf{T}^{i*}_w, \mathbf{x}^{w*}_j\} = \argmin_{\mathbf{T}^i_w,\,\mathbf{x}^w_j}\ \frac12\sum_{i,j}\rho\Big(\norm{\mathbf{z}_{ij} - \pi\big(\mathbf{R}^i_w\mathbf{x}^w_j + \mathbf{t}^i_w\big)}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ij}}\Big). \tag{7.3} \]

(The intrinsics \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) are dropped from the notation from here on.) Count the sizes. Each camera has 6 unknowns and each point 3; each observation gives 2 equations. A map with 200 images and 20,000 points, each seen in 5 images, has \(200\cdot6 + 20{,}000\cdot3 = 61{,}200\) unknowns and \(2\cdot100{,}000 = 200{,}000\) equations. Solving that for every new frame is out of reach, and the book lists how systems cope. §7.3.4 · pp. 205–206

ApproachWhat it keepsConsequence
Batch optimizationAll poses, all points, all observations; Gauss-Newton or Levenberg-Marquardt on (7.3)The gold standard in SfM; too expensive to run per image
Filtering (EKF, information filter)Only the latest camera pose; older poses are marginalized outSparsity is destroyed, which limits the map to a few hundred features; past observations are never re-linearized

Factor graphsA language for all of the above: variables are poses and landmarks, factors are reprojection errors and priorsPart I applies unchanged

Figure 7.4 shows why filtering loses sparsity. Marginalizing a pose is the elimination step of Week 3 · Day 3: it leaves a new factor joining everything that pose was connected to. Remove the three old poses in the figure, and all five landmarks they observed become linked to each other, 10 landmark pairs for 5 landmarks, and in general about \(n^2/2\) for \(n\). Keyframe methods avoid creating these links by throwing the in-between frames away.

3. Two views: the epipolar constraint

Problem (7.3) is non-linear and is solved iteratively, so it needs a reasonable initial guess. This matters most for a monocular camera, where a single frame does not make the state observable. §7.3.7 · p. 207 The book leaves the geometry to the literature. The classical starting point is a relation between two images of the same point that involves no 3D point at all.

Filling the gap: deriving the essential and fundamental matrices

Let a point have coordinates \(\mathbf{x}^1\) in camera 1 and \(\mathbf{x}^2\) in camera 2, related by the rigid motion \(\mathbf{x}^2 = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{x}^1 + \mathbf{t}\). Each camera sees it along a ray: \(\mathbf{x}^1 = \lambda_1\mathbf{b}_1\) and \(\mathbf{x}^2 = \lambda_2\mathbf{b}_2\), where \(\mathbf{b}_k = \pi^{-1}(\mathbf{z}_k)\) is the unprojected pixel of Day 3 and \(\lambda_k \gt 0\) is the unknown depth. Substituting,

\[ \lambda_2\,\mathbf{b}_2 = \lambda_1\,\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{t}. \]

Step 1: take the cross product of both sides with \(\mathbf{t}\). Since \(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{0}\),

\[ \lambda_2\,\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{b}_2 = \lambda_1\,\mathbf{t}\times(\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{b}_1). \]

Step 2: take the dot product of both sides with \(\mathbf{b}_2\). The left side is zero, because \(\mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{b}_2\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{b}_2\). Divide by \(\lambda_1\):

\[ \mathbf{b}_2\T\,[\mathbf{t}]_\times\mathbf{R}\;\mathbf{b}_1 = 0, \qquad [\mathbf{t}]_\times = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -t_z & t_y \\ t_z & 0 & -t_x \\ -t_y & t_x & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \]

where \([\mathbf{t}]_\times\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{t}\times\mathbf{a}\) writes the cross product as a matrix. This is the epipolar constraint, and \(\mathbf{E} = [\mathbf{t}]_\times\mathbf{R}\) is the essential matrix. Both depths have dropped out. Geometrically it says that the two rays and the line joining the camera centres lie in one plane.

In pixels, with pinhole cameras and homogeneous pixels \(\mathbf{p}_k = \begin{bmatrix} u_k & v_k & 1 \end{bmatrix}\T\), we have \(\mathbf{b}_k = \mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{p}_k\), so

\[ \mathbf{p}_2\T\,\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{p}_1 = 0, \qquad \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{K}^{-\top}\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{K}^{-1}, \]

the fundamental matrix. For a fixed pixel \(\mathbf{p}_1\), the vector \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2 = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{p}_1 = (\alpha, \beta, \gamma)\) defines a line \(\alpha u + \beta v + \gamma = 0\) in image 2, the epipolar line: the match of \(\mathbf{p}_1\) must lie on it.

Check with stereo. Camera 2 is 1 m to the right of camera 1 with the same orientation: \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\), \(\mathbf{t} = (-1, 0, 0)\). Then \(\mathbf{E} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), and for rays \(\mathbf{b}_k = (x'_k, y'_k, 1)\) the constraint reads \(y'_2 - y'_1 = 0\). Matches lie on the same image row, which is the setting of Day 2's disparity formula.

Scale. Replace \(\mathbf{t}\) by \(2\mathbf{t}\): the constraint is unchanged. Two images fix the direction of the translation but not its length. A monocular reconstruction is therefore defined only up to an overall scale. \(\mathbf{E}\) has 5 degrees of freedom (3 for \(\mathbf{R}\), 2 for the direction of \(\mathbf{t}\)), so five correspondences are the minimum to estimate it, inside a RANSAC loop (Week 6 · Day 2) because some matches are wrong.

Try it: press Next point a few times. Drag camera 2 in the top view. Then move the two error sliders one at a time.

Notice three things. The selected point always sits on its epipolar line in image 2 (0.0 px), while the other points are usually tens of pixels away, so most wrong matches violate the constraint; a few do not (after one press of Next point, another point lies only 3 px from the line). A depth error slides the prediction along the line and is invisible in image 1: +1.5 m gives a 15.6 px residual. A 1° yaw error shifts every prediction by 5 to 6 px and raises the cost from 0 to about 125. Drag camera 2 onto camera 1 and the depth slider stops having any effect: without baseline, depth is unobservable.

4. From two views to a map

The pieces chain into the standard monocular initialization. Match keypoints between two frames (Day 4). Estimate \(\mathbf{E}\) with RANSAC and split it into \(\mathbf{R}\) and the direction of \(\mathbf{t}\). Then triangulate: with \(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t}\) known, the ray equation \(\lambda_2\mathbf{b}_2 - \lambda_1\mathbf{R}\mathbf{b}_1 = \mathbf{t}\) is three linear equations in the two depths, solved by least squares because noisy rays do not meet exactly. Finally refine everything with BA (7.3). From then on each new frame is located against the existing 3D points, a problem called Perspective-n-Point (PnP), which you met in Week 4 · Day 3 and which opens Week 12.

The section closes with four short pointers. §7.3.5–7.3.8 · p. 207

  • Photometric error: the alternative to reprojection error used by direct methods. It compares pixel intensities, assuming that a scene point looks equally bright in consecutive frames.
  • Visual place recognition: compute one descriptor per image, \(\mathbf{d} = f(I)\), and retrieve the nearest stored descriptor. Bag-of-words methods build it by quantizing local descriptors such as ORB into clusters called visual words; learned descriptors are more invariant to appearance change.
  • Initialization: the topic of this section.
  • Map representations: a balance of accuracy, memory, and computation, treated in Chapter 5 (Week 8).

Common mistakes

  • "The reprojection error is a distance in meters." It lives in the image, in pixels. A 1-pixel error can mean a millimeter for a near point or meters for a far one.
  • "The epipolar constraint gives the match." It gives a line. Any point on that line satisfies it, at any depth.
  • "A filter is cheaper because it keeps one pose." Removing poses makes the remaining landmark block dense, and the cost grows quickly with the number of features.

Check yourself

Q1. A keypoint is observed at (212, 148). The current estimate projects the point to (209, 152). With \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{I}_2\), what does this observation contribute to \(E_{\text{reproj}}\)?

e = (3, −4), so ‖e‖² = 25 and the term is ½·25 = 12.5.

Q2. A bundle adjustment problem has 10 camera poses and 500 points. How many scalar unknowns does (7.3) have?

Each pose in SE(3) has 6 degrees of freedom and each point 3: 10·6 + 500·3 = 1,560.

Q3. Two identical pinhole cameras are related by \(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\), \(\mathbf{t} = (-1, 0, 0)\). A keypoint is at row \(v = 130\) in image 1. Where is its epipolar line in image 2?

For a sideways translation with no rotation the constraint reduces to y′₂ = y′₁, and with identical intrinsics equal y′ means equal v. Depth only decides where on that row the match is.

Q4. Why are filtering approaches limited to a few hundred features?

Eliminating a pose creates a factor over all its neighbours. After many poses are removed the landmarks are fully connected, and the cost of a dense problem grows much faster than a sparse one.

Q5. Why can two images from one moving camera not tell a small nearby scene from a large distant one?

In λ₂b₂ = λ₁Rb₁ + t, multiplying t, λ₁ and λ₂ by the same number keeps the equation true with the same rays. The images carry no information about absolute scale.

Recap

  1. Reprojection error: \(\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{z} - \pi(\mathbf{x}^c)\), in pixels. Gaussian pixel noise turns maximum likelihood into the weighted least-squares cost (7.1).
  2. Full BA (7.3) minimizes the robust reprojection error over all poses \(\mathbf{T}^i_w\) and points \(\mathbf{x}^w_j\).
  3. Batch is the reference; filtering marginalizes old poses and becomes dense; keyframe methods stay sparse; factor graphs express them all.
  4. Two views of a point satisfy \(\mathbf{b}_2\T\mathbf{E}\,\mathbf{b}_1 = 0\) with \(\mathbf{E} = [\mathbf{t}]_\times\mathbf{R}\), or \(\mathbf{p}_2\T\mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{p}_1 = 0\) in pixels. The match lies on the epipolar line \(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{p}_1\).
  5. Monocular initialization: matches, essential matrix, triangulation, then BA. The overall scale stays unknown.
