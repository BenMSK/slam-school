The camera model: from a 3D point to a pixel, and back to a ray

After today you can project a 3D point to a pixel with the pinhole model, unproject a pixel to a ray, apply and undo radial distortion, and say which lens needs which model.

Today's big idea

A camera model is a function \(\pi\) that maps a 3D point in the camera's frame to a pixel, using a handful of calibrated numbers. Going forward is a division by depth. Going back is impossible for a point and possible only for a ray, because the division threw the depth away. Every visual measurement model in the rest of the chapter is built on \(\pi\).

1. Projection and unprojection

A camera model has a geometric part, saying where a 3D point lands in the image, and a photometric part, saying how bright the pixel gets. §7.3.1 · p. 197 The geometric part is the projection function

\[ \mathbf{z} = \pi(\mathbf{x}^c, \boldsymbol{\xi}), \qquad \mathbf{x}^c = \begin{bmatrix} x & y & z \end{bmatrix}\T \in \R^3, \qquad \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} u & v \end{bmatrix}\T \in \Omega \subset \R^2 . \]

Here \(\mathbf{x}^c\) is the point in camera coordinates (origin at the camera, \(z\) pointing forward along the optical axis), \(\mathbf{z}\) is its pixel position in the image domain \(\Omega\), and \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) holds the intrinsic parameters, usually calibrated beforehand. The unprojection \(\mathbf{x}^c = \pi^{-1}(\mathbf{z}, \boldsymbol{\xi})\) returns a 3D ray: the depth is unknown, so the result is known only up to scale. §7.3.1.1 · p. 198

Two notation warnings. In Part I, \(\boldsymbol{\xi}\) was a pose perturbation (Week 4 · Day 3); in Chapter 7 it is the vector of camera intrinsics. And the book uses \(z\) both for the depth coordinate and, in bold, for the measurement \(\mathbf{z}\).

2. The pinhole model

The pinhole (also rectilinear or perspective) model suits narrow-angle lenses without distortion. §7.3.1.1 · p. 198

\[ \boldsymbol{\xi}_p = \begin{bmatrix} f_u & f_v & u_0 & v_0 \end{bmatrix}\T, \qquad \pi_p(\mathbf{x}^c, \boldsymbol{\xi}) = \begin{bmatrix} f_u \dfrac{x}{z} + u_0 \\ f_v \dfrac{y}{z} + v_0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \pi_p^{-1}(\mathbf{z}, \boldsymbol{\xi}) \sim \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{u-u_0}{f_u} \\ \dfrac{v-v_0}{f_v} \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \]

\(f_u, f_v\) are the focal lengths in pixels, horizontally and vertically; with square pixels \(f_u \approx f_v\). \((u_0, v_0)\) is the principal point, the pixel where the optical axis meets the image.

Filling the gap: where the formula comes from, and the matrix form

Similar triangles. In the figure, the ray from the point through the camera centre crosses a plane at distance \(f\). The small triangle (legs \(f\) and the image height) and the large one (legs \(z\) and \(x\)) have the same angles, so image height \(= f\,x/z\). The same holds for \(y\).

Pixels. A sensor counts pixels from a corner of the image, not from the optical axis, so the principal point is added. If the lens has focal length 4 mm and pixels are 5 µm wide, then \(f_u = 4\,\text{mm} / 0.005\,\text{mm} = 800\) pixels. This is why \(f\) is "in pixel units".

Example. \(f_u = f_v = 500\), \((u_0, v_0) = (320, 240)\), and \(\mathbf{x}^c = (0.4, -0.2, 2)\) m:

\[ u = 500\cdot\frac{0.4}{2} + 320 = 420, \qquad v = 500\cdot\frac{-0.2}{2} + 240 = 190 . \]

Unprojecting \((420, 190)\) gives \(\big(\tfrac{420-320}{500}, \tfrac{190-240}{500}, 1\big) = (0.2, -0.1, 1)\), which is \(\mathbf{x}^c/2\): the right direction, with the depth gone. The point \((4, -2, 20)\) lands on the same pixel.

Homogeneous coordinates. Represent the pixel \((u, v)\) by any triple \((\lambda u, \lambda v, \lambda)\) with \(\lambda \ne 0\), and write \(\sim\) for "equal up to a nonzero scale factor". Then the pinhole model is a matrix product:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{x}^c, \qquad \mathbf{K} = \begin{bmatrix} f_u & 0 & u_0 \\ 0 & f_v & v_0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{x}^c = \begin{bmatrix} f_u x + u_0 z \\ f_v y + v_0 z \\ z \end{bmatrix}. \]

Dividing by the last entry, \(z\), gives back \(\pi_p\). The matrix \(\mathbf{K}\) is the calibration matrix, and \(\pi_p^{-1}(\mathbf{z}) \sim \mathbf{K}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} u & v & 1 \end{bmatrix}\T\). The \(\sim\) in the book's unprojection formula is this same symbol. Homogeneous coordinates turn the awkward division into linear algebra, which Day 5 uses to relate two views.

Field of view. A pixel at the image edge, \(W/2\) from the principal point, sees a ray at angle \(\arctan\frac{W/2}{f_u}\) from the axis. An image 640 pixels wide with \(f_u = 500\) has a horizontal field of view of \(2\arctan(0.64) \approx 65^\circ\).

3. Lens distortion

Real lenses bend straight lines. The most common correction is the radial-tangential model, which adds four parameters, \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{RT} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\xi}_p\T & k_1 & k_2 & p_1 & p_2 \end{bmatrix}\T\), and inserts one step between the division and the scaling to pixels. §7.3.1.1 · pp. 198–199

\[ \begin{aligned} x' &= \frac{x}{z}, \quad y' = \frac{y}{z}, \quad r = \sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2}, \\ x'' &= x'(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4) + 2p_1 x'y' + p_2(r^2 + 2x'^2), \\ y'' &= y'(1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4) + p_1(r^2 + 2y'^2) + 2p_2 x'y', \\ \pi(\mathbf{x}^c, \boldsymbol{\xi}) &= \begin{bmatrix} f_u x'' + u_0 \\ f_v y'' + v_0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned} \]

\((x', y')\) are normalized image coordinates: the pinhole image for \(f = 1\) and principal point at zero. The \(k\) terms are radial: they move a point along the line through the image center, by an amount that grows with the distance \(r\). The \(p\) terms are tangential, and model a lens that is slightly off-center or tilted.

Example: \(k_1 = -0.2\), all other coefficients zero, \(x' = 0.5\), \(y' = 0\). Then \(r^2 = 0.25\), the factor is \(1 - 0.05 = 0.95\), and \(x'' = 0.475\). With \(f_u = 500\) the pixel moves \(500 \times 0.025 = 12.5\) pixels toward the center.

Try it: slide \(k_1\) from negative to positive, then change the focal length.

Notice that negative \(k_1\) pulls the corners inward (barrel distortion) and positive \(k_1\) pushes them outward (pincushion). The center barely moves; the corner at \(r = 1\) moves 72 pixels at the default settings (\(0.18 \times 400\)). The last tile counts the iterations needed to undo the distortion for that corner.

Filling the gap: undoing distortion by iteration

To unproject a pixel you need \((x', y')\) from \((x'', y'')\). That means solving a polynomial of degree five in \(r\) (and worse with tangential terms), for which there is no formula. The book mentions Newton-Raphson. §7.3.1.1 · p. 199 An even simpler scheme works for moderate distortion. With radial terms only, the model says \(x' = x'' / (1 + k_1 r^2 + k_2 r^4)\), where \(r\) belongs to the unknown point. So guess, and repeat:

x', y' = x'', y''                      # first guess: no distortion
repeat until the change is tiny:
    r2 = x'*x' + y'*y'
    g  = 1 + k1*r2 + k2*r2*r2
    x', y' = x''/g, y''/g

For the example above (\(x'' = 0.475\), \(k_1 = -0.2\)) the iterates are \(0.475 \to 0.49745 \to 0.49973 \to 0.49997\), heading to the true value 0.5. Systems that need this for every pixel compute it once and store a lookup table.

4. Wide-angle and fisheye lenses

A pinhole image point lies at distance \(f\tan\theta\) from the principal point, where \(\theta\) is the angle between the ray and the optical axis. As \(\theta \to 90^\circ\) this goes to infinity, so a pinhole-based model cannot represent rays from the side, let alone from behind. The book lists the usual choices (Figure 7.1 shows the lenses). §7.3.1.1 · pp. 198–199

ModelParametersUseUnprojection
Pinhole4: \(f_u, f_v, u_0, v_0\)Narrow-angle lenses without distortionClosed form
Radial-tangential8: pinhole + \(k_1, k_2, p_1, p_2\)Ordinary lenses with some distortionIterative
Brown-Conrady (as defined in the book)6: pinhole + \(k_1, k_2\)Wide-angle lenses, fields of view up to 180°Iterative
Kannala-Brandt (KB)8: pinhole + \(k_1,\dots,k_4\)Fisheye lenses beyond 180°Iterative
Double Sphere (DS)6Fisheye and wide-angle lensesClosed form

The KB model replaces \(\tan\theta\) by a polynomial in the angle itself:

\[ \pi_{KB}(\mathbf{x}^c, \boldsymbol{\xi}) = \begin{bmatrix} f_u\, r(\theta)\cos\psi + u_0 \\ f_v\, r(\theta)\sin\psi + v_0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \theta = \arctan\frac{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}{z}, \quad \psi = \arctan\frac{y}{x}, \quad r(\theta) = \theta + \sum_{n=1}^{4} k_n\theta^{2n+1}. \]

With all \(k_n = 0\), a ray at \(\theta = 90^\circ\) lands at radius \(f\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), a finite number: about 471 pixels for \(f = 300\). The DS model projects the point onto two unit spheres in turn and then applies a shifted pinhole projection; the book reports it to be around five times faster to compute than KB at comparable reprojection error. §7.3.1.1 · p. 199

The choice matters. A fisheye lens should get a spherical model and a rectilinear lens a linear one; an ill-suited model leaves systematic errors that bias the whole SLAM estimate. §7.3.1.4 · p. 200

5. Brightness and time

The photometric model \(I = f(E, T)\) maps irradiance \(E\) (the light power arriving at a pixel) to the stored intensity \(I\), given camera settings \(T\): exposure time, gain, gamma correction, de-bayering, lens vignetting. It matters for methods that compare pixel brightness across images (the direct methods of Day 2) and for textured dense reconstruction. §7.3.1.2 · pp. 199–200

Time matters too. A global shutter exposes all rows at once. A rolling shutter, found in most consumer cameras, reads the rows one after another, so each row is taken from a slightly different camera pose. Suppose read-out takes 30 ms while the camera turns at 60°/s: the last row is taken 1.8° later than the first, which at \(f = 500\) is a shift of \(500\tan 1.8^\circ \approx 16\) pixels. The remedies are a global-shutter camera, or a model with one pose per row, which becomes practical when an IMU measures the motion during the exposure. §7.3.1.3 · p. 200 The continuous-time trajectories of Week 5 · Day 1 were built for exactly this.

Common mistakes

  • "Unprojection recovers the 3D point." It recovers a direction. Every point \(\lambda\,\pi^{-1}(\mathbf{z})\) with \(\lambda \gt 0\) projects to the same pixel.
  • "The focal length is a distance in meters." In \(\boldsymbol{\xi}_p\) it is in pixels: the physical focal length divided by the pixel size.
  • "Distortion is a small cosmetic effect." At the image corners it can be tens of pixels, far more than the sub-pixel noise of a good feature detector.

Check yourself

Q1. A pinhole camera has \(f_u = f_v = 400\), \((u_0, v_0) = (320, 240)\). Where does the point \(\mathbf{x}^c = (1, 0.5, 4)\) project?

u = 400·(1/4) + 320 = 420 and v = 400·(0.5/4) + 240 = 290. (720, 440) forgets the division by depth; (100, 50) forgets the principal point.

Q2. With the same camera, which 3D point could have produced the pixel (520, 240)?

Unprojection gives the ray direction ((520−320)/400, 0, 1) = (0.5, 0, 1). The point (2, 0, 4) is 4 times that direction, so it lies on the ray.

Q3. An image is 640 pixels wide and \(f_u = 320\). What is the horizontal field of view of this pinhole camera?

Half the width is 320 pixels, so the half-angle is arctan(320/320) = 45°, and the full field of view is 90°.

Q4. A lens has \(k_1 = -0.1\) and no other distortion. A point has normalized coordinates \(x' = 1\), \(y' = 0\), and \(f_u = 400\). How does distortion move its pixel?

r² = 1, so x″ = 1·(1 − 0.1) = 0.9. The change of 0.1 in normalized units is 0.1 × 400 = 40 pixels, inward because k₁ is negative.

Q5. Why can a pinhole model with distortion terms not describe a 190° fisheye lens?

The normalized coordinate is tan θ, which diverges at 90° and has the wrong sign behind the camera. Models such as Kannala-Brandt and Double Sphere parametrize by the ray angle or by a sphere instead.

Recap

  1. \(\mathbf{z} = \pi(\mathbf{x}^c, \boldsymbol{\xi})\) maps a camera-frame point to a pixel; \(\pi^{-1}\) returns a ray, known up to scale.
  2. Pinhole: \(u = f_u x/z + u_0\), \(v = f_v y/z + v_0\), or \(\begin{bmatrix} u & v & 1 \end{bmatrix}\T \sim \mathbf{K}\mathbf{x}^c\) in homogeneous coordinates.
  3. Radial-tangential distortion acts on normalized coordinates between the division and the scaling to pixels; undoing it takes iteration.
  4. Fisheye lenses need models built on the ray angle (Kannala-Brandt) or on spheres (Double Sphere).
  5. The photometric model matters for direct methods, and a rolling shutter gives each image row its own pose.
