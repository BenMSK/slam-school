Linear least squares

After today you can set up a linear least-squares problem, derive and solve its normal equations by hand, weight measurements by their noise, and explain why the result is the Gaussian maximum likelihood estimate.

Today's big idea

Almost every computation in a SLAM back-end ends in the same small problem: find the \(\mathbf{x}\) that makes \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\) as close as possible to \(\mathbf{b}\). The answer solves the normal equations \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\). Dividing each equation by its noise level first turns this into the maximum likelihood estimate under Gaussian noise.

1. More equations than unknowns

A robot drives along a line at constant speed. Its position is measured at four times:

Time t (s)Measured position y (m)Prediction of guess (1, 1)ResidualPrediction of (1.5, 1)Residual
01101.5−0.5
13212.50.5
24313.50.5
34404.5−0.5

The model is \(y = x_1 + x_2\,t\), with two unknowns: the start position \(x_1\) and the speed \(x_2\). Each measurement gives one linear equation, \(x_1 + x_2 t_i = y_i\). Stack them:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 4 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{b}} . \]

Four equations, two unknowns: the system is overdetermined, and because the measurements are noisy no \(\mathbf{x}\) satisfies all four. For any candidate \(\mathbf{x}\) the residual \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\) lists how far each equation is from holding. The least-squares solution is the candidate with the smallest sum of squared residuals:

\[ \mathbf{x}^\star = \argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\ \norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}}_2^2 = \argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\ \sum_i r_i^2 . \]

In the table, the guess \((1, 1)\) has cost \(0+1+1+0 = 2\). The line \((1.5, 1)\) has cost \(4\times 0.25 = 1\), and the next section shows that nothing does better. The book writes this problem as equation (1.24b), with the unknown called \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) because there it is a correction to a current guess. §1.3.2 · p. 31

2. The normal equations

The minimizer satisfies a square linear system, the normal equations, which is the book's equation (1.25): §1.3.3 · p. 31

\[ \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}^\star = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}. \]

For the example,

\[ \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 12 \\ 23 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The entries have plain meanings: 4 is the number of points, 6 is \(\sum t_i\), 14 is \(\sum t_i^2\), 12 is \(\sum y_i\), and 23 is \(\sum t_i y_i\). Solve \(4x_1+6x_2 = 12\) and \(6x_1+14x_2 = 23\): multiply the first by 1.5 and subtract it from the second to get \(5x_2 = 5\), so \(x_2 = 1\) and then \(x_1 = (12-6)/4 = 1.5\). The best line is \(y = 1.5 + 1.0\,t\).

Filling the gap: where the normal equations come from

Write the cost as a function \(f(\mathbf{x})\) and expand the square. The two cross terms are equal because each is a scalar and a scalar equals its transpose:

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})\T(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - 2\,\mathbf{b}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}\T\mathbf{b}. \]

Two differentiation rules are all you need: \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{c}\T\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{c}\) and, for a symmetric matrix \(\mathbf{Q}\), \(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}) = 2\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}\). They are the vector versions of \((cx)' = c\) and \((qx^2)' = 2qx\). With \(\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\):

\[ \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = 2\,\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} - 2\,\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}. \]

Is this stationary point a minimum, and the only one? The second derivative of \(f\) is the constant matrix \(2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\). For any direction \(\mathbf{v}\), \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}_2^2 \ge 0\), so \(f\) curves upward everywhere. If the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\) are linearly independent (\(\mathbf{A}\) has full column rank), then \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\) whenever \(\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}\), the curvature is strictly positive in every direction, and \(f\) is a bowl with exactly one lowest point. If the columns are dependent, a whole line of \(\mathbf{x}\) values gives the same cost. That happens in SLAM when nothing anchors the map: with odometry only and no prior, shifting every pose by the same amount changes no residual.

The normal equations also have a geometric reading. Move everything to one side: \(\mathbf{A}\T(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}^\star) = \mathbf{0}\). Each row of \(\mathbf{A}\T\) is a column of \(\mathbf{A}\), so this says that the residual is perpendicular ("normal") to every column of \(\mathbf{A}\).

Check it on the example: \(\mathbf{r} = (-0.5,\ 0.5,\ 0.5,\ -0.5)\). Its dot product with the first column \((1,1,1,1)\) is 0, and with the second column \((0,1,2,3)\) it is \(0.5+1-1.5 = 0\).

3. Weights: trusting some measurements more

Plain least squares treats every equation alike. If measurement \(i\) has noise standard deviation \(\sigma_i\), a residual should count in units of its own \(\sigma_i\). Weighted least squares minimizes \(\sum_i r_i^2/\sigma_i^2\). With the covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) of the measurement noise (diagonal with entries \(\sigma_i^2\) when the noises are independent) this is

\[ \norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}), \qquad\text{solved by}\qquad \mathbf{A}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{x}^\star = \mathbf{A}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{b}. \]

The quantity \(\norm{\mathbf{e}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} = \mathbf{e}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{e}\) is the squared Mahalanobis distance, the book's equation (1.10). §1.2.2 · p. 27 It measures an error in standard deviations. Example: \(\mathbf{e} = (3, 1)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \diag(9,\ 0.25)\) gives \(9/9 + 1/0.25 = 1 + 4 = 5\). The first component is 3 units off where \(\sigma = 3\): one sigma. The second is 1 unit off where \(\sigma = 0.5\): two sigma, so it contributes four times as much, although it is the smaller number.

You never need a separate weighted solver. Divide row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) and entry \(i\) of \(\mathbf{b}\) by \(\sigma_i\) (in general, multiply both by \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\)) and the weighted problem becomes a plain one. This is whitening, and the book does it once in equations (1.22)–(1.23) and then drops the covariances from the notation. §1.3.1 · p. 30 Whitening also removes units, so rows that came from ranges in metres and rows that came from angles in radians can sit in the same \(\mathbf{A}\).

Try it: the amber measurement disagrees with the other five. Change how much noise you believe it has. You can also drag any point.

Notice: at σ = 1 the weighted line and the equal-weight line coincide, and the single amber point tilts the fit a long way because it sits far out at the right end. Raise its σ to 5 and the solid line swings back onto the other five points, while the amber residual, though large, is under one of its own σ. Lower σ to 0.2 and the line passes almost through the amber point.

In plain words

Six witnesses describe the same event. Least squares finds the story that disagrees with all of them as little as possible. Weights say how reliable each witness is. A witness you barely trust (large σ) can be far from the final story without it counting for much.

4. Why squares? Gaussian maximum likelihood

Squaring the residuals is not a matter of taste. It is what Gaussian noise implies.

Filling the gap: weighted least squares is the MLE

Let \(\mathbf{a}_i\T\) be row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{A}\), and suppose \(b_i = \mathbf{a}_i\T\mathbf{x} + \eta_i\) with independent noises \(\eta_i\sim\N(0,\sigma_i^2)\). Independence makes the likelihood of all the measurements a product:

\[ p(\mathbf{b}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \prod_i \frac{1}{\sigma_i\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(b_i-\mathbf{a}_i\T\mathbf{x})^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\right). \]

Take the negative logarithm. The product becomes a sum and the factors in front become a constant that does not depend on \(\mathbf{x}\):

\[ -\log p(\mathbf{b}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac12\sum_i \frac{(b_i-\mathbf{a}_i\T\mathbf{x})^2}{\sigma_i^2} + \text{const} = \tfrac12\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} + \text{const}. \]

Maximizing the likelihood is minimizing its negative log, so \(\mathbf{x}^{\text{MLE}}\) is the weighted least-squares solution. A Gaussian prior on \(\mathbf{x}\) contributes one more squared term of the same kind, as in the function \(J(x)\) of Day 2, so MAP estimation is least squares with a few extra rows.

The same matrix tells you how good the answer is. The solution \(\mathbf{x}^\star = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{b}\), with \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \mathbf{A}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\), is a linear function of \(\mathbf{b}\), and \(\mathbf{b}\) has covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\). A linear map \(\mathbf{M}\) turns a covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) into \(\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{M}\T\), so

\[ \cov(\mathbf{x}^\star) = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}. \]

So \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \mathbf{A}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{A} = \sum_i \mathbf{a}_i\mathbf{a}_i\T/\sigma_i^2\) is the information matrix of Day 3: every measurement row adds its own piece of information. The book gives \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) (after whitening) exactly this name in equation (1.26). §1.3.3 · p. 31

In the example with every \(\sigma_i = 1\) m:

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1} = \frac{1}{20}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.7 & -0.3 \\ -0.3 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

The start position is known to \(\sqrt{0.7} = 0.84\) m and the speed to \(\sqrt{0.2} = 0.45\) m/s. Four measurements of 1 m quality give an estimate better than any one of them.

Common mistakes

  • "Least squares minimizes the perpendicular distance from the points to the line." It minimizes the error in the measured quantity, here the vertical distance. The perpendicularity in the figure is in the space of measurement vectors, not in the plot of the data.
  • "A larger σ gives a measurement more weight." The weight is \(1/\sigma^2\). Doubling σ quarters the weight.
  • "The subscript in \(\norm{\mathbf{e}}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\) means multiply by Σ." The subscript names the covariance; the formula uses its inverse.
  • "If \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) is singular, the software is at fault." A singular \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) means the measurements do not determine \(\mathbf{x}\). The cure is another measurement or a prior.

Check yourself

Q1. Three equally good measurements of one unknown x read 2, 3, and 7. What is the least-squares estimate?

A is a column of three ones, so AᵀA = 3 and Aᵀb = 12. The estimate is 12/3 = 4, the mean. The mean is the least-squares answer to "one number that fits them all".

Q2. Two measurements of one unknown: 10 with σ = 1 and 16 with σ = 2. What is the weighted least-squares estimate?

Weights are 1/σ²: 1 and 0.25. (10 × 1 + 16 × 0.25) / (1 + 0.25) = 14 / 1.25 = 11.2. This is the fusion rule of Day 3.

Q3. At the least-squares solution, what is true of the residual r = b − A x?

The normal equations are Aᵀr = 0: the residual has zero dot product with each column of A. It is zero only if the equations happen to be consistent.

Q4. An error e = (4, 1) has covariance diag(4, 1). What is its squared Mahalanobis distance?

eᵀΣ⁻¹e = 16/4 + 1/1 = 5. The value 17 is the plain squared length, and 3 comes from dividing by σ instead of σ².

Q5. One row of a least-squares problem reads x₁ + 2x₂ = 5, from a sensor with σ = 0.1. What is the whitened row?

Whitening divides the row and its right-hand side by σ, here multiplying by 10. Squaring the whitened residual then gives the 1/σ² = 100 weight.

Recap

  1. Least squares picks the \(\mathbf{x}\) that minimizes \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}}_2^2\), the sum of squared residuals.
  2. The minimizer solves the normal equations \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\), and is unique when \(\mathbf{A}\) has full column rank. The residual is orthogonal to the columns of \(\mathbf{A}\).
  3. Weighted least squares minimizes the Mahalanobis norm \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\). Whitening (dividing each row by its σ) reduces it to the plain problem.
  4. Under independent Gaussian noise the weighted least-squares solution is the maximum likelihood estimate.
  5. \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \mathbf{A}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\) is the information matrix of the estimate, and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\) is its covariance.
