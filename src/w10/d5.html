Today's big idea

Yesterday the FIM of a simplified pose graph turned out to be a Laplacian. So the design criteria of Day 3 become graph quantities: the determinant is the weighted number of spanning trees, and the smallest eigenvalue is tied to the algebraic connectivity. A robot can evaluate these before it moves, which is what makes them useful for planning and for pruning.

1. D-criterion: counting spanning trees

A spanning tree of a graph is a set of edges that connects all nodes and contains no loop; with \(n\) nodes it always has \(n-1\) edges. Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem says that the determinant of the reduced weighted Laplacian is the weighted number of spanning trees: §6.2.2 · p. 178

\[ \det\mathbf{L}_w=\sum_{\text{spanning trees }T}\ \prod_{e\in T}w_e\ =:\ t_w(G). \]

With unit weights this is a plain count. Check it on a triangle with nodes 0, 1, 2, node 0 anchored, and weights \(w_{01}=2\), \(w_{12}=3\), \(w_{02}=1\). Any two of the three edges form a spanning tree, so \(t_w=2\cdot 3+2\cdot 1+3\cdot 1=11\). And

\[ \mathbf{L}_w=\begin{bmatrix} 2+3 & -3 \\ -3 & 3+1 \end{bmatrix},\qquad\det\mathbf{L}_w=5\cdot 4-9=11 . \]

Try it: the default graph has five edges. Count the trees on the right, then add the edge 1–3. Then remove edges until node 3 is cut off.

Notice: with the default five edges there are 8 spanning trees and the determinant is 8. With all six edges both are 16. As soon as some node has no edge left, both are 0: the Laplacian is singular, and that node's position cannot be estimated at all.

Filling the gap: why a determinant counts trees

The Cauchy-Binet formula expands the determinant of a product of a wide matrix and a tall one as a sum over square pieces. For \(\mathbf{L}_w=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\T\), with \(\mathbf{A}\) of size \((n-1)\times m\):

\[ \det(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\T)=\sum_{S}\ \det(\mathbf{A}_S)^2\prod_{e\in S}w_e , \]

where \(S\) runs over all choices of \(n-1\) edges and \(\mathbf{A}_S\) keeps only their columns. Two facts finish the proof. If the edges in \(S\) contain a loop, adding their columns around the loop with suitable signs gives zero, so the columns are dependent and \(\det\mathbf{A}_S=0\). If they contain no loop, they form a spanning tree, and then \(\det\mathbf{A}_S=\pm 1\) (peel off a leaf of the tree: its row has a single \(\pm 1\); expand along it and repeat). So each spanning tree contributes exactly \(\prod_{e\in T}w_e\) and everything else contributes nothing.

Back to the FIM. For the simplified problem \(\mathcal{I}=\mathbf{L}_w\otimes\mathbf{I}_3\), and \(\det(\mathbf{L}_w\otimes\mathbf{I}_3)=(\det\mathbf{L}_w)^3\), so

\[ \log\det\mathcal{I}=3\,\log t_w(G). \]

The D-criterion of Day 3, the volume of the uncertainty ellipsoid, is decided by the weighted number of spanning trees. More trees means more independent ways for information to flow from the anchor to every pose. This makes precise the feeling that a well-connected graph gives an accurate map. §6.2.2 · p. 178

2. E- and A-criteria on the graph

Criterion (Day 3)Graph quantityReading
D: \(\log\det\mathcal{I}\)\(\log t_w(G)\), the weighted number of spanning treesOverall connectivity
E: \(\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{I})\)\(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{L}_w)\), related to the algebraic connectivityThe weakest direction: is there a bottleneck?
A: \(\tr(\mathcal{I}^{-1})\)\(3\,\tr(\mathbf{L}_w^{-1})\), three times the sum of effective resistances to the anchorAverage pose variance

The algebraic connectivity (Fiedler value) is the second-smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_2\) of the full Laplacian (Box 6.1, p. 158). It is zero exactly when the graph falls into two pieces and small when it nearly does. The book says the E-criterion "is related to" it. §6.2.2 · p. 178

Filling the gap: how the two eigenvalues are related

The reduced Laplacian is the full one with one row and the same column deleted. Cauchy's interlacing theorem says the eigenvalues of such a submatrix lie between consecutive eigenvalues of the full matrix. Since the smallest eigenvalue of a Laplacian is 0,

\[ 0\ \le\ \lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{L}_w)\ \le\ \lambda_2(\mathbf{L}_{\text{full}}). \]

So a graph with low algebraic connectivity has a poorly determined direction wherever you put the anchor. In yesterday's widget with odometry only, the two numbers are 0.044 and 0.152; after closing the big loop they are 0.152 and 0.586.

3. Which measurement is worth adding?

These graph scores have two practical advantages over the FIM itself. They are cheaper: in 3-D pose-graph optimization the Laplacian has one row per pose where the FIM has six. And they need neither a SLAM solution nor actual measurements, only the list of who would observe whom. §6.2.2 · p. 178 That is the situation of active SLAM, where the robot chooses its path to maximize the accuracy it expects, and of measurement selection, where a long-running system keeps only its most informative edges.

Filling the gap: the value of one more edge

A new edge between poses \(i\) and \(j\) with weight \(w\) changes the Laplacian by a rank-one term, \(\mathbf{L}_w\to\mathbf{L}_w+w\,\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}\T\), where \(\mathbf{a}\) is the new column of the incidence matrix. The matrix determinant lemma gives the new determinant without refactoring:

\[ \det(\mathbf{L}_w+w\,\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}\T)=\det(\mathbf{L}_w)\,\big(1+w\,\mathbf{a}\T\mathbf{L}_w^{-1}\mathbf{a}\big)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\Delta\log\det=\log\big(1+w\,R_{ij}\big), \]

where \(R_{ij}=\mathbf{a}\T\mathbf{L}_w^{-1}\mathbf{a}\) is the effective resistance between \(i\) and \(j\) in the current graph. In estimation terms \(R_{ij}\) is the variance per axis of the current estimate of \(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i\). So the rule is: measure what you are most uncertain about. On an 8-pose chain with unit weights, the ends are 7 edges apart, \(R_{70}=7\), and closing that loop adds \(\log 8=2.08\); poses 2 and 0 have \(R_{20}=2\), and that loop adds \(\log 3=1.10\). This is the book's "a larger loop helps more", with numbers.

Try it: this is yesterday's widget. Start from "All off" and always press the button with the largest number.

Notice: the first pick is 7–0 (+2.08), and the tree count goes from 1 to 8. After that every remaining gain is smaller than before: 6–1 drops from +1.79 to +1.06, because the first loop closure already shortened the electrical distance between poses 6 and 1. Adding 6–1 next gives 23 trees. Each new edge is worth less in a graph that is already well connected.

4. Further reading: what works and what is open

Section 6.3 is a map of the literature. The certifiable approach began with 2-D SLAM, and SE-Sync gave the first fast algorithm and the first exactness guarantee under bounded noise. Certifiable algorithms now exist for rotation averaging, PGO, landmark-based, multi-robot, and range-aided SLAM, and for several front-end problems such as 3-D registration, two-view geometry, perspective-n-point, and calibration. §6.3 · pp. 178–179 The open problems fall into three groups.

TopicWhat is openWhere
CoverageSome problems have no certifiable algorithm. In visual SLAM the perspective projection is a ratio of polynomials; it can be made polynomial with extra variables, but one per keypoint measurement is impractical. IMU models have also resisted.p. 179
SpeedOutside the problems where the Riemannian Staircase applies, the SDP needs interior-point methods (fine for small front-end problems, too slow for a back-end) or special-purpose solvers that are still slow next to local solvers. Current work shrinks the SDP or removes constraints.p. 179
TheoryCertificates are per instance: computed after solving. There is little general understanding of when a relaxation will be exact.p. 179
OutliersCommon practice wraps a certifiable solver in a graduated non-convexity loop (Week 6 · Day 5), which is robust in experiments but not certified. Directly certifiable robust solvers use the moment relaxation and are slow. An alternative solves locally and only checks optimality afterwards.pp. 179–180
Odometry backboneRobust formulations usually trust odometry and protect only loop closures. That fails when visual tracking breaks, and in multi-robot SLAM, where no odometry links the robots. Making odometry robust too gives loose relaxations.p. 180
Mostly outliersIf most measurements are wrong, even the optimal robust estimate can be wrong. One direction returns several hypotheses with a guarantee that one is right ("list-decodable" regression).p. 180
UncertaintyThe covariance of Days 3–4 is only as good as the measurement covariances fed in, which are often guesses; some work learns them. With outliers the true spread of estimates can have several peaks, which one covariance cannot describe.pp. 180–181

In plain words

Chapter 6 asked two questions about the answer a SLAM system gives. "Is this the best answer the data allow?" can now be checked for many problems, though not yet for cameras and IMUs, and not yet fast when outliers are in the cost. "How good is the best answer?" depends on how the measurements are wired together, and you can estimate it from the wiring alone, provided you were honest about how noisy each measurement is.

Common mistakes

  • "More spanning trees means every pose is well estimated." The determinant is an overall volume. A graph can have many trees and still one weakly attached pose; the E- and A-criteria look at that.
  • "The Laplacian scores are exact for real SLAM." They are exact for the simplified problem of Day 4. For full PGO they approximate the FIM criteria, closely under certain conditions.
  • "A certified optimum has a trustworthy covariance." The certificate concerns the cost. The covariance still rests on the noise model, and outliers or wrong noise levels break it.

Check yourself

Q1. A triangle graph has edge weights 1, 2, and 2. What is its weighted number of spanning trees?

Each pair of edges is a spanning tree: 1·2 + 1·2 + 2·2 = 8. With node 0 anchored and w₀₁ = 1, w₁₂ = 2, w₀₂ = 2, the reduced Laplacian [[3, −2], [−2, 4]] has determinant 12 − 4 = 8.

Q2. The current graph is a chain 0–1–2–3–4 of unit-weight edges. You can add a loop closure between poses 4 and 0 with weight 3. By how much does log det of the reduced Laplacian rise?

The effective resistance between 4 and 0 along the chain is 4, so the gain is log(1 + 3·4) = log 13.

Q3. Why can a robot evaluate the Laplacian-based criteria before driving a candidate path?

The scores are functions of the graph structure and the precisions. The full FIM (6.53) needs the trajectory, which is not known in advance.

Q4. Why is there not yet a practical certifiable algorithm for visual SLAM with pixel measurements?

The moment relaxation needs polynomials. A rational projection model can be rewritten with auxiliary variables, but their number makes the SDP impractical.

Recap

  1. Matrix-tree theorem: \(\det\mathbf{L}_w\) is the weighted number of spanning trees, so the D-criterion of the simplified FIM is \(3\log t_w(G)\).
  2. The E-criterion is \(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{L}_w)\), bounded above by the algebraic connectivity; the A-criterion sums effective resistances to the anchor.
  3. One new edge raises \(\log\det\) by \(\log(1+wR_{ij})\): long loops, between poses whose relative position is uncertain, are worth the most, and gains shrink as the graph fills in.
  4. Laplacian scores are small and need no measurements, which suits active SLAM and measurement selection.
  5. Open: certifiable solvers for cameras and IMUs, fast solvers for moment relaxations, exactness theory, unreliable odometry, mostly-outlier data, and honest covariances.
