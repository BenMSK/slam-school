Landmarks and range measurements in a certifiable solver

After today you can add landmarks to SE-Sync by treating them as poses without rotation, and turn a range measurement into a quadratic term by adding a unit vector.

Today's big idea

The SE-Sync recipe from Week 9 (write the problem as a QCQP, relax it to an SDP, solve the SDP with the Riemannian Staircase, check the certificate) works for any problem you can bring into QCQP form. Landmark measurements already have that form: a landmark is a pose with no rotation. Range measurements do not, until you add one auxiliary unit vector per range.

1. A landmark is a pose without rotation

Week 9 ended with a certifiable solver for pose-graph optimization (PGO), where every measurement is a relative pose. Now add \(\ell\) landmarks with unknown positions \(\mathbf{m}_{n+1},\dots,\mathbf{m}_{n+\ell}\in\R^d\) to the \(n\) poses. The robot at pose \(i\) measures the position of landmark \(k\) in its own frame (a range-and-bearing measurement). The model is the same as for the translation part of a relative pose in equation (6.8): §6.1.3 · p. 165

\[ \tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik} = \bar{\mathbf{m}}_{ik} + \mathbf{m}^{\epsilon}_{ik}, \qquad \mathbf{m}^{\epsilon}_{ik}\sim\N\!\left(\mathbf{0},\ \mu_{ik}^{-1}\mathbf{I}_d\right), \qquad \bar{\mathbf{m}}_{ik} = \mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{m}_k-\mathbf{t}_i). \tag{6.28} \]

Here \(\bar{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}\) is the true relative position, and \(\mu_{ik}\) is the precision (inverse variance) of the measurement. The noise is isotropic: the same in every direction. The graph gets a second kind of vertex and a second kind of edge.

Maximum likelihood under these models gives Problem 6.5: §6.1.3 · p. 166

\[ \min_{\mathbf{R}_i\in\SO(d),\ \mathbf{t}_i,\mathbf{m}_k\in\R^d}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}_r}\Big(\kappa_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{R}_j-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}}_F^2+\tau_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}}_2^2\Big)+\sum_{(i,k)\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}_m}\mu_{ik}\norm{\mathbf{m}_k-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}}_2^2 . \tag{6.29} \]

Filling the gap: where the landmark term comes from

The measurement lives in the robot frame, so the natural residual is \(\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}-\mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{m}_k-\mathbf{t}_i)\). Pull \(\mathbf{R}_i\T\) out in front:

\[ \tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}-\mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{m}_k-\mathbf{t}_i) = -\,\mathbf{R}_i\T\big(\mathbf{m}_k-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}\big). \]

A rotation does not change the length of a vector, \(\norm{\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{v}}_2=\norm{\mathbf{v}}_2\), so the squared residual equals \(\norm{\mathbf{m}_k-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}}_2^2\). The negative log of the Gaussian in (6.28) is \(\tfrac{\mu_{ik}}{2}\) times that, plus a constant; dropping the common factor \(\tfrac12\) gives the last sum in (6.29). This step needs isotropic noise. Tomorrow you will see what breaks without it.

Compare the two translation-like terms. Replace \(\mathbf{t}_j\) by \(\mathbf{m}_k\), \(\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}\) by \(\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}\), and \(\tau_{ij}\) by \(\mu_{ik}\), and the pose term becomes the landmark term. A landmark behaves exactly like a pose whose rotation nobody measures. So the book reuses all of §6.1.2 and only enlarges two matrices: the weight matrix becomes \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\operatorname{BlockDiag}(\boldsymbol{\Omega}_\tau,\boldsymbol{\Omega}_\mu)\) and the measurement matrix \(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\) gets one more row per landmark edge, holding \(-\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{kj}\T\) (equation (6.30)). The connection Laplacian \(\mathbf{L}(\tilde G^\rho)\), which is built from rotation measurements only, does not change. §6.1.3 · p. 166

2. Why positions can be solved for and removed

SE-Sync's first move (Week 9 · Day 3) was to eliminate the translations and keep a problem in the rotations only. The same move now removes the landmarks too.

Filling the gap: with rotations fixed, positions are a linear least-squares problem on a graph

Fix every \(\mathbf{R}_i\). Then \(\mathbf{c}_e=\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}\) (or \(\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}\)) is a known vector for each edge \(e\), and with \(\mathbf{p}\) standing for any position, pose or landmark, the position part of (6.29) is

\[ \sum_{e=(i,j)} w_e\,\norm{\mathbf{p}_j-\mathbf{p}_i-\mathbf{c}_e}_2^2 , \]

a sum of squares of functions that are linear in the unknown positions. Setting its gradient to zero gives linear equations whose matrix is the weighted graph Laplacian of Box 6.1 (p. 157). A one-dimensional example with three positions, \(p_0=0\) held fixed, unit weights, and measurements \(p_1-p_0=1.0\), \(p_2-p_1=1.0\), \(p_2-p_0=2.3\):

\[ f(p_1,p_2)=(p_1-1)^2+(p_2-p_1-1)^2+(p_2-2.3)^2 . \] \[ \tfrac12\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_1}=(p_1-1)-(p_2-p_1-1)=2p_1-p_2=0,\qquad \tfrac12\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_2}=(p_2-p_1-1)+(p_2-2.3)=-p_1+2p_2-3.3=0 . \] \[ \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} p_1 \\ p_2 \end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3.3 \end{bmatrix}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_1=1.1,\ \ p_2=2.2 . \]

The matrix is the Laplacian of the triangle graph with the row and column of the fixed node removed. The 0.3 of disagreement around the loop is shared equally: the three residuals are \(0.1,\ 0.1,\ -0.1\).

In general the optimal positions are a linear function of the right-hand side, and the right-hand side is linear in the rotations. That is what (6.16) and its landmark version (6.31) say, \(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{t}^{*\mathsf T} & \mathbf{m}^{*\mathsf T}\end{bmatrix}\T=-\operatorname{vec}\!\big(\mathbf{R}^*\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\T\mathbf{L}(W^\tau)^{\dagger}\big)\). The dagger is a pseudo-inverse because the full Laplacian is singular: shifting every position by the same vector changes nothing. Substituting a linear function of \(\mathbf{R}\) into a quadratic cost leaves a cost that is quadratic in \(\mathbf{R}\), so the reduced problem is still \(\min \tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})\) with a new \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\), and Shor's relaxation applies as before.

3. Many landmarks: cost and exactness

A typical map has far more landmarks than poses. Two facts keep the method practical. §6.1.3 · pp. 166–167

  • Cost. With many landmarks the slow step is building \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\). The Schur complement trick makes that step linear in the number of landmarks, and the other parts of SE-Sync do not get slower (Figure 6.4(b)).
  • Exactness. More landmarks, and more pose-to-landmark edges, raise the noise level up to which the relaxation stays exact (Figure 6.4(c)).

Filling the gap: why eliminating landmarks is cheap

The book names the trick without showing it. The structural reason is the one you met for bundle adjustment in Week 3 · Day 3. Order the positions as poses first, landmarks second, and split the weighted Laplacian into blocks:

\[ \mathbf{L}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}_{tt} & \mathbf{L}_{tm}\\ \mathbf{L}_{mt} & \mathbf{L}_{mm}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \text{Schur complement: } \mathbf{L}_{tt}-\mathbf{L}_{tm}\mathbf{L}_{mm}^{-1}\mathbf{L}_{mt}. \]

An off-diagonal entry of a Laplacian is non-zero only where an edge exists. No measurement joins two landmarks, so \(\mathbf{L}_{mm}\) is diagonal: entry \(k\) is the sum of the weights \(\mu_{ik}\) of the edges at landmark \(k\). Inverting a diagonal matrix is one division per landmark, so the work grows in proportion to the number of landmarks.

4. Range measurements: one square root too many

Some sensors, such as ultra-wideband radios, report only a distance \(\tilde r_{ij}\) between two things, with no direction. Problem 6.6 adds such terms, weighted by their inverse variance \(\gamma_{ij}\): §6.1.4.1 · p. 168

\[ \min_{\mathbf{R}_i\in\SO(d),\ \mathbf{t}_i\in\R^d}\ \sum_{(i,j)\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}}\Big(\kappa_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{R}_j-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}}_F^2+\tau_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}}_2^2\Big)+\sum_{(i,j)\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}_d}\gamma_{ij}\big(\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i}_2-\tilde r_{ij}\big)^2 . \tag{6.32} \]

Expand one range term:

\[ \big(\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i}-\tilde r_{ij}\big)^2=\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i}^2-2\,\tilde r_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i}+\tilde r_{ij}^2 . \]

The first and last terms are fine. The middle one contains a norm that is not squared, which is a square root of a quadratic and therefore not a polynomial. So (6.32) is not a QCQP and Shor's relaxation has nothing to act on. (The book prints the middle term with a plus sign on p. 168; the sign does not affect the argument.)

Try it: the simplest range-only problem, one unknown position and three beacons. Leave the start where it is and read the three tiles. Then drag the start above the beacons. Then switch to 2 beacons.

Notice: from the default start, Gauss-Newton stops below the beacons near (6.06, 1.09) with cost 0.131, although the true position (6, 4.8) has cost 0. The low point below the row of beacons is a mirror image of the true one, and a local solver cannot tell that a better point exists. With 2 beacons the mirror point has cost 0 as well: then the data really are ambiguous, and no solver can fix that.

Filling the gap: why the unit-vector trick is exact

The reformulation (6.33) replaces each range term by \(\gamma_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i-\tilde r_{ij}\mathbf{b}_{ij}}_2^2\) with a new unknown unit vector \(\mathbf{b}_{ij}\). Claim: for any vector \(\mathbf{v}\) and any \(r\ge 0\),

\[ \min_{\norm{\mathbf{b}}=1}\ \norm{\mathbf{v}-r\,\mathbf{b}}^2=\big(\norm{\mathbf{v}}-r\big)^2 . \]

Proof. Expand, using \(\norm{\mathbf{b}}=1\): \(\norm{\mathbf{v}-r\mathbf{b}}^2=\norm{\mathbf{v}}^2-2r\,\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{b}+r^2\). Only the middle term depends on \(\mathbf{b}\), and it is smallest when \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{b}\) is largest. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{b}\le\norm{\mathbf{v}}\), with equality when \(\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{v}/\norm{\mathbf{v}}\). Substituting gives \(\norm{\mathbf{v}}^2-2r\norm{\mathbf{v}}+r^2=(\norm{\mathbf{v}}-r)^2\).

Example: \(\mathbf{v}=(3,4)\), \(r=4\). Then \(\norm{\mathbf{v}}=5\), the best \(\mathbf{b}=(0.6,0.8)\), and \(\mathbf{v}-r\mathbf{b}=(0.6,0.8)\) has squared length \(1=(5-4)^2\).

So minimizing (6.33) over the \(\mathbf{b}_{ij}\) first gives back (6.32): the two problems have the same optimal poses. In the widget, the violet segment is \(\tilde r\,\mathbf{b}\) for the best \(\mathbf{b}\) and the thick pink segment is what is left over.

The new problem is a QCQP. The range term is now quadratic in the unknowns \(\mathbf{t}_i,\mathbf{t}_j,\mathbf{b}_{ij}\), and the new constraint \(\mathbf{b}_{ij}\T\mathbf{b}_{ij}=1\) is quadratic too. In words, the solver also estimates the bearing that the sensor did not measure, which turns a range into a range-and-bearing measurement like those of Section 1. Shor's relaxation plus the Riemannian Staircase then gives CORA, a certifiable solver for range-aided SLAM. §6.1.4.1 · pp. 168–169

More generally, how well connected the measurement graph is strongly affects exactness. §6.1.4.1 · p. 169 The same graph connectivity returns on Day 4, where it controls accuracy.

Common mistakes

  • "Adding the vectors b changes the answer." It changes the list of unknowns, not the optimal poses. Minimizing over each \(\mathbf{b}_{ij}\) gives back the original range term exactly.
  • "A certified optimum is the true position." A certificate says no other point has lower cost. With 2 beacons both mirror points are optimal, and with noise the optimum is not the truth. How far it can be from the truth is the subject of Days 3–5.
  • "Landmarks add rotation unknowns." They add positions only, which is why the connection Laplacian is unchanged.

Check yourself

Q1. Why can SE-Sync treat a landmark as "a pose without rotation"?

Swap t_j for m_k and τ for μ and the two terms in (6.29) are the same expression. Nothing measures a landmark's orientation, so it has no rotation term.

Q2. Two positions differ by v = t_j − t_i = (6, 8) and the measured range is 7. What is the minimum over unit vectors b of ‖v − 7b‖²?

‖v‖ = 10, and the minimum is (‖v‖ − r)² = (10 − 7)² = 9, reached at b = (0.6, 0.8).

Q3. Which part of the range-aided cost (6.32) stops it from being a QCQP?

An unsquared norm is the square root of a quadratic, so it is not a polynomial. The rotation constraints are quadratic and were already handled in Week 9.

Q4. In the one-dimensional example of Section 2, change the third measurement to p₂ − p₀ = 2.6 (the other two stay 1.0). What is the optimal p₂?

The equations become 2p₁ − p₂ = 0 and −p₁ + 2p₂ = 3.6, so p₁ = 1.2 and p₂ = 2.4. The loop disagreement of 0.6 is split into three residuals of size 0.2.

Recap

  1. A landmark measurement with isotropic noise gives the term \(\mu_{ik}\norm{\mathbf{m}_k-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{m}}_{ik}}^2\), the same form as a relative translation. Landmarks are poses without rotation.
  2. With rotations fixed, all positions solve a linear least-squares problem whose matrix is a graph Laplacian, so they can be eliminated and the rotation-only QCQP of SE-Sync remains.
  3. Landmarks are cheap to eliminate because their Laplacian block is diagonal, and more landmarks make the relaxation exact at higher noise.
  4. A range term is not quadratic. Adding a unit vector \(\mathbf{b}_{ij}\) with \(\norm{\mathbf{b}_{ij}}=1\) makes it quadratic without changing the optimum; relaxing that QCQP gives CORA.
  5. The range-aided relaxation is not always exact; graph connectivity decides.
