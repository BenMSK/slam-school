The Cramér-Rao lower bound and the Fisher information matrix

After today you can compute the Fisher information of a measurement model with Gaussian noise, use it to bound the covariance of any unbiased estimator, and summarize that bound in one number with the D, A, and E criteria.

Today's big idea

Yesterday's question was "did the solver find the best answer?". Today's is "how good can the best answer be?". For any unbiased estimator the covariance cannot be smaller than the inverse of the Fisher information matrix. With Gaussian noise that matrix is \(\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\), the same matrix Gauss-Newton has been building since Week 2.

1. Estimators, bias, variance, mean squared error

The measurements \(\mathbf{z}\) are random because the noise is. An estimator is a function \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{z})\) from measurements to an estimate, so the estimate is random too: run the same robot along the same path twice and you get two slightly different maps. Three numbers describe a scalar estimator of a true value \(x\): §6.2 · pp. 173–174

QuantityDefinitionMeaning
Bias\(\E[\hat x]-x\)How far off the estimator is on average
Variance\(\E\big[(\hat x-\E[\hat x])^2\big]\)How much it scatters from run to run
Mean squared error (MSE)\(\E\big[(\hat x-x)^2\big]\)Average squared distance from the truth

Filling the gap: MSE = bias² + variance

Write \(m=\E[\hat x]\) and split the error as \(\hat x-x=(\hat x-m)+(m-x)\). The second bracket is a constant, the bias.

\[ \E\big[(\hat x-x)^2\big]=\E\big[(\hat x-m)^2\big]+2(m-x)\,\underbrace{\E[\hat x-m]}_{=0}+(m-x)^2=\text{variance}+\text{bias}^2 . \]

For a vector the same steps give \(\text{MSE}=\tr\big(\cov(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\big)+\norm{\text{bias}}^2\). An unbiased estimator has zero bias, so its MSE is decided by its covariance alone.

2. The bound

The Cramér-Rao lower bound (CRLB) says that for any unbiased estimator \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) of \(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}}\in\R^m\), §6.2.1 · p. 174

\[ \cov(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\succeq\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}})^{-1}, \tag{6.41} \]

where \(\mathbf{A}\succeq\mathbf{B}\) means \(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{B}\) is positive semidefinite, and \(\mathcal{I}\) is the Fisher information matrix (FIM):

\[ [\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}})]_{i,j}=\E_{\mathbf{z}}\!\left[\frac{\partial\log p(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_i}\,\frac{\partial\log p(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_j}\right]=-\E_{\mathbf{z}}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2\log p(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}\right]. \tag{6.42, 6.43} \]

\(p(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{x})\) is the likelihood, the derivatives are taken at \(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}}\), and the average is over all measurements the sensors could have produced. The second form reads: the FIM is the average curvature of the negative log-likelihood at the truth. A sharply curved log-likelihood pins the parameter down; a flat one cannot, whatever estimator you use. §6.2.1 · p. 174

What the matrix inequality gives you in practice: taking \(\mathbf{v}\T(\cdot)\mathbf{v}\) with \(\mathbf{v}\) a unit coordinate vector, \(\operatorname{var}(\hat x_i)\ge[\mathcal{I}^{-1}]_{ii}\). Note the order of operations: invert first, then read the diagonal. For \(\mathcal{I}=\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\mathcal{I}^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), so the bound on \(\operatorname{var}(\hat x_1)\) is \(0.667\), not \(1/2\). The value \(1/2\) would be right only if \(x_2\) were known.

Filling the gap: a proof sketch for one parameter

Define the score \(s(\mathbf{z})=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log p(\mathbf{z};x)\), so that \(\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}=p\,s\). Assume derivatives and integrals may be swapped (the book's "regularity conditions").

Step 1: the score has mean zero. \(\int p\,d\mathbf{z}=1\) for every \(x\). Differentiate: \(\int p\,s\,d\mathbf{z}=\E[s]=0\). Hence \(\operatorname{var}(s)=\E[s^2]=\mathcal{I}\), which is (6.42).

Step 2: unbiasedness ties the estimator to the score. Unbiased means \(\int\hat x(\mathbf{z})\,p(\mathbf{z};x)\,d\mathbf{z}=x\) for every \(x\). Differentiate both sides: \(\int\hat x\,p\,s\,d\mathbf{z}=\E[\hat x\,s]=1\). Subtract \(x\,\E[s]=0\): \(\E[(\hat x-x)\,s]=1\).

Step 3: Cauchy-Schwarz. For any two random variables, \(\E[uv]^2\le\E[u^2]\,\E[v^2]\). With \(u=\hat x-x\) and \(v=s\):

\[ 1=\E[(\hat x-x)s]^2\le\operatorname{var}(\hat x)\cdot\mathcal{I}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\operatorname{var}(\hat x)\ge\frac{1}{\mathcal{I}} . \]

Why (6.42) equals (6.43). Differentiate \(\int p\,s\,d\mathbf{z}=0\) once more: \(\int\big(p\,s^2+p\,\tfrac{\partial s}{\partial x}\big)d\mathbf{z}=0\), that is \(\E[s^2]=-\E\big[\tfrac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\log p\big]\). The matrix case repeats these steps with vectors.

3. Gaussian noise: the FIM is JᵀΣ⁻¹J

The standing model of this course is \(\mathbf{z}=h(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}})+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) with \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\N(\mathbf{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) (6.44). For it, §6.2.1 · p. 175

\[ \mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x})\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{J}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial\mathbf{x}} . \tag{6.45} \]

Filling the gap: deriving (6.45)

The log-likelihood is \(\log p(\mathbf{z};\mathbf{x})=-\tfrac12\big(\mathbf{z}-h(\mathbf{x})\big)\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{z}-h(\mathbf{x})\big)+\text{const}\). Its gradient with respect to \(\mathbf{x}\), by the chain rule, is the score vector

\[ \mathbf{s}=\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{z}-h(\mathbf{x})\big)=\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\quad\text{at }\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}} . \]

Then (6.42) in matrix form is \(\E[\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}\T]=\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\,\E[\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\T]\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\).

You have met this matrix before. It is the left-hand side of the normal equations (Week 2 · Day 3), the Gauss-Newton approximation of the Hessian (Week 2 · Day 4), and the "information matrix" whose sparsity pattern you studied in Week 3. Footnote 9 on p. 175 makes the same remark. So every SLAM back-end computes the ingredients of its own accuracy bound.

Example 1: averaging. \(N\) measurements \(z_k=x+\epsilon_k\) with variance \(\sigma^2\). Then \(\mathbf{J}\) is a column of \(N\) ones, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\sigma^2\mathbf{I}\), and \(\mathcal{I}=N/\sigma^2\). No unbiased estimator has variance below \(\sigma^2/N\). The sample mean has exactly that variance, so it cannot be improved.

Example 2: a range. A robot at position \(x\) on a line measures its distance to a beacon 3 m off the line: \(h(x)=\sqrt{x^2+9}\), with \(\sigma=0.1\) m. The Jacobian is \(J=x/\sqrt{x^2+9}\). At \(x=4\): \(J=0.8\), \(\mathcal{I}=0.8^2/0.1^2=64\), so the standard deviation of any unbiased estimate of \(x\) is at least \(1/\sqrt{64}=0.125\) m. At \(x=0\), directly beside the beacon, \(J=0\) and \(\mathcal{I}=0\): a small step along the line does not change the range to first order, so one range says nothing about \(x\) there.

Try it: read the table at the default settings, then switch the histogram between the three estimators.

Notice: with N = 10 and σ = 1 the bound is 0.1. The sample mean's variance is about 1.0 times the bound, and its histogram matches the black curve. The median is unbiased too but wastes information: about 1.4 times the bound. The third estimator has variance 0.65 times the bound, which looks like a violation, but it is biased by about −0.4, and its MSE (about 0.22) is more than twice the bound.

Common mistakes

  • "Nothing can have variance below the CRLB." Nothing unbiased can. An estimator that always answers "7" has variance zero.
  • "The bound on one variable is one over its diagonal entry of the FIM." It is the diagonal entry of the inverse, which is larger whenever the variable is coupled to others.
  • "A solver that reaches the global optimum beats the bound." The bound is about the data, not the solver. A certified optimum (Days 1–2) is the best use of the measurements; the CRLB says how good that best can be.

4. Maximum likelihood, and one number for a matrix

Under regularity conditions, as the number of measurements grows, the maximum likelihood estimator \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{mle}}\) becomes distributed as \(\N\big(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}},\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}})^{-1}\big)\): asymptotically it is unbiased and attains the bound. Since \(\mathbf{x}_{\text{true}}\) is unknown, practice evaluates the FIM at the estimate and reports \(\mathcal{I}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{mle}})^{-1}\) as the covariance of the solution. §6.2.1 · pp. 174–175 This is the covariance SLAM libraries return.

To compare two sensor layouts or two candidate paths you need one number, not a matrix. The field of optimal experimental design supplies three standard choices, all functions of the eigenvalues of the FIM. §6.2.1 · p. 175

CriterionNumberWhat it measuresValue for \(\mathcal{I}=\diag(1,4)\)
D-optimality\(\det\mathcal{I}\) (larger is better)Volume of the uncertainty ellipsoid, which is proportional to \(1/\sqrt{\det\mathcal{I}}\)4
A-optimality\(\tr(\mathcal{I}^{-1})\) (smaller is better)Average variance: the sum of the variance bounds\(1+0.25=1.25\)
E-optimality\(\lambda_{\min}(\mathcal{I})\) (larger is better)Worst direction: the largest variance is \(1/\lambda_{\min}\)1

Going deeper (optional): two caveats

Biased estimators. If the bias is \(b(x)\), the scalar bound becomes \(\operatorname{var}(\hat x)\ge(1+b'(x))^2/\mathcal{I}\). For "0.8 × sample mean", \(b(x)=-0.2x\), so the bound is \(0.64\,\sigma^2/N\), which is what the widget measures.

Rotations and poses. The classical bound assumes parameters in a Euclidean space. For rotations and poses there is a version with an extra term that depends on the curvature of the space; the book notes that it is often negligible when the noise is small compared with the signal (footnote 8, p. 174).

Check yourself

Q1. An estimator has bias 0.3 and variance 0.16. What is its mean squared error?

MSE = bias² + variance = 0.09 + 0.16 = 0.25.

Q2. Two scalar measurements of the same unknown: z₁ = x + ε₁ and z₂ = 2x + ε₂, with independent unit-variance noise. What is the smallest variance an unbiased estimator of x can have?

J = [1; 2] and Σ = I, so the FIM is 1² + 2² = 5 and the bound is 1/5 = 0.2. The second measurement is four times as informative as the first.

Q3. In a simulation, an estimator's variance comes out at 60% of the Cramér-Rao bound, over many trials. What is the most likely explanation?

The bound (6.41) applies to unbiased estimators only. Shrinking an estimate towards a fixed value lowers variance and adds bias, as the third estimator in the widget shows.

Q4. A FIM is diag(9, 1). Which statement is correct?

λmin = 1, so the largest variance bound is 1/1 = 1. The A-criterion is 1/9 + 1 ≈ 1.11, and the D-criterion is 9.

Q5. In Example 2, the robot is at x = 3 (beacon 3 m off the line, σ = 0.1 m). What is the Fisher information for x from one range?

J = 3/√18 = 0.707, so J² = 0.5 and the FIM is 0.5/0.01 = 50.

Recap

  1. MSE = bias² + variance. For an unbiased estimator the covariance is the whole story.
  2. CRLB: \(\cov(\hat{\mathbf{x}})\succeq\mathcal{I}^{-1}\) for every unbiased estimator. The FIM is the expected curvature of the negative log-likelihood at the truth.
  3. With additive Gaussian noise, \(\mathcal{I}=\mathbf{J}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\): the information matrix of SLAM.
  4. Maximum likelihood attains the bound asymptotically, so \(\mathcal{I}(\hat{\mathbf{x}})^{-1}\) is used as the covariance of a SLAM solution.
  5. D, A, and E criteria reduce the FIM to one number: volume, average variance, worst direction.
