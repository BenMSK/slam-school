Week 12 · Review

Week 12 review: from one tracking step to a full visual SLAM system

After today you can carry out by hand a pose-only Gauss-Newton step, a Lucas-Kanade step and a Schur-complement solve, count gauge freedoms, and explain how full visual SLAM systems combine these pieces.

About 60 min · No new pages

The week in one paragraph

Full bundle adjustment is too slow for every frame, so visual SLAM runs three loops at three rates: pose-only BA against a frozen map for every frame, local BA over a covisibility window for every keyframe, and pose-graph optimization or full BA when a loop closes. The residual can be geometric (reprojection error of matched keypoints) or photometric (brightness difference after warping pixels with depth and motion); the photometric one is minimized by Lucas-Kanade-style Gauss-Newton and needs a pyramid to start far from the answer. Inside every BA iteration the Schur complement removes the points and leaves a small reduced camera system, whose fill-in is the covisibility graph; a power series can stand in for its inverse, and variable projection removes the need for a good start. Images alone leave a 7-parameter similarity undetermined. Depth cameras add metric depth and dense TSDF maps, and an IMU adds scale and gravity, cutting the gauge to 4.

2. Mixed quiz

Check yourself

Q1. Which thread of a keypoint-based system changes the positions of map points?

Tracking solves pose-only BA with the points held constant. Local BA in the mapping thread (and full BA after a loop) updates points.

Q2. A camera at the identity pose has \(f_u = 600\) px. A map point lies on the optical axis at depth 3 m. Its keypoint is observed 4 px to the right of the prediction. Using only a translation along \(x\) in the perturbation, what does one Gauss-Newton step give?

The Jacobian entry is f_u / z = 200 px per meter. One residual, one unknown: 4 / 200 = 0.02 m.

Q3. In the photometric loss (7.6), which quantities are unknown?

The book minimizes over all camera parameters and all depth values d_z. The images are the data.

Q4. Lucas-Kanade at full resolution has a basin of about ±3 px on some image. Roughly what basin do you expect with a pyramid of three extra levels, each half the size of the one before?

At level 3 one pixel spans 2³ = 8 original pixels, so a basin of about ±3 level pixels is about ±24 original pixels.

Q5. In BA, camera \(i\) observes point \(j\). Which blocks of the Hessian receive a contribution from this one observation?

The residual depends on camera i and point j only, so JᵀJ gains AᵀA, BᵀB and AᵀB (with its transpose) and nothing else.

Q6. Five cameras in a row; each point is seen only by two neighbouring cameras, and every neighbouring pair shares some point. How many of the 10 off-diagonal block pairs \((i, k)\), \(i \lt k\), of the reduced camera system are non-zero?

A block fills in exactly when two cameras share a point. Only the neighbouring pairs (1,2), (2,3), (3,4), (4,5) do.

Q7. Which sensor setup has a gauge freedom of 6?

The known baseline fixes scale, leaving global position (3) and orientation (3). One camera has 7; with an IMU 4 remain.

Q8. Why is the weighted average (7.12) the right way to fuse signed distances?

Minimizing the sum of squared, variance-weighted differences gives the weighted mean. Watertightness needs extra steps.

3. Pen-and-paper problems

  1. A tracking step with two points. A camera sits at the identity pose with \(f_u = 500\) px. Two map points lie at \((1, 0, 5)\) m and \((-1, 0, 2.5)\) m. Their keypoints are observed \(+2\) px and \(+3\) px away from the predictions in \(u\). Allow only a translation \(\xi_x\) along \(x\) in the perturbation. Find the Gauss-Newton step and the residuals after it.

    Solution

    The \(u\)-row of the Jacobian for a translation along \(x\) is \(f_u/z\): \(J_1 = 500/5 = 100\) and \(J_2 = 500/2.5 = 200\) px per meter. The normal equation is \((J_1^2 + J_2^2)\,\xi_x = J_1 e_1 + J_2 e_2\):

    \[ \xi_x = \frac{100 \cdot 2 + 200 \cdot 3}{100^2 + 200^2} = \frac{800}{50{,}000} = 0.016 \ \text{m}. \]

    Predicted residuals after the step: \(2 - 100 \cdot 0.016 = 0.4\) px and \(3 - 200 \cdot 0.016 = -0.2\) px. One unknown cannot satisfy both observations; least squares leans towards the nearer point because its keypoint is more sensitive to translation.

  2. One Lucas-Kanade step. Let \(I_1(x) = x^2\) and \(I_2(x) = I_1(x - 0.5) = (x - 0.5)^2\). Starting from \(s = 0\) and using the pixels \(x = 1, 2, 3\), compute the update \(\delta\). Why is it not exactly 0.5?

    Solution

    Gradients \(g_x = I_2'(x) = 2(x - 0.5)\): 1, 3, 5. Residuals \(r_x = I_2(x) - I_1(x) = (x-0.5)^2 - x^2 = 0.25 - x\): \(-0.75, -1.75, -2.75\).

    \[ \delta = -\frac{\sum g_x r_x}{\sum g_x^2} = -\frac{-0.75 - 5.25 - 13.75}{1 + 9 + 25} = \frac{19.75}{35} \approx 0.564 . \]

    The true shift is 0.5. The step overshoots because the image is quadratic and Lucas-Kanade treats it as linear between \(x\) and \(x + \delta\). A second step from \(s = 0.564\) removes almost all of the remaining error; that is ordinary Gauss-Newton behaviour.

  3. Schur complement by hand. Two cameras and two points, one unknown each. Camera 1 sees point 1; camera 2 sees both points:

    \[ \mathbf{H}_{cc} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{H}_{cp} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{H}_{pp} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}_c = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b}_p = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

    Solve for \(\mathbf{d}_c\) and \(\mathbf{d}_p\) with equation (7.7). Which point created the off-diagonal entry of the reduced system?

    Solution

    \(\mathbf{H}_{cp}\mathbf{H}_{pp}^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Then

    \[ \mathbf{H}_{cp}\mathbf{H}_{pp}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{cp}\T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{H}^{red}_{cc} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1.5 \end{pmatrix}. \]

    Right-hand side: \(\mathbf{b}_c - \mathbf{H}_{cp}\mathbf{H}_{pp}^{-1}\mathbf{b}_p = (4, 3) - (2, 2) = (2, 1)\). Solving the \(2 \times 2\) system (determinant 2): \(\mathbf{d}_c = (2, 2)\). Back-substitution: \(\mathbf{H}_{pp}\mathbf{d}_p = \mathbf{b}_p - \mathbf{H}_{cp}\T\mathbf{d}_c = (2, 1) - (6, 2) = (-4, -1)\), so \(\mathbf{d}_p = (-2, -1)\).

    Check with the first row of the full system: \(4 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot (-2) + 0 = 4\). The entry \(-1\) comes from point 1, the only point both cameras see: \(-2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = -1\).

  4. Gauge and counting in the flat world (stretch). A flatland camera at position \(\mathbf{c}\) with heading \(\theta\) sees a point \(\mathbf{p}\) at \(u = f\,x_c/z_c\), where \((x_c, z_c)\) are the coordinates of \(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c}\) in the camera's rotated axes. (a) Show that multiplying all camera positions and all points by \(s \gt 0\), with headings unchanged, changes no observation. (b) Three cameras each observe the same \(n\) points. How large must \(n\) be before the observations can determine the solution up to gauge?

    Solution

    (a) Scaling gives \(s\mathbf{p} - s\mathbf{c} = s(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c})\). The rotation into the camera axes is linear, so \((x_c, z_c)\) becomes \((s x_c, s z_c)\) and \(u = f\,s x_c / (s z_c)\) is unchanged.

    (b) Unknowns: \(3 \times 3\) for the cameras and \(2n\) for the points. The gauge (2 shifts, 1 rotation, 1 scale) removes 4, leaving \(5 + 2n\) quantities to determine. Each observation is one number, and there are \(3n\). We need \(3n \ge 5 + 2n\), so \(n \ge 5\). Put differently, a point seen three times spends two observations on its own position and offers one to the cameras, and the cameras need five. With two cameras a point offers nothing (\(2n\) observations for its \(2n\) coordinates), so two flatland views never constrain the motion. In 3-D each observation is two numbers, and two views are enough.
