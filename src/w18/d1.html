Leg odometry: the idea, the frames, and the state

After today you can explain how a legged robot measures its own motion from its joints, name the four kinds of reference frame involved, and read the state vector of equation (12.1).

Today's big idea

A foot that stands on the ground without slipping is a fixed point, and the joint encoders tell the robot where that point is relative to its body. So while the foot stays put, every change in the shape of the leg is a motion of the body. Leg odometry is that observation turned into a measurement: decide which feet are planted, then read the body's motion off their joints.

1. From wheels to legs

Wheel odometry counts how far the wheels turn. A wheel of radius 0.1 m whose encoder reports 3 full turns has rolled \(2\pi \times 0.1 \times 3 = 1.885\) m, as long as it did not skid. The name "leg odometry" was chosen by analogy: the robot infers its motion from the motion of the parts that touch the ground. §12.1 · p. 334

A leg, unlike a wheel, makes and breaks contact. Each stride has a swing phase (the book also calls it the aerial phase), in which the foot is lifted and carried forward, and a stance phase, in which the foot stays on the ground without slipping. Only stance legs push the robot, so only stance legs say anything about how the body moved. That gives the two sub-problems the rest of the chapter solves: §12.1 · p. 334

  1. Contact estimation: which legs are in stance right now? (Day 4)
  2. Motion estimation: how did the body move, given the joints of those legs? (Day 3)

Why bother, when the robot already carries cameras, LiDARs, and an IMU? A walking controller needs pose and velocity at a high rate and with low drift to keep the robot from falling, and the legs supply that information for free. The chapter treats leg odometry as one more measurement for the SLAM factor graph, on the same footing as wheel odometry or IMU preintegration (Week 16 · Day 4). Ch 12 opening · p. 333

In plain words

Stand on one foot and bend your knee. Your foot did not move, so your body did. If you know your own joint angles, you know how far. A legged robot does this hundreds of times a second, switching to the other foot each time it takes a step.

3. Four kinds of frame

The chapter uses these reference frames (Figure 12.1): §12.1.2 · p. 335

  • The inertial (world) frame \(\mathcal{F}^w\), fixed to the ground.
  • The base frame \(\mathcal{F}^b\), fixed to the robot's main body, which is called the floating base because nothing bolts it to the world. The IMU is assumed to sit exactly at \(\mathcal{F}^b\).
  • A foot frame \(\mathcal{F}^{f}\) at the end of each leg.
  • A contact frame \(\mathcal{F}^k\), created each time a foot lands. It is fixed to the ground, not to the robot. For a humanoid's flat foot it coincides with the foot frame at touchdown. For a quadruped's point foot only its position is defined by the foot.

Filling the gap: how to read \(\mathbf{T}^{a}_{b}\)

The chapter writes a pose as a \(4 \times 4\) matrix with two indices and never stops to explain them. \(\mathbf{T}^{w}_{b}\) is the pose of frame \(b\) expressed in frame \(w\). It holds a rotation \(\mathbf{R}^w_b\) and a translation \(\mathbf{t}^w_b\) (the origin of \(b\), in \(w\) coordinates), and it converts the coordinates of a point from \(b\) to \(w\):

\[ \mathbf{T}^w_b = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R}^w_b & \mathbf{t}^w_b \\ \mathbf{0} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{p}^w = \mathbf{R}^w_b\,\mathbf{p}^b + \mathbf{t}^w_b . \]

Two rules do all the work this week.

  • Chain: \(\mathbf{T}^w_f = \mathbf{T}^w_b\,\mathbf{T}^b_f\). The lower index of the first factor must match the upper index of the second, and they cancel.
  • Invert: \((\mathbf{T}^w_b)^{-1} = \mathbf{T}^b_w = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R}\T & -\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{t} \\ \mathbf{0} & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), which swaps the indices.

A worked example in the vertical plane, with coordinates \((x, z)\): \(x\) forward, \(z\) up. The base is at \(\mathbf{t}^w_b = (2.0,\ 0.65)\) m and is pitched by \(10^\circ\), so \(\mathbf{R}^w_b = \begin{bmatrix} 0.985 & -0.174 \\ 0.174 & 0.985 \end{bmatrix}\). The encoders say the foot is at \(\mathbf{p}^b = (0.15,\ -0.65)\) m in the base frame. In the world,

\[ \mathbf{p}^w = \begin{bmatrix} 0.985 \times 0.15 + 0.174 \times 0.65 \\ 0.174 \times 0.15 - 0.985 \times 0.65 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2.0 \\ 0.65 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.261 \\ -0.614 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 2.0 \\ 0.65 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.261 \\ 0.036 \end{bmatrix} . \]

The foot is 3.6 cm above flat ground at \(z = 0\): with this pitch, that leg is in the air. With no pitch (\(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}\)) the same joint angles would put the foot at \((2.15,\ 0)\), on the ground. The orientation of the body matters as much as its position.

4. The state

A legged robot's full state is the pose and velocity of its base plus the positions and velocities of all its joints. The chapter assumes the joints are measured directly by their own sensors (Day 2), so only the base remains to be estimated: §12.1.3 · pp. 335–336

\[ \mathbf{x}_k = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{t}_k & \mathbf{R}_k & \mathbf{v}_k & \boldsymbol{\omega}_k \end{bmatrix}\T \tag{12.1} \]
SymbolMeaningExpressed in
\(\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{t}^w_b \in \R^3\)Position of the baseWorld coordinates
\(\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}^w_b \in \SO(3)\)Orientation of the baseWorld coordinates
\(\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^b_b \in \R^3\)Linear velocity of the baseBase coordinates
\(\boldsymbol{\omega} = \boldsymbol{\omega}^b_b \in \R^3\)Angular velocity of the baseBase coordinates

The pair \((\mathbf{v}, \boldsymbol{\omega})\) is called the base's twist. Because nothing here closes loops, the chapter uses "state estimation" and "odometry" as the same word, and reminds you that odometry is SLAM without loop closures. §12.1.3 · p. 335

Filling the gap: a velocity in base coordinates

\(\mathbf{v}^b_b\) is the velocity of the base relative to the world, with its three numbers written along the base's own axes. To get the world-frame velocity, rotate it: \(\mathbf{v}^w = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{v}\). Example: a robot walks straight ahead at 0.5 m/s. Then \(\mathbf{v} = (0.5, 0, 0)\) whichever way it faces. If it faces along the world's \(y\) axis (a yaw of \(90^\circ\)), then

\[ \mathbf{v}^w = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0.5 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \text{ m/s}. \]

Base coordinates are the natural choice here: the IMU and the leg joints are both attached to the base, so what they measure comes out in base coordinates. You saw the same convention for the gyroscope in Week 16 · Day 1.

Common mistakes

  • "The contact frame is a frame on the robot." It is attached to the ground. It is created at touchdown, and it is abandoned when the foot lifts.
  • "The velocity in (12.1) is the world-frame velocity." Both \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\) are in base coordinates. Only \(\mathbf{t}\) and \(\mathbf{R}\) refer to the world.
  • "A foot on the ground is a foot in stance." Stance means the contact point is not moving. A foot that touches and slides tells you nothing reliable (Day 4).

Check yourself

Q1. A wheel of radius 0.15 m turns 2 full revolutions without skidding. How far did the vehicle roll?

Distance = circumference × turns = 2π × 0.15 × 2 = 1.88 m. Leg odometry replaces "the wheel did not skid" with "the stance foot did not slip".

Q2. Which frame is created anew every time a foot lands, and what is it attached to?

The contact frame is defined at touchdown and stays fixed to the earth. The foot frame travels with the foot, in swing as well as in stance.

Q3. In the plane (x forward, z up), the base is upright (R = I) at t = (1.0, 0.5) m. Forward kinematics puts a foot at (0.2, −0.5) m in the base frame. Where is the foot in the world?

With R = I, the world position is p + t = (0.2 + 1.0, −0.5 + 0.5) = (1.2, 0.0): the foot is on the ground.

Q4. A robot faces along the world's +y axis and walks straight ahead at 0.4 m/s. What is v in the state (12.1)?

The state stores the velocity in base coordinates. "Straight ahead" is the base's own x axis, whatever the heading. (0, 0.4, 0) is the same velocity in world coordinates.

Recap

  1. Leg odometry infers body motion from the joints of the legs that are planted on the ground.
  2. It splits into contact estimation (which legs are in stance) and motion estimation (what those legs say).
  3. Frames: world, base (with the IMU), one per foot, and a contact frame fixed to the ground for each touchdown.
  4. \(\mathbf{T}^w_b\) maps base coordinates to world coordinates; chain by cancelling inner indices, invert by swapping them.
  5. The estimated state (12.1) is base position and orientation in the world, and base linear and angular velocity in base coordinates.
