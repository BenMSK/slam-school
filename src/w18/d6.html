Week 18 review: leg odometry, and all of Part II

After today you can work a leg-odometry problem from joint angles to a factor, and compare the six sensing modalities of Part II by what they measure, where their odometry comes from, and how they fail.

About 75 min · No new pages

The week in one paragraph

A legged robot can read its own motion from its joints. Encoders give the joint angles; forward kinematics turns them into the foot's pose in the base frame, and the Jacobian turns joint rates into foot velocity. While a foot stands still, the foot's motion seen from the body is the body's motion seen from the ground, with the sign flipped. That gives a relative-pose measurement, \(\mathbf{f}_p(\mathbf{q}) = \mathbf{R}\T(\mathbf{c}_i - \mathbf{t})\), and a velocity measurement, \(\mathbf{v} = -\boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{f}_p - \mathbf{J}_v\dot{\mathbf{q}}\). Both depend on knowing which feet are truly stationary, which is decided by thresholding the normal force as a stand-in for the friction cone. With covariances from encoder noise and from a slip model, the measurements become factors: forward-kinematics and contact factors for bipeds, a preintegrated velocity factor for quadrupeds. Slip, soft ground, and bending legs break the assumptions and appear as biased motion.

2. Mixed quiz: Week 18

Check yourself

Q1. In the state (12.1), which quantities are expressed in base coordinates?

t and R give the base pose in the world. The twist (v, ω) is written along the base's own axes.

Q2. A planar leg has l₁ = l₂ = 0.4 m and a knee angle q₂ = −30°. What is the determinant of its Jacobian J_v?

det J_v = l₁ l₂ sin q₂ = 0.16 × (−0.5) = −0.08 m². It does not depend on the hip angle, and it vanishes only for a straight leg.

Q3. A stance foot is at f_p = (0.1, 0, −0.6) m, J_v q̇ = (−0.5, 0, 0) m/s, and the gyroscope reads a pure yaw rate ω = (0, 0, 1) rad/s. What is v from (12.21)?

ω × f_p = (0 × (−0.6) − 1 × 0, 1 × 0.1 − 0, 0) = (0, 0.1, 0). Then v = −(0, 0.1, 0) − (−0.5, 0, 0) = (0.5, −0.1, 0) m/s.

Q4. Encoder noise adds an independent 5 mm (1σ) position error per footstep; slip adds 2 mm per footstep, always backward. After 100 footsteps, what are the two contributions?

Independent errors add in variance: 5 × √100 = 50 mm. A bias adds linearly: 2 × 100 = 200 mm. The smaller per-step error ends up four times larger.

Q5. A foot's force sensor shows a small force that has just begun to rise. How should leg odometry treat that leg?

A small, rising force means the foot is striking but may still slide. Contact for odometry means a stationary contact point, so the leg is not trusted until it is loaded.

Q6. In the contact factor (12.34), a stance lasts four times as long as another. By what factor does the 1σ uncertainty of the contact position grow?

The covariance is proportional to Δt, so the standard deviation is proportional to √Δt: √4 = 2.

3. Pen-and-paper problems

  1. A planar leg has \(l_1 = l_2 = 0.3\) m and joint angles \(q = (45^\circ, -90^\circ)\). (a) Find the foot position \(\mathbf{f}_p\) and the Jacobian \(\mathbf{J}_v\). (b) Check the determinant against \(l_1 l_2 \sin q_2\). (c) The joint rates are \(\dot{\mathbf{q}} = (-2,\ 1)\) rad/s, the foot is in stance, and the body does not rotate. What is the body velocity?

    Solution

    (a) \(q_1 + q_2 = -45^\circ\) and \(\sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = 0.707\). \(x = 0.3(0.707) + 0.3(-0.707) = 0\), \(z = -0.3(0.707) - 0.3(0.707) = -0.424\) m. The Jacobian is

    \[ \mathbf{J}_v = \begin{bmatrix} 0.3(0.707) + 0.3(0.707) & 0.3(0.707) \\ 0.3(0.707) + 0.3(-0.707) & 0.3(-0.707) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.424 & 0.212 \\ 0 & -0.212 \end{bmatrix} \text{ m/rad}. \]

    (b) \(\det = 0.424 \times (-0.212) = -0.090\), and \(l_1 l_2 \sin q_2 = 0.09 \times (-1) = -0.09\). They agree.

    (c) Foot velocity: \(\mathbf{J}_v\dot{\mathbf{q}} = (0.424(-2) + 0.212(1),\ -0.212(1)) = (-0.636,\ -0.212)\) m/s. With no rotation, \(\mathbf{v} = -\mathbf{J}_v\dot{\mathbf{q}} = (0.636,\ 0.212)\) m/s: the body moves forward and rises.

  2. An upright planar biped starts with its base at \(\mathbf{t} = (0,\ 0.50)\) m. Leg A is in stance with \(\mathbf{f}_p^A = (0.10,\ -0.50)\) m. At the end of A's stance, \(\mathbf{f}_p^A = (-0.10,\ -0.50)\) and leg B touches down with \(\mathbf{f}_p^B = (0.12,\ -0.50)\). Later in B's stance, \(\mathbf{f}_p^B = (-0.08,\ -0.52)\). (a) Find the two contact points and the final base position. (b) If foot A in fact slid 3 cm backward during its stance, where is the base really, and does the error go away once B is planted?

    Solution

    (a) Use \(\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{t} + \mathbf{f}_p\) at touchdown and \(\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{f}_p\) afterwards. \(\mathbf{c}_A = (0, 0.50) + (0.10, -0.50) = (0.10,\ 0)\). End of A's stance: \(\mathbf{t} = (0.10, 0) - (-0.10, -0.50) = (0.20,\ 0.50)\). Then \(\mathbf{c}_B = (0.20, 0.50) + (0.12, -0.50) = (0.32,\ 0)\). Finally \(\mathbf{t} = (0.32, 0) - (-0.08, -0.52) = (0.40,\ 0.52)\) m.

    (b) During A's stance the body really advanced \(0.20 - 0.03 = 0.17\) m, so the true final position is \(x = 0.37\) m. The error does not go away: \(\mathbf{c}_B\) was computed from the wrong \(\mathbf{t}\), so it is 3 cm too far forward, and every later estimate inherits that.

  3. The leg of problem 1 stands still (ignore \(\mathbf{h}\)) and its motors hold \(\boldsymbol{\tau} = (-8.49,\ 15.0)\) N·m. (a) Find the ground reaction force \((f_x, f_z)\). (b) Does the foot hold on a floor with \(\mu = 0.3\)? With \(\mu = 0.2\)? (c) What would a 50 N threshold on \(f_z\) conclude in each case?

    Solution

    (a) Solve \(\mathbf{J}_v\T\mathbf{f} = -\boldsymbol{\tau}\) with \(\mathbf{J}_v\T = \begin{bmatrix} 0.424 & 0 \\ 0.212 & -0.212 \end{bmatrix}\). First row: \(0.424 f_x = 8.49\), so \(f_x = 20.0\) N. Second row: \(0.212(20.0) - 0.212 f_z = -15.0\), so \(f_z = (15.0 + 4.24)/0.212 = 90.7\) N.

    (b) The cone allows \(\mu f_z\): \(0.3 \times 90.7 = 27.2\) N, which is more than 20 N, so the foot holds. With \(\mu = 0.2\) the limit is 18.1 N, less than 20 N: the foot slips.

    (c) \(f_z = 90.7\) N exceeds 50 N in both cases, so the detector reports contact both times. On the slippery floor it is wrong, and leg odometry would use a moving foot.

  4. (Stretch.) In the walking widget of Day 3, the rows of \(\mathbf{J}_v\) that map joint noise to the foot's \(x\) coordinate are \((0.65,\ 0.275)\) for the leg that lifts and \((0.65,\ 0.375)\) for the leg that lands, in m/rad. Each encoder reading has independent noise \(\sigma_q = 0.3^\circ\). (a) Find the 1σ error in \(x\) added at one foot switch. (b) The foot also slips 3 mm per step. After how many steps does slip exceed the noise-driven error? (c) Give both after 100 steps, as a fraction of the 30 m walked.

    Solution

    (a) \(\sigma_q = 0.3 \times \pi/180 = 0.00524\) rad. Each leg contributes \(\sigma_q\) times the length of its row: \(0.00524\sqrt{0.65^2 + 0.275^2} = 0.00524 \times 0.706 = 3.7\) mm and \(0.00524\sqrt{0.65^2 + 0.375^2} = 0.00524 \times 0.750 = 3.9\) mm. They are independent, so \(\sigma_{\text{step}} = \sqrt{3.7^2 + 3.9^2} = 5.4\) mm.

    (b) Noise grows as \(5.4\sqrt{n}\) mm, slip as \(3n\) mm. They are equal when \(\sqrt{n} = 5.4/3 = 1.8\), that is \(n = 3.2\). From the fourth step on, slip is the larger error.

    (c) Noise: \(5.4\sqrt{100} = 54\) mm, 0.18% of 30 m. Slip: \(300\) mm, 1%. Over long distances, the bias decides the drift rate, and no encoder upgrade changes it.

4. Part II in review: six sensors, one factor graph

Part I built the back-end: a factor graph and the tools to optimize it. Part II was about front-ends: how each sensor's raw data becomes factors. The Prelude's organizing idea is that almost every front-end has the same modules. An odometry module produces a relative measurement between consecutive nodes. A loop closure module first recognizes a place and then estimates a relative pose to an old node. Some sensors add unary factors on single nodes, such as GPS. §II.1 · pp. 185–187 Each chapter then filled in those modules for one sensor.

Three ideas recurred across the chapters. Odometry drifts, whatever the sensor, and only loop closures or absolute measurements bound it (Week 11 · Day 1). High-rate measurements are summarized between keyframes by preintegration, first for the IMU (Week 16 · Day 4), then for contacts and leg velocities (Day 5). And every sensor leaves some directions unobservable: scale for one camera, global position and yaw for a camera with an IMU (Week 17 · Day 2), so a second modality is added to fill them.

6. Mixed quiz: all of Part II

Check yourself

Q1. A GPS fix is added to a SLAM factor graph. What kind of factor is it?

GPS measures a property of one node, its position, and not a relation between two nodes (§II.1.3, p. 187).

Q2. A pinhole camera has focal length 500 px and principal point (320, 240). A landmark is at (0.2, −0.1, 2.0) m in camera coordinates and is observed at pixel (373, 211). How large is the reprojection error?

Projection: u = 500 × 0.2 / 2.0 + 320 = 370, v = 500 × (−0.1) / 2.0 + 240 = 215. The error is (373 − 370, 211 − 215) = (3, −4), with length 5 px.

Q3. A vision-only system has 7 unknowns that no measurement fixes. Adding an IMU leaves 4. Which 4?

The accelerometer senses gravity, which fixes roll and pitch, and gives metric scale. Where the world origin is and which way is north remain free (p. 221 and §11.3.2, p. 324).

Q4. Which two steps does ICP alternate between?

Each iteration recomputes the correspondence set from the latest transformation, then minimizes the registration error (8.1) over R and t (§8.2.1, p. 228).

Q5. A radar echo returns 1 microsecond after transmission. How far away is the target? (c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s)

d = c t₀ / 2 = 3 × 10⁸ × 10⁻⁶ / 2 = 150 m. The factor 2 is there because the wave travels out and back.

Q6. For which two modalities does the Prelude say odometry can be computed by integrating a measured velocity, with no matching of consecutive measurements?

Doppler gives radial velocities, from which ego-velocity follows; legs give body velocity from kinematics and contact (§II.1.1, p. 186). Cameras and LiDAR match pixels, points, or features between frames.

Q7. An event-camera pixel with contrast threshold C = 0.2 sees its log brightness rise steadily by 0.45. What does it output?

The pixel fires each time the change since its last event reaches C: at +0.2 and at +0.4, both with polarity +1. The remaining 0.05 is below threshold. Events carry a sign, not a magnitude.

Q8. An IMU lies still and level on a table. According to (11.1), with zero bias and noise, what does its accelerometer read?

With a = R(a^w − g^w) and a^w = 0, the reading is −g rotated into the body frame: 9.81 m/s² upward. An accelerometer measures specific force, here the table's push.

Q9. Why are IMU measurements preintegrated before they enter the factor graph?

Naive integration depends on the initial state, so it would have to be redone at every solver iteration. Preintegration separates what depends on the state from what depends only on the measurements (§11.2, p. 308).

Q10. A robot must map a tunnel filled with smoke. Which exteroceptive sensor does the Prelude single out for such conditions, and at what price?

Radar is the least affected by the environment and is one of the few sensors that keep working where LiDAR and cameras are impaired, but its data is noisy and sparse (§II.2.1, p. 189).
