Triangular chirps, angle of arrival, and picking targets out of noise with CFAR

Today's big idea

Radar gets every quantity by comparing two almost identical measurements: two chirp slopes separate range from Doppler, and two antennas give the angle. What comes out is a noisy power map and not a point cloud, so a filter has to decide which cells hold targets. CFAR decides by comparing each cell with its neighbours, which keeps the false alarm rate constant when the noise level changes.

1. Triangular modulation: separating range and Doppler

Day 1 treated the beat frequency as pure range. That is not exact. If the target moves, the echo is also Doppler-shifted, and the beat frequency of an up chirp is the sum of a time-of-flight part \(f_{0,t}\) and a Doppler part \(f_{0,d}\). Triangular modulation follows each up chirp with a down chirp of the same slope. On the down chirp the time-of-flight part changes sign and the Doppler part does not: §9.1.2.4 · p. 256

\[ f_{0,\text{up}} = f_{0,t} + f_{0,d}, \tag{9.6} \] \[ f_{0,\text{down}} = -f_{0,t} + f_{0,d}. \tag{9.7} \]

Two equations, two unknowns. Subtract and add:

\[ f_{0,t} = \frac{f_{0,\text{up}} - f_{0,\text{down}}}{2}, \qquad f_{0,d} = \frac{f_{0,\text{up}} + f_{0,\text{down}}}{2}. \tag{9.8} \]

The range then follows from the time-of-flight part alone, \(d = c f_{0,t}/(2S)\), which is the first half of equation (9.9). §9.1.2.4 · p. 257

Filling the gap: how large is the Doppler part, and why the signs behave this way

Equation (9.4) says the beat phase advances by \(4\pi vT/\lambda\) during a time \(T\). A phase that advances at a steady rate is a frequency: divide by \(2\pi T\) and you get

\[ f_{0,d} = \frac{2v}{\lambda} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad v = \frac{\lambda\, f_{0,d}}{2}. \]

This is the standard two-way Doppler shift. The factor 2 is there because the path to the target and back changes by \(2\,\Delta d\).

For the signs, redo the geometry of Day 1 for a down chirp with frequency \(f_M - St\). The echo that arrives at time \(t\) left at \(t - t_0\), when the frequency was higher, \(f_M - S(t - t_0)\). Transmitted minus received is now \(-S t_0\): the time-of-flight part has flipped. The Doppler shift comes from the motion of the target and does not care which way the chirp slopes.

A caution about the second half of equation (9.9). It is printed as \(v = c f_{0,d}/(2ST)\). The product \(ST\) is the bandwidth \(B\). The derivation above, which uses only the book's own equation (9.4), gives \(v = \lambda f_{0,d}/2 = c f_{0,d}/(2f)\) with the carrier frequency \(f\) in that place. We use \(v = \lambda f_{0,d}/2\) below.

Worked example. Use the radar of Day 1: \(S = 7.5\times10^{12}\) Hz/s, \(\lambda = 3.896\) mm. A target at 12 m moves away at 5 m/s. Then \(f_{0,t} = 600\) kHz and \(f_{0,d} = 2\times5/0.003896 = 2.57\) kHz.

2. Angle of arrival

A spinning radar knows the angle of a return: it is where the antenna points. An SoC radar has to compute it. Take two receive antennas (RX) a distance \(\ell\) apart, and a far target at angle \(\theta\) from the array's forward direction. The reflected wave reaches the two antennas along parallel paths, and the path to the second antenna is longer by \(\Delta d = \ell\sin\theta\) (Figure 9.5). A path difference is a phase difference: §9.1.2.5 · p. 257

\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,\ell\sin\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \theta = \arcsin\frac{\lambda\,\Delta\phi}{2\pi\ell}. \tag{9.10} \]

The factor is \(2\pi/\lambda\) here and \(4\pi/\lambda\) in equation (9.3). In (9.3) a range change is travelled twice, out and back. Here the extra path is travelled once, on the way in.

Worked example. Antennas half a wavelength apart, \(\ell = \lambda/2\), measure \(\Delta\phi = \pi/2\). Then \(\sin\theta = (\lambda\cdot\pi/2)/(2\pi\cdot\lambda/2) = 1/2\), so \(\theta = 30°\).

Filling the gap: antenna spacing and angular resolution

As with velocity, the phase is unambiguous only while \(|\Delta\phi| \lt \pi\). From (9.10) that means \(|\sin\theta| \lt \lambda/(2\ell)\). With \(\ell = \lambda/2\) every angle from −90° to 90° is covered, which is why arrays use half-wavelength spacing. With \(\ell = \lambda\) only angles within ±30° are unambiguous.

With \(N\) antennas in a row, the phase steps by the same \(\Delta\phi\) from one antenna to the next, and an FFT across the antennas finds it, exactly like the FFT across chirps on Day 1. §9.1.2.5 · p. 257 By the same resolution argument, \(N\) samples resolve \(\Delta\phi\) to \(2\pi/N\), so two targets can be separated when

\[ \Delta(\sin\theta) \approx \frac{\lambda}{N\ell} = \frac{2}{N} \quad\text{for } \ell = \lambda/2 . \]

Eight antennas give 0.25 rad, about 14° straight ahead. This is the reason radar angles are coarse next to LiDAR, and the reason a point far away is uncertain sideways: 0.25 rad at 50 m is 12.5 m. Keep it in mind for Day 3, where registration methods give far points a larger covariance.

The two-receiver example is a single-input, multiple-output (SIMO) array. Most SLAM radars are MIMO: several TX and several RX antennas. Adding one more transmitter gives the same angular resolution as doubling the receivers, provided the receivers can tell the transmitters apart. They can if the transmitters use different frequency bands, different codes, or different time slots (TDMA). A two-dimensional array measures elevation as well, which gives 3+1D data. §9.1.2.5 · p. 258

3. What goes wrong: speckle, multipath, motion distortion

Radar data is harder to use than LiDAR data, and the reasons are physical. §9.1.3 · pp. 258–259

Speckle noise. One pulse collects energy from everything inside a wide beam. The scattered waves add up, sometimes reinforcing and sometimes cancelling. Together with thermal noise and changing RCS this produces peaks where there is no object and removes peaks where there is one. Without a way to tell them apart, the output is a scatter of spurious points.

Multipath. Part of the energy reaches the target and returns by way of the ground or a wall. The radar only knows the path length and the arrival direction, so it places a second target beyond the reflecting surface: a ghost object.

Motion distortion. A spinning radar builds one image during one rotation. The book's example sensor rotates at 4 Hz, so a scan takes 0.25 s. A car at 20 m/s moves 5 m in that time, and an object seen at the start and at the end of the rotation is recorded 5 m apart. Faster sensors exist (10 Hz, up to 50 Hz), but on a fast vehicle the effect stays significant. §9.1.3.3 · p. 259 Day 3 shows how to undo it. The book also lists biased and sparse range readings caused by the wide beam, and receiver saturation. §9.1.3 · p. 258

4. Filtering with CFAR

The obvious filter, keeping every cell above a fixed power, fails because the noise level is not fixed. It changes with range, direction and surroundings, so one threshold is too strict in one place and lets false alarms through in another. §9.1.4 · p. 259

The constant false alarm rate (CFAR) filter works along one azimuth and slides a window over the range cells. The cell being judged is the cell under test (CUT). The training cells on both sides estimate the local noise level. Optional guard cells next to the CUT are left out, so that a target that spills into its neighbours does not raise its own noise estimate. In cell-averaging CFAR (CA-CFAR) the threshold is the mean power of the training cells times a multiplier chosen from the wanted probability of false alarm. §9.1.4 · p. 260

Filling the gap: where the multiplier comes from

The book says the multiplier is "selected from the desired probability of false alarm" and stops. Here is the calculation for the standard noise model, in which the power \(x\) of a noise-only cell is exponentially distributed with mean \(\mu\): \(p(x) = \frac{1}{\mu}e^{-x/\mu}\). This is what a receiver produces when it squares the two components of complex Gaussian noise.

If \(\mu\) were known, a threshold \(\tau\) would be exceeded by noise with probability \(P(x \gt \tau) = e^{-\tau/\mu}\). Setting this equal to \(P_{fa}\) gives \(\tau = -\mu\ln P_{fa}\). For \(P_{fa} = 10^{-3}\) the threshold is \(6.91\,\mu\).

But \(\mu\) is unknown, so CA-CFAR estimates it from \(N\) training cells, \(\hat\mu = \frac1N\sum_i x_i\), and uses \(\tau = \alpha\hat\mu\). The threshold is now random, so average the false alarm probability over it. The training cells are independent, and for an exponential variable \(\E[e^{-s x}] = 1/(1 + s\mu)\):

\[ P_{fa} = \E\!\left[e^{-\alpha\hat\mu/\mu}\right] = \prod_{i=1}^{N}\E\!\left[e^{-\frac{\alpha}{N\mu}x_i}\right] = \left(1 + \frac{\alpha}{N}\right)^{-N}. \]

The unknown \(\mu\) has cancelled. Whatever the noise level, the false alarm probability is the same: that is the "constant" in CFAR. Solve for the multiplier:

\[ \alpha = N\left(P_{fa}^{-1/N} - 1\right). \]

For \(N = 20\) and \(P_{fa} = 10^{-3}\): \(\alpha = 20\,(10^{0.15} - 1) = 20 \times 0.4125 = 8.25\). That is more than 6.91, the price of having to estimate the noise. With only \(N = 4\) cells, \(\alpha = 4\,(10^{0.75} - 1) = 18.5\). As \(N\) grows, \(\alpha\) falls towards \(-\ln P_{fa}\).

Try it: the grey curve is the power along one azimuth, with five targets and a stretch of stronger clutter. Compare the adaptive threshold with the dashed fixed one, then set the training cells to 3 per side.

Notice: at the start CA-CFAR finds 4 of the 5 targets with no false alarm, while the fixed threshold finds all 5 and adds 24 false alarms inside the clutter. The target CFAR misses is the one at 31 m: the stronger target 3 m behind it lies in its training window and lifts the threshold. With 3 training cells per side the window is short enough, all 5 are found, and the multiplier has risen from 8.25 to 12.97. At "1 in 100" two false alarms appear, exactly at the two edges of the clutter, where half the window sees quiet noise.

The misses and edge effects you just saw are the known weaknesses of averaging. Ordered-statistic CFAR (OS-CFAR) replaces the mean by a robust statistic such as the median, which a single strong neighbour cannot move. BFAR (bounded false alarm rate), designed for radar odometry, applies an affine map \(T = aZ + b\) to the CFAR output \(Z\), with a learnable \(b\), so that the result blends the adaptive threshold with a fixed level. §9.1.4 · p. 260

Many odometry and SLAM pipelines use something simpler than CFAR. CFAR was built for detection, where missing a weak target is costly. Odometry wants only points that will be found again in the next scan and from another viewpoint. Two common choices are to keep the k strongest returns above a static threshold along each azimuth, or to keep every return more than one standard deviation above the mean. In Figure 9.7 CFAR reports several false alarms, and k-strongest with k = 12 keeps only points around the main targets. §9.1.4 · pp. 260–261 A third route is learned filtering: networks trained with LiDAR as ground truth that keep only the returns a LiDAR would also see. §9.1.4 · pp. 260–261

Common mistakes

  • "CFAR removes false alarms." It fixes their rate. At \(P_{fa} = 10^{-3}\), a radargram with a million cells still produces about a thousand of them per scan.
  • "More training cells are always better." They lower the multiplier, but they assume the noise is the same across the whole window. A window that reaches another target or a clutter edge gives a wrong noise estimate.
  • "Ghosts average out like noise." A multipath ghost is repeatable from the same place. It has to be rejected as an outlier, not averaged.

Check yourself

Q1. A triangular radar measures a beat of 410 kHz on the up chirp and −390 kHz on the down chirp. What are the time-of-flight and Doppler parts?

By (9.8): half the difference is (410 − (−390))/2 = 400 kHz, half the sum is (410 + (−390))/2 = 10 kHz.

Q2. Two receive antennas are half a wavelength apart and measure a phase difference of π/3. What is the angle of arrival?

sin θ = λΔφ/(2πℓ) = (π/3)/π = 1/3, so θ = arcsin(1/3) ≈ 19.5°.

Q3. A CA-CFAR filter uses N = 10 training cells and a false alarm probability of 0.01. What multiplier does it need?

α = N(P_fa^(−1/N) − 1) = 10 × (100^0.1 − 1) = 10 × 0.585 = 5.85. The value 4.61 = −ln 0.01 would be right only if the noise level were known exactly.

Q4. Why do radar odometry pipelines often keep the k strongest returns per azimuth instead of running CFAR?

CFAR was designed for detection, where a missed weak target is costly, and it passes ambiguous peaks. Odometry wants points that are detected again in later scans and from other viewpoints (p. 260).

Recap

  1. An up chirp measures \(f_{0,t} + f_{0,d}\) and a down chirp \(-f_{0,t} + f_{0,d}\). Half the difference gives range, half the sum gives the Doppler shift \(2v/\lambda\).
  2. Two antennas \(\ell\) apart see a phase difference \((2\pi/\lambda)\ell\sin\theta\), so \(\theta = \arcsin(\lambda\Delta\phi/(2\pi\ell))\). More antennas, real or virtual (MIMO), give finer angles.
  3. Radar suffers from speckle, multipath ghosts, wide beams and, for spinning sensors, motion distortion.
  4. CA-CFAR compares each cell with \(\alpha\) times the mean of its training cells, with \(\alpha = N(P_{fa}^{-1/N} - 1)\). The false alarm rate then does not depend on the noise level.
  5. SLAM pipelines often prefer conservative filters such as k-strongest, because they need repeatable points.
