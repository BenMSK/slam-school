Week 8 review: dense maps

After today you can update occupancy and TSDF voxels by hand, extract a surface from a distance grid, size a map's memory, and argue for a representation that fits a task.

About 60 min · No new pages

The week in one paragraph

With the trajectory known, dense mapping turns range measurements into a model of all the geometry. Beams become points through the sensor's angles or intrinsics. An occupancy grid stores a per-cell probability and updates it by adding log-odds. A TSDF stores an averaged, clamped distance along beams, and its zero crossing is the surface. These quantities are carried by points, surfels, meshes, voxels, or continuous functions, and Marching Cubes converts a distance grid into a mesh. Arrays, hash tables, and octrees trade lookup speed against memory. Gaussian processes and Hilbert maps drop the grid and model correlation and uncertainty, at a higher cost. The right choice depends on the environment and on what the downstream task asks of the map.

2. Mixed quiz

Check yourself

Q1. A 2D LiDAR sits at (1, 2) in the map with heading 0. A beam at azimuth 150° returns r = 4 m. Where is the hit point in the map?

In the sensor frame the point is (4 cos 150°, 4 sin 150°) = (−3.46, 2.00). Adding the sensor position gives (−2.46, 4.00). The second option forgets the pose.

Q2. A cell has log-odds l = −1.2. One beam ends in it, and a hit means p = 0.7. What is its probability now?

l = −1.2 + log(0.7/0.3) = −1.2 + 0.847 = −0.353, and p = 1/(1 + e^0.353) = 0.41. One hit does not overturn the earlier evidence that the cell is free.

Q3. A round robot of radius 0.30 m is centred where the ESDF reads 0.25 m with gradient (0.6, 0.8). What is the smallest move that frees it?

The gradient has length 1 and points away from the nearest surface, so each centimetre along it gains one centimetre of clearance. The robot needs 0.30 − 0.25 = 0.05 m more.

Q4. Which statement about a TSDF built from projective distances is true?

The distance along a beam is at least the distance to the nearest surface point, so values are overestimates. Both distances are zero at the same place, which is why the extracted surface is still correct.

Q5. A marching-squares cell has corner values −0.2 (top left), 0.1 (top right), 0.3 (bottom right), −0.4 (bottom left). On which edges does it place vertices?

An edge gets a vertex when its two ends differ in sign: top (−0.2, 0.1) and bottom (−0.4, 0.3). The left edge is negative at both ends, the right edge positive at both. This is case 1 + 8 = 9.

Q6. Voxels are grouped in blocks of 8 × 8 × 8. Which block contains the voxel with index (37, 5, 70)?

Divide by 8 and round down: (⌊37/8⌋, ⌊5/8⌋, ⌊70/8⌋) = (4, 0, 8). The remainders (5, 5, 6) are the voxel's position inside the block.

Q7. A 3D map stores only voxels near surfaces. Going one octree level deeper (half the voxel size), by about what factor does the number of stored voxels grow?

A surface is two-dimensional, so the number of cells it crosses grows with the square of the resolution: 2² = 4. A dense grid would grow by 2³ = 8.

Q8. Why can a robot with a GP map rate a candidate measurement location before measuring there?

Equation (5.11) contains kernel matrices and the noise variance, but not y. The reduction in uncertainty from a new sample can be computed from its position alone.

3. Pen-and-paper problems

  1. An inverse sensor model assigns \(p = 0.8\) to the cell where a beam ends and \(p = 0.3\) to cells a beam passes through. A cell starts unknown and is observed as: hit, hit, pass, hit. (a) Find its log-odds and probability. (b) The object is then removed, and every later beam passes through the cell. How many passes until its probability drops below 0.5?

    Solution

    (a) A hit adds \(\log(0.8/0.2) = \log 4 = 1.386\). A pass adds \(\log(0.3/0.7) = -0.847\). So \(l = 3 \times 1.386 - 0.847 = 3.312\). The odds are \(4^3 \times 3/7 = 27.4\), and \(p = 27.4/28.4 = 0.965\).

    (b) Each pass subtracts 0.847, and \(3.312/0.847 = 3.9\), so 4 passes are needed: \(l = 3.312 - 3.389 = -0.077\), \(p = 0.48\). Had the cell been hit 100 times first, \(l\) would be 138.6 and 164 passes would be needed. This is why implementations clamp \(l\) to a fixed interval: a map of a changing world must be able to change its mind.

  2. Four voxel centres lie on one beam at 1.9, 2.0, 2.1, and 2.2 m from the sensor. Three scans from the same pose return ranges 2.06, 2.02, and 2.07 m along that beam. With \(\tau = 0.15\) m and weight 1 per measurement, find \(D\) and \(W\) for each voxel, then the surface position.

    Solution

    The projective distance is \(r\) minus the voxel's distance. Values above \(+\tau\) are clamped to 0.15, and values below \(-\tau\) are skipped.

    Voxel 1.9: 0.16, 0.12, 0.17 become 0.15, 0.12, 0.15, so \(D = 0.14\), \(W = 3\). Voxel 2.0: 0.06, 0.02, 0.07, so \(D = 0.05\), \(W = 3\). Voxel 2.1: −0.04, −0.08, −0.03, so \(D = -0.05\), \(W = 3\). Voxel 2.2: −0.14, −0.18 (skipped), −0.13, so \(D = -0.135\), \(W = 2\).

    The sign changes between 2.0 and 2.1: \(x^{*} = 2.0 + 0.1 \times 0.05/(0.05 + 0.05) = 2.05\) m. That equals the mean of the three ranges, \(6.15/3 = 2.05\) m. Inside the band nothing was clamped, so the averaging of distances is the averaging of ranges.

  3. A signed distance function \(f(x, y) = \sqrt{(x-1)^2 + (y-1)^2} - 0.8\) is sampled at the nine points with \(x, y \in \{0, 1, 2\}\). (a) Give the sign at each sample. (b) Run marching squares with linear interpolation: where are the vertices, and what shape results? (c) Compare its perimeter with the true one. (d) Where would the vertices be without interpolation?

    Solution

    (a) Centre \((1, 1)\): \(-0.8\). The four edge midpoints such as \((1, 0)\): \(1 - 0.8 = +0.2\). The four corners: \(\sqrt{2} - 0.8 = +0.61\). Only the centre is inside.

    (b) Sign changes occur on the four grid edges that leave the centre sample. On each, \(t = f_a/(f_a - f_b) = -0.8/(-0.8 - 0.2) = 0.8\) from the centre. The vertices are \((1 \pm 0.8, 1)\) and \((1, 1 \pm 0.8)\). Each of the four cells has one inside corner and contributes one segment, so the outline is a square standing on its corner.

    (c) Each side is \(0.8\sqrt{2} = 1.13\), so the perimeter is 4.53, against \(2\pi \times 0.8 = 5.03\) for the circle. The vertices lie exactly on the circle, because \(f\) is linear along a line through the centre. The 10% error comes from the straight segments between them, which a finer grid would shorten.

    (d) At the edge midpoints, 0.5 from the centre: a square of perimeter 2.83 whose vertices are 0.3 off the true outline.

  4. A zero-mean GP has kernel \(\mathcal{K}(x, x') = \exp(-(x - x')^2/2)\) and noise-free measurements \(y_1 = y_2 = 1\) at \(x_1 = 0\) and \(x_2 = 2\). (a) Find the predictive mean and variance at \(x_* = 1\). (b) What changes if both measurements are 5 instead of 1?

    Solution

    (a) With \(e^{-2} = 0.135\) and \(e^{-0.5} = 0.607\),

    \[ \mathbf{K} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.135 \\ 0.135 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{K}_* = \begin{bmatrix} 0.607 & 0.607 \end{bmatrix}. \]

    By symmetry \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{y}\) has two equal entries \(\alpha\) with \(\alpha(1 + 0.135) = 1\), so \(\alpha = 0.881\). The mean is \(2 \times 0.607 \times 0.881 = 1.07\). In the same way \(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_*\T\) has entries \(0.607/1.135 = 0.534\), so the variance is \(1 - 2 \times 0.607 \times 0.534 = 0.35\), a standard deviation of 0.59.

    The mean sits slightly above both samples because this kernel prefers smooth curves, and the smoothest curve through two equal values one length-scale either side bulges a little. The standard deviation of 0.59 says not to rely on it.

    (b) The mean scales with the data to \(5 \times 1.07 = 5.34\). The variance stays 0.35: equation (5.11) does not contain \(\mathbf{y}\).
