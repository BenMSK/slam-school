Today's big idea

A visual-inertial odometry system is the preintegration factor graph of Week 16 with vision factors added, solved over a short sliding window so that the answer arrives within milliseconds. States that leave the window are marginalized into a prior. That prior is frozen at old linearization points, and if newer factors on the same variables are linearized elsewhere, the estimator gains information about global yaw that no sensor supplied. The observability analysis of Day 3 is what detects this and what fixes it.

1. What a VIO has to deliver

A VIO is used as the odometry source inside control loops and in virtual-reality headsets, and both need the estimate with very low delay, typically 10–50 ms. The book's example is a headset that refreshes between 72 and 120 times per second: that is one frame every \(1/72 \approx 13.9\) ms to \(1/120 \approx 8.3\) ms. A late pose shows the user a world that lags behind the head, and in a controller a long delay can destabilize tracking. §11.4.1 · pp. 326–327

So the estimator cannot optimize the whole trajectory at every frame. It has to bound its work.

3. Fixed-lag smoothing and marginalization

Factor-graph VIO systems usually run a fixed-lag smoother, also called sliding-window optimization: only the states of a receding horizon, for example the last 5 to 10 seconds, are optimized. A longer horizon is more accurate and costs more. States and factors that fall out of the horizon are marginalized. §11.4.1 · p. 327

Filling the gap: what marginalizing a state does to the information matrix

Split the unknowns into the part to remove (\(m\)) and the part to keep (\(r\)). The linearized factors that touch \(m\) give an information matrix in blocks. Marginalizing is the Schur complement of Week 3 · Day 3:

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{mm} & \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{mr} \\ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{rm} & \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{rr} \end{bmatrix} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{prior}} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{rr} - \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{rm}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{mm}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{mr} . \]

Smallest example. Two scalar positions \(x_0, x_1\). A prior on \(x_0\) with variance 0.25 (information 4) and an odometry factor \(x_1 - x_0 = u\) with variance 1 (information 1) give

\[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 + 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \Lambda_{\text{prior}} = 1 - (-1)\cdot\tfrac15\cdot(-1) = 0.8 . \]

The variance of \(x_1\) is \(1/0.8 = 1.25 = 0.25 + 1\): the two variances add, as they should along a chain.

Marginalization keeps unobservable directions unobservable. Suppose \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}\) with \(\mathbf{u} = (\mathbf{u}_m, \mathbf{u}_r)\). The first block row says \(\mathbf{u}_m = -\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{mm}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{mr}\mathbf{u}_r\). Insert that into the second block row, \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{rm}\mathbf{u}_m + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{rr}\mathbf{u}_r = \mathbf{0}\), and you get \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{prior}}\mathbf{u}_r = \mathbf{0}\). The prior has the same null direction, restricted to the variables that remain. Without the prior on \(x_0\) in the example, \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) has the null vector \((1, 1)\) and \(\Lambda_{\text{prior}} = 1 - 1 = 0\): nothing is known about \(x_1\) alone.

The alternative the book mentions is an incremental solver such as iSAM2 (Week 3 · Day 5), which keeps the whole history and reuses earlier computation. It can be very accurate, but its run time per update is not bounded and can spike, which a latency-critical system cannot accept. §11.4.1 · p. 327

4. Consistency and first-estimate Jacobians

The book introduces observability analysis partly as a tool "to improve estimation consistency", and its example system is named after the technique: First-Estimate Jacobian (FEJ) window bundle adjustment. §11.3 · p. 320; §11.4.1 · p. 328 It does not explain the mechanism. Here it is.

An estimator is consistent when the covariance it reports matches the errors it actually makes. An inconsistent estimator is usually overconfident: it reports a small covariance and then trusts new measurements too little.

Filling the gap: how a sliding window invents information

A marginalization prior is a quadratic built from Jacobians evaluated at the estimates of that moment. Call them estimate A. The original factors are gone, so the prior can never be relinearized. Later, new factors arrive that involve some of the same variables, and Gauss-Newton linearizes them at the current estimates. Call them estimate B.

Now look at the yaw null vector of Day 3. Its blocks are \(-\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{g}\) and \(-\mathbf{p}_f\times\mathbf{g}\): they contain the values of the variables themselves. The prior's information matrix has the null vector built from A. The new factors have the null vector built from B. If A and B differ, no single vector is in both nullspaces, and the sum of the two information matrices has no yaw null vector at all. Its rank has gone up by one. The estimator now holds a finite variance for global yaw, although no sensor measured it. That is information from nowhere, and it makes the estimator overconfident.

The three shift vectors contain only identity blocks. They do not depend on the linearization point, so they survive.

The fix. For every variable that takes part in the prior, keep evaluating Jacobians at the estimate that was used when the prior was built, its "first estimate", even though the variable's value keeps being updated. Then all factors share one null vector and yaw stays unobservable. The price is a slightly less accurate linearization.

Try it: a window of 2-D poses and landmarks after the first pose has been marginalized. Move the slider to zero and back, then switch on first-estimate Jacobians.

Notice: with the slider at zero, A and B coincide and three eigenvalues are zero (x, y, yaw). As soon as the estimate has moved, only two are zero and a third small eigenvalue appears: the estimator now "knows" its global yaw. With first-estimate Jacobians the third zero comes back for any slider value.

5. How good is it?

The book lists widely used systems (a visual-inertial ORB-SLAM, Direct Sparse Visual-Inertial Odometry, VINS-Mono, OpenVINS, Kimera, BASALT, DM-VIO) and gives a yardstick: a good VIO drifts by less than 1% of the distance travelled, and in some cases as little as 0.1%. §11.4.1 · pp. 327–328

Its worked example is a sliding-window FEJ system on a 36-minute, 11.42 km drive through a city (KAIST urban dataset, sequence 38), with a camera at 10 Hz and an IMU at 100 Hz, so ten IMU samples are preintegrated per frame. With no loop closure, the final error is about 2.05° and 21.2 m, which the book states as 0.18% of the distance. §11.4.1 · pp. 328–329, Fig. 11.5

6. Calibration and time synchronization

Extrinsic calibration is the estimation of the relative pose between sensors, for example the camera with respect to the IMU. Every measurement model this week assumed it known. §11.4.2 · p. 329

ApproachHowForAgainst
Offlinea procedure before deployment: a calibration target, a known motion pattern, or prior knowledge of the environmentgenerally more accuratetakes time, equipment, and trained operators
Onlinethe extrinsic parameters are added to the estimated stateno procedure; adapts if a sensor shiftscan be less accurate; the problem gets harder and may become ill-posed

"Ill-posed" is this week's subject again: the extrinsics are extra unknowns, and for some motions they are unobservable.

Temporal synchronization makes sure all sensors agree on when each measurement was taken. A time offset that is not accounted for corrupts the trajectory estimate and can bias benchmark results. §11.4.3 · pp. 329–330 The size of the effect is plain kinematics: if an image is stamped \(t_d\) too late, the estimator pairs it with a pose that is off by about \(\mathbf{v}\,t_d\) in position and \(\boldsymbol{\omega}\,t_d\) in angle. At 10 m/s and \(t_d = 5\) ms that is 5 cm; at a turn rate of 100°/s it is 0.5°.

The book lists the options: a hardware trigger that fires all sensors from one clock; a clock-synchronization protocol such as PTP over Ethernet; time-stamping at the sensor and aligning afterwards, which is less accurate and robust; or, when none of these is possible, estimating the time offset as one more state variable. §11.4.3 · p. 330

Common mistakes

  • "States that leave the window are thrown away." They are marginalized: what they implied about the remaining states stays, as a prior factor.
  • "First-estimate Jacobians freeze the estimate." The estimate keeps moving. Only the point at which the Jacobians are evaluated is held, and only for variables tied to the prior.
  • "A drift of 0.18% means the pose is accurate to 0.18%." It is the final position error divided by the path length, without loop closure. The error keeps growing with distance.

Check yourself

Q1. A sliding window holds 8 keyframes and 60 point landmarks. How many unknowns does the optimizer have before any elimination?

\(8 \times 15 = 120\) for poses, velocities, and biases, plus \(60 \times 3 = 180\) for the landmarks.

Q2. Scalar states \(x_0, x_1\). A prior on \(x_0\) has variance 1, the odometry factor between them has variance 1. After marginalizing \(x_0\), what is the information on \(x_1\)?

\(\boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(\Lambda_{\text{prior}} = 1 - (-1)\cdot\tfrac12\cdot(-1) = 0.5\). The variance is 2, the sum of the two variances.

Q3. Why does a latency-critical VIO usually prefer a fixed-lag smoother to iSAM2?

The window has a fixed size, so each solve costs about the same. iSAM2 can be very accurate but gives no guarantee on latency.

Q4. In a sliding window without first-estimate Jacobians, which unobservable direction is typically lost, and why that one?

The yaw null vector contains the positions themselves. A prior linearized at one estimate and new factors linearized at another do not share it. The shift null vectors are the same at every linearization point.

Q5. A camera's time stamps are 10 ms late on a vehicle moving at 20 m/s. Roughly how large is the position error this introduces for each image?

\(20\ \text{m/s} \times 0.010\ \text{s} = 0.2\) m.

Recap

  1. A VIO must answer within about 10–50 ms, so it optimizes a sliding window of recent keyframes: 15 unknowns per keyframe plus 3 per landmark.
  2. Its factors are preintegrated IMU, bias, projection, and prior factors.
  3. States leaving the window are marginalized with a Schur complement, which preserves unobservable directions.
  4. Mixing linearization points for one variable destroys the yaw null vector and makes the estimator overconfident. First-estimate Jacobians prevent it.
  5. Good systems drift by well under 1% of the distance travelled. Extrinsic calibration and time synchronization have to be right for that.
