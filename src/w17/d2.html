Week 17 · Day 2

Linearized measurement models for points, lines, and planes

After today you can write the measurement Jacobian of a point, line, or plane feature as a chain of two factors, and say which part of the state each factor can and cannot see.

Today's big idea

Every exteroceptive measurement is a function of a feature expressed in the sensor frame. So every measurement Jacobian is a product: a sensor-specific factor (how the reading changes when the feature moves in the sensor frame) times a geometric factor (how the feature in the sensor frame changes when the state changes). The geometric factor only ever sees the feature relative to the sensor, and that single fact produces the unobservable directions of Day 3.

1. Point features: range, bearing, and the switch matrix

A point landmark \(\mathbf{p}^w_f\) in the world appears in the sensor frame \(\mathcal{F}^c\) at §11.3.1.2 · p. 322

\[ \mathbf{p}^c_f = \mathbf{R}^c_w\,(\mathbf{p}^w_f - \mathbf{p}^w_c) . \]

A sensor can report the point's range \(z_r = \norm{\mathbf{p}^c_f}\) (how far), its bearing \(\mathbf{z}_b = h_b(\mathbf{p}^c_f)\) (in which direction, two numbers), or both. The book writes all cases at once, equation (11.42):

\[ \mathbf{z}_p = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_r & \mathbf{0}_{1\times 2} \\ \mathbf{0}_{2\times 1} & \lambda_b \mathbf{I}_2 \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}} \begin{bmatrix} \norm{\mathbf{p}^c_f} + \eta^r \\ h_b(\mathbf{p}^c_f) + \boldsymbol{\eta}^b \end{bmatrix} . \]

\(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) is a measurement selection matrix: \(\lambda_r\) and \(\lambda_b\) are each 0 or 1 and switch the range row and the bearing rows on or off.

Sensor\(\lambda_r\)\(\lambda_b\)What it reports about a point
Monocular camera01direction only
LiDAR, stereo or depth camera11direction and distance

2. The chain rule, one factor at a time

Linearizing (11.42) at the estimate \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) gives the error equation (11.43): §11.3.1.2 · p. 322

\[ \tilde{\mathbf{z}}_p = \mathbf{z}_p - \hat{\mathbf{z}}_p \simeq \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{H}_r \\ \mathbf{H}_b \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}_f\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}} + \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\begin{bmatrix} \eta^r \\ \boldsymbol{\eta}^b \end{bmatrix} =: \mathbf{H}_x\tilde{\mathbf{x}} + \boldsymbol{\eta}^p, \qquad \mathbf{H}_r = \frac{\partial z_r}{\partial \mathbf{p}^c_f},\ \ \mathbf{H}_b = \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_b}{\partial \mathbf{p}^c_f},\ \ \mathbf{H}_f = \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}^c_f}{\partial \mathbf{x}} . \]

The book stops at the names. The three factors are short enough to write out.

Filling the gap: the three Jacobians

Write \(\mathbf{p} = (x, y, z)\T\) for \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f\).

Range. \(z_r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}\), so \(\partial z_r/\partial x = x/\norm{\mathbf{p}}\) and likewise for \(y, z\):

\[ \mathbf{H}_r = \frac{\mathbf{p}\T}{\norm{\mathbf{p}}} \quad (1\times 3). \]

It is the unit vector along the ray. Range responds only to motion of the point along the ray.

Bearing. The book leaves \(h_b\) generic. For a pinhole camera in normalized image coordinates (Week 11 · Day 3), \(h_b(\mathbf{p}) = (x/z,\ y/z)\T\), and differentiating each entry gives

\[ \mathbf{H}_b = \frac{1}{z}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -x/z \\ 0 & 1 & -y/z \end{bmatrix} \quad (2\times 3). \]

Multiply it by \(\mathbf{p}\): the first row gives \(\tfrac1z(x - x) = 0\), the second \(\tfrac1z(y - y) = 0\). So \(\mathbf{H}_b\,\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{0}\): sliding the point along its own ray does not change its bearing. A bearing measurement carries no depth information. This holds for every bearing function, because a bearing by definition depends only on the direction of \(\mathbf{p}\).

Geometry. Take the camera frame equal to the body (IMU) frame to keep the algebra short (so \(\mathbf{R}^c_w = (\mathbf{R}^w_b)\T\) and \(\mathbf{p}^w_c = \mathbf{p}^w\)), and insert the error state of Day 1, \(\mathbf{R}^w_b = \hat{\mathbf{R}}(\mathbf{I} + \tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\wedge})\). Since \((\mathbf{I} + \tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\wedge})\T = \mathbf{I} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\wedge}\),

\[ \mathbf{p}^c_f = (\mathbf{I} - \tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\wedge})\,\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\big(\hat{\mathbf{p}}_f + \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_f - \hat{\mathbf{p}} - \tilde{\mathbf{p}}\big) \simeq \hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f + (\hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f)^{\wedge}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}} - \hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\tilde{\mathbf{p}} + \hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_f , \]

where \(-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\wedge}\hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f = (\hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f)^{\wedge}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) by (11.20). In the state order \((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}, \tilde{\mathbf{b}}^g, \tilde{\mathbf{v}}, \tilde{\mathbf{b}}^a, \tilde{\mathbf{p}}, \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_f)\):

\[ \mathbf{H}_f = \begin{bmatrix} (\hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f)^{\wedge} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{0} & -\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T & \hat{\mathbf{R}}\T \end{bmatrix} \quad (3\times 18). \]

Worked example. A point sits at \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f = (1, 2, 2)\T\) m, so \(\norm{\mathbf{p}} = 3\) m. Then

\[ \mathbf{H}_r = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac13 & \tfrac23 & \tfrac23 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{H}_b = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -\tfrac12 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0 & -0.25 \\ 0 & 0.5 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix} . \]

Check: \(\mathbf{H}_b\,\mathbf{p} = (0.5 - 0.5,\ 1 - 1)\T = \mathbf{0}\), and \(\mathbf{H}_r\,\mathbf{p} = (1 + 4 + 4)/3 = 3\), the range itself. If the point moves 3 cm further along the ray, the range reading grows by 3 cm and the bearing does not move.

Try it in 2-D, where the bearing is one angle \(\beta = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x)\) with \(\mathbf{H}_b = [-y,\ x]/\norm{\mathbf{p}}^2\). Drag the point, and switch the range and the bearing off in turn.

Notice: \(\mathbf{H}_b\cdot\mathbf{p}\) is zero wherever the point is. With both measurements the point is confined to a small ellipse whose width across the ray grows with distance (range × bearing noise) while its length along the ray stays 0.15 m. Switch range off and the ellipse opens into a wedge with no limit along the ray: this is a monocular camera.

3. What \(\mathbf{H}_f\) already tells you

Two features of \(\mathbf{H}_f\) decide most of this week.

The columns for \(\tilde{\mathbf{b}}^g, \tilde{\mathbf{v}}, \tilde{\mathbf{b}}^a\) are zero. One camera frame says nothing about velocity or biases. These become visible only when errors propagate through \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\) into position and orientation at later frames. That is why observability needs the stack over time that you will build on Day 3.

The position and feature blocks are equal and opposite. Shift the robot and the landmark by the same vector \(\mathbf{t}\): \(\tilde{\mathbf{p}} = \tilde{\mathbf{p}}_f = \mathbf{t}\). Then

\[ \mathbf{H}_f\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = -\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\mathbf{t} + \hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{0} . \]

No point measurement, of any sensor type, reacts to a common shift. This is the 3-D version of the figure at the start of Day 1.

4. Line features

A 3-D line through two points \(\mathbf{p}^w_1, \mathbf{p}^w_2\) is stored in Plücker coordinates, equation (11.44): §11.3.1.2 · pp. 322–323

\[ \mathbf{l}^w = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{n}^w_\ell \\ \mathbf{v}^w_\ell \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}^w_1 \times \mathbf{p}^w_2 \\ \mathbf{p}^w_2 - \mathbf{p}^w_1 \end{bmatrix} . \]

\(\mathbf{v}_\ell\) is the direction of the line. \(\mathbf{n}_\ell\) is its moment: a vector normal to the plane that contains the line and the origin. The distance from the origin to the line is \(d_\ell = \norm{\mathbf{n}_\ell}/\norm{\mathbf{v}_\ell}\).

Worked example. \(\mathbf{p}_1 = (1, 0, 2)\), \(\mathbf{p}_2 = (1, 1, 2)\): the line \(x = 1, z = 2\), parallel to the \(y\) axis. Then \(\mathbf{v} = (0, 1, 0)\) and

\[ \mathbf{n} = \mathbf{p}_1 \times \mathbf{p}_2 = (0\cdot 2 - 2\cdot 1,\ \ 2\cdot 1 - 1\cdot 2,\ \ 1\cdot 1 - 0\cdot 1) = (-2,\ 0,\ 1), \qquad d = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{1} \approx 2.24 , \]

which is the distance \(\sqrt{1^2 + 2^2}\) from the origin to that line. Also \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0\).

Filling the gap: where the frame change (11.45) comes from

Move the origin to the camera position \(\mathbf{p}_c\). The two points become \(\mathbf{p}_i - \mathbf{p}_c\), so the direction is unchanged and the moment becomes

\[ (\mathbf{p}_1 - \mathbf{p}_c)\times(\mathbf{p}_2 - \mathbf{p}_c) = \mathbf{p}_1\times\mathbf{p}_2 - \mathbf{p}_c\times\mathbf{p}_2 + \mathbf{p}_c\times\mathbf{p}_1 = \mathbf{n} - \mathbf{p}_c \times \mathbf{v} . \]

Then rotate both vectors into the camera axes with \((\mathbf{R}^w_c)\T\). That is (11.45):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{n}^c_\ell \\ \mathbf{v}^c_\ell \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{R}^{w\,\mathsf{T}}_c & -\mathbf{R}^{w\,\mathsf{T}}_c(\mathbf{p}^w_c)^{\wedge} \\ \mathbf{0} & \mathbf{R}^{w\,\mathsf{T}}_c \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{n}^w_\ell \\ \mathbf{v}^w_\ell \end{bmatrix} . \]

With the example line and a camera at \(\mathbf{p}_c = (1, 0, 0)\), axes aligned with the world: \(\mathbf{n}^c = (-2, 0, 1) - (1,0,0)\times(0,1,0) = (-2, 0, 1) - (0, 0, 1) = (-2, 0, 0)\). Its norm, 2, is the distance from the camera to the line, as it should be.

The image of the line is \(\boldsymbol{\ell} = \mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{n}^c_\ell\), equation (11.46), where \(\mathbf{K}\) is built from the intrinsics. Only the moment enters. The reason is geometric: the camera centre and the 3-D line span a plane with normal \(\mathbf{n}^c_\ell\), and the image line is where that plane cuts the image. The direction \(\mathbf{v}^c_\ell\) says where the line lies inside that plane (how far away, at what angle), and one image cannot see that. §11.3.1.2 · p. 323

The measurement (11.47) is the signed distance, in the image, from each detected endpoint \(\mathbf{q}_i = (u_i, v_i, 1)\T\) of the segment to the projected line: \(\mathbf{q}_i\T\boldsymbol{\ell}/\sqrt{\ell_1^2 + \ell_2^2}\). For \(\boldsymbol{\ell} = (3, 4, -10)\) and \(\mathbf{q} = (2, 3, 1)\) it is \((6 + 12 - 10)/5 = 1.6\) pixels. The Jacobian is again a chain, \(\mathbf{H}_x = \frac{\partial \mathbf{z}_\ell}{\partial \boldsymbol{\ell}}\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{\ell}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\).

5. Plane features

A plane is the set of points with \(\mathbf{n}_\pi\cdot\mathbf{x} = d_\pi\): a unit normal and a distance from the origin. In the sensor frame, equation (11.48) gives §11.3.1.2 · p. 323

\[ \mathbf{n}^c_\pi = \mathbf{R}^c_w\,\mathbf{n}^w_\pi, \qquad d^c_\pi = d^w_\pi - (\mathbf{p}^w_c)\T\mathbf{n}^w_\pi . \]

The normal rotates; the distance shrinks by however far the sensor has moved along the normal. The book then stores the plane as its closest point to the origin, \(\mathbf{p}_\pi = d_\pi\mathbf{n}_\pi\), and the measurement (11.49) is that point seen from the sensor, \(\mathbf{z}_\pi = d^c_\pi\mathbf{n}^c_\pi + \boldsymbol{\eta}^\pi\). §11.3.1.2 · p. 324 Three numbers for three degrees of freedom, with no unit-length constraint to maintain.

Worked example. A ceiling at height 2 m is \(\mathbf{n}^w = (0, 0, 1)\), \(d^w = 2\). A sensor at \(\mathbf{p}_c = (5, 1, 0.5)\) sees it at \(d^c = 2 - 0.5 = 1.5\) m. Moving the sensor sideways changes nothing: a plane measurement is blind to motion parallel to the plane.

Common mistakes

  • "\(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) deletes rows." It multiplies them by 0 or 1. The vector \(\mathbf{z}_p\) always has three entries; switched-off entries are zero and carry no information.
  • "A camera frame constrains velocity." The velocity and bias columns of \(\mathbf{H}_f\) are zero. Those states are seen only through their effect on later poses.
  • "Six Plücker numbers mean six unknowns per line." A line has four degrees of freedom. Scale and the constraint \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0\) remove two.
  • "The closest-point form works for every plane." A plane through the origin has \(d_\pi = 0\), so its closest point is the origin itself and the normal is lost.

Check yourself

Q1. A point is at \(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f = (0, 3, 4)\T\) m. What is the range Jacobian \(\mathbf{H}_r\)?

\(\mathbf{H}_r = \mathbf{p}\T/\norm{\mathbf{p}}\) with \(\norm{\mathbf{p}} = 5\), so \([0,\ 0.6,\ 0.8]\): the unit vector along the ray.

Q2. Which error-state blocks does the Jacobian of a single point measurement touch?

\(\mathbf{H}_f\) has non-zero blocks for \(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\), \(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\), \(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_f\) only. The columns for velocity and both biases are zero.

Q3. A line passes through \(\mathbf{p}_1 = (1, 0, 0)\) and \(\mathbf{p}_2 = (1, 1, 0)\). What are its moment \(\mathbf{n}\) and its distance from the origin?

\(\mathbf{p}_1\times\mathbf{p}_2 = (0\cdot0 - 0\cdot1,\ 0\cdot1 - 1\cdot0,\ 1\cdot1 - 0\cdot1) = (0,0,1)\). \(\mathbf{v} = (0,1,0)\), so \(d = 1/1 = 1\): the line \(x = 1\) in the \(xy\) plane.

Q4. Why can a monocular camera not determine the depth of a point from one image?

A bearing depends only on the direction of \(\mathbf{p}^c_f\). Its Jacobian annihilates the ray direction, so the information matrix of one bearing measurement has a zero eigenvalue along the ray.

Q5. A wall is the plane \(\mathbf{n}^w = (1, 0, 0)\), \(d^w = 6\). The sensor is at \(\mathbf{p}_c = (2, 3, 1)\). What is \(d^c_\pi\)?

\(d^c = d^w - \mathbf{p}_c\cdot\mathbf{n}^w = 6 - 2 = 4\) m. The \(y\) and \(z\) coordinates of the sensor do not matter, because they are motion parallel to the wall.

Recap

  1. Point measurements are range and/or bearing of \(\mathbf{p}^c_f\); the selection matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) picks which.
  2. \(\mathbf{H}_x = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}[\mathbf{H}_r; \mathbf{H}_b]\mathbf{H}_f\). Range sees motion along the ray, bearing sees motion across it, and \(\mathbf{H}_b\mathbf{p}^c_f = \mathbf{0}\).
  3. \(\mathbf{H}_f = [(\hat{\mathbf{p}}^c_f)^{\wedge},\ \mathbf{0},\ \mathbf{0},\ \mathbf{0},\ -\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T,\ \hat{\mathbf{R}}\T]\): blind to velocity and biases, and blind to a common shift of robot and feature.
  4. Lines use Plücker coordinates (moment and direction); only the moment reaches the image.
  5. Planes use the closest point \(d_\pi\mathbf{n}_\pi\), transformed by (11.48).
