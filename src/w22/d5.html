Meshes that bend, hierarchies, and 3D scene graphs

Today's big idea

A loop closure moves the trajectory, and the dense map has to follow. A mesh can: tie a few of its vertices to the pose graph, optimize both as one pose graph, and drag the rest along. The same graph then grows upward into objects, places, and rooms: a 3D scene graph, where each symbol is stored once and a query touches only the branch it needs.

1. A mesh tied to the pose graph

Voxel maps fuse information well but are expensive to store and hard to edit: after a loop closure, past observations have to be removed and integrated again at their corrected poses. A mesh, usually extracted from a voxel map kept in a window around the robot, stores only surfaces and can be deformed in place. The method is pose-graph and mesh optimization (PGMO). §16.3.3 · p. 479

PGMO builds a deformation graph whose nodes each carry a rigid transformation: §16.3.3 · pp. 479–480

  • Pose vertices \(\mathbf{X}_i = [\mathbf{R}^X_i, \mathbf{t}^X_i]\), the nodes of the robot's pose graph, joined by its odometry and loop-closure edges.
  • Mesh vertices \(\mathbf{M}_k = [\mathbf{R}^M_k, \mathbf{t}^M_k]\), the vertices of a downsampled mesh, joined by its edges. Before any deformation \(\mathbf{R}^M_k = \mathbf{I}_3\) and \(\mathbf{t}^M_k = \mathbf{g}_k\), the vertex's original position.
  • An edge between pose \(i\) and mesh vertex \(k\) whenever the vertex was visible from that pose.

2. The cost, and why it is a pose graph

When a loop closure arrives, all transformations are re-estimated by minimizing §16.3.3 · p. 481

\[ \sum_{\mathbf{Z}_{ij}} \norm{\mathbf{X}_i\mathbf{Z}_{ij} - \mathbf{X}_j}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{ij}} + \sum_{k}\sum_{l \in \mathcal{N}^M(k)} \norm{\mathbf{R}^M_k(\mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_k) + \mathbf{t}^M_k - \mathbf{t}^M_l}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{kl}} + \sum_{i}\sum_{l \in \mathcal{N}^M(i)} \norm{\mathbf{R}^X_i\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{il} + \mathbf{t}^X_i - \mathbf{t}^M_l}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{il}}. \tag{16.27} \]

The first sum is ordinary pose-graph optimization over the relative-pose measurements \(\mathbf{Z}_{ij}\). The second keeps the mesh locally rigid: if the patch around node \(k\) moved as a rigid body, neighbour \(l\) would be at \(\mathbf{R}^M_k(\mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_k) + \mathbf{t}^M_k\), and the term penalizes its distance from where \(l\) actually is. The third does the same between a pose and the mesh nodes it saw, with \(\tilde{\mathbf{g}}_{il} = \tilde{\mathbf{R}}_i\T(\mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_i)\) the original position of node \(l\) in the frame of pose \(i\). Before deformation every rigidity term is zero.

Filling the gap: from (16.27) to the pose-graph form (16.30)

The book defines \(\mathbf{G}_{kl} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_3 & \mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_k\\ \mathbf{0} & 1\end{bmatrix}\) and claims the second sum equals \(\sum \norm{\mathbf{M}_k\mathbf{G}_{kl} - \mathbf{M}_l}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\). Multiply out, writing \(\mathbf{M}_k\) as a \(4\times4\) matrix:

\[ \mathbf{M}_k\mathbf{G}_{kl} - \mathbf{M}_l = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_k & \mathbf{R}_k(\mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_k) + \mathbf{t}_k\\ \mathbf{0} & 1\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_l & \mathbf{t}_l\\ \mathbf{0} & 1\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_k - \mathbf{R}_l & \mathbf{R}_k(\mathbf{g}_l - \mathbf{g}_k) + \mathbf{t}_k - \mathbf{t}_l\\ \mathbf{0} & 0\end{bmatrix}. \]

The last column is exactly the rigidity residual. The weighted Frobenius norm of (16.28), \(\norm{\mathbf{A}}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} = \tr(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{A}\T)\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \diag(\omega_R\mathbf{I}_3, \omega_t)\), weights the squared entries of the first three columns by \(\omega_R\) and those of the last column by \(\omega_t\). Setting \(\omega_R = 0\) on mesh edges leaves \(\omega_t\) times the squared rigidity residual, and the third sum works the same way. So every term has the form \(\norm{\mathbf{T}_i\mathbf{E}_{ij} - \mathbf{T}_j}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{ij}}\), equation (16.31): one pose graph over \(n + m\) nodes, for the solvers of Week 13 · Day 5.

3. Moving every other vertex

The optimization moved only the control nodes. Each vertex \(\mathbf{v}_i\) of the full mesh is then carried by its \(m\) nearest control nodes: §16.3.3 · p. 482

\[ \tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{m} w_j(\mathbf{v}_i)\left[\mathbf{R}^M_j(\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}_j) + \mathbf{t}^M_j\right], \qquad w_j = \frac{\bar{w}_j}{\sum_k \bar{w}_k}, \quad \bar{w}_j(\mathbf{v}_i) = \left(1 - \frac{\norm{\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{g}_j}}{d_{\max}}\right)^2 . \tag{16.32, 16.33} \]

Each bracket is "where \(\mathbf{v}_i\) would go if it were glued to node \(j\)", and \(d_{\max}\) is the distance to the furthest of the \(m\) nodes. Take \(m = 3\) nodes at distances 1, 2, and 4 m. Then \(\bar{w} = (0.75^2, 0.5^2, 0) = (0.5625, 0.25, 0)\) and \(w = (0.69, 0.31, 0)\). If the nodes only translated, by \((0.4, 0)\), \((0.1, 0)\), and \((-2, 0)\) m, the vertex moves by \(0.69 \times 0.4 + 0.31 \times 0.1 = 0.31\) m in \(x\). The furthest node has weight zero.

4. Symbols, and what hierarchy saves

Symbol grounding is tying a symbol that means something to a person, such as "chair" or "kitchen", to a location in the map. Raw images are too bulky to ground symbols in, so they are attached to a compact sub-symbolic representation such as voxels or a mesh. §16.4.1 · p. 483

The naive way stores in every voxel which of the \(L\) symbols apply to it (a voxel can belong to a chair and to a kitchen). For volume \(V\) and resolution \(\delta\): §16.4.1 · p. 484

\[ m = O\!\left(L \cdot V/\delta^3\right). \tag{16.34} \]

Organizing the symbols by containment gives instead

\[ m = O\!\left(V/\delta^3 + N_{\text{objects}} + N_{\text{rooms}} + \dots + N_{\text{buildings}}\right). \tag{16.35} \]

Each symbol is now stored once, as a node of a graph whose edges mean "contains". Nothing is lost; the per-voxel labels can be regenerated. For a flat of 150 m³ at \(\delta = 0.1\) m with 15 symbols, (16.34) is \(15 \times 150{,}000 = 2{,}250{,}000\) entries and (16.35) is \(150{,}000 + 14 + 4 + 1\). Replacing the voxel grid by an octree or by a graph of places shrinks the first term to \(N_{\text{sub-sym}}\) (16.36), now with some loss. §16.4.1 · p. 484

5. Hierarchical graphs and 3D scene graphs

A graph is hierarchical if its nodes split into layers \(1, \dots, l\) such that (1) a node has at most one parent in the layer above, (2) edges between layers join consecutive layers only, and (3) children of different parents are never connected. Then the descendants of two nodes of the same layer never mix, and the treewidth of the whole graph is bounded by Definition 16.2, §16.4.1 · p. 485

\[ \max_{v \in \mathcal{V}}\; tw\big[\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{C}(v)]\big] + 1, \tag{16.37} \]

where \(\mathcal{G}[\mathcal{C}(v)]\) is the subgraph formed by the children of \(v\).

Filling the gap: what treewidth buys

Treewidth measures how far a graph is from a tree: a tree has treewidth 1, a fully connected graph on \(n\) nodes has \(n - 1\). With the best elimination order (Week 3 · Day 3), the largest clique created has treewidth \(+\,1\) variables, and exact inference over discrete variables costs exponentially in that number but only linearly in the number of nodes. So (16.37) says the cost is set by the most tangled group of siblings, such as the objects of one cluttered room, and not by the size of the building.

A 3D scene graph is a graph \(\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})\) whose nodes carry features that always include a position, and whose edges carry features too. Its layers are typically objects, places (a sparse model of free space), rooms, and buildings. Edges between layers mean containment. Edges inside a layer mean relations such as "on top of" or "nearby", some of them directed. Relations such as "left of", common in 2D scene graphs, depend on the viewpoint and mean little in 3D. §16.4.2 · pp. 486–487

Going deeper (optional): loop closures and where §16.5 points

To keep a scene graph consistent, one approach adds the places layer to the deformation graph of (16.27) and re-interpolates the other layers; another puts factors between all layers, which is no longer a plain pose graph. §16.4.2 · p. 487 The trends: maps that store language-aligned features in place of a fixed label set (next week), scene graphs for outdoor scenes with task-driven layers, uncertainty over mixed discrete and continuous variables, and multi-robot semantic mapping. §16.5 · pp. 487–489

Check yourself

Q1. In the pose-graph form (16.30), why is the rotation weight ω_R set to zero on mesh edges?

The rigidity terms are 3-vectors equal to the last column of the matrix difference, so the other three columns must get weight zero.

Q2. A mesh vertex has m = 3 nearest control nodes at distances 1, 3, and 4 m. What is the normalized weight of the nearest node in (16.33)?

d_max = 4. Unnormalized weights: (1 − 1/4)² = 0.5625, (1 − 3/4)² = 0.0625, and 0. Normalized: 0.5625/0.625 = 0.90.

Q3. A floor of 200 m³ is stored at δ = 0.1 m with L = 50 symbols, 300 objects, and 12 rooms in 1 building. Roughly how much smaller is the hierarchical count (16.35) than the flat count (16.34)?

V/δ³ = 200,000 voxels. Flat: 50 × 200,000 = 10,000,000. Hierarchical: 200,000 + 313. The ratio is about 50, the number of symbols.

Q4. Which of these breaks Definition 16.2?

Two parents violate the single-parent property. Edges between siblings are allowed; they only raise the treewidth of that group.

Recap

  1. PGMO joins the pose graph and a downsampled mesh in a deformation graph and optimizes (16.27): pose-graph terms plus local-rigidity terms. With \(\omega_R = 0\) on mesh edges it is a standard pose graph.
  2. The full mesh follows by blending the motions of each vertex's nearest control nodes (16.32).
  3. Storing each symbol once in a hierarchy cuts memory from \(L \cdot V/\delta^3\) to \(V/\delta^3 + N\), without loss.
  4. A 3D scene graph is a layered graph of positioned nodes. Single parents, locality, and disjoint children bound its treewidth by that of one group of siblings, plus one.
