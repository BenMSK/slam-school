Ellipsoids, meshes, and learned shapes

Today's big idea

Every shape model is paired with a detector output: an ellipsoid with a bounding box, a mesh with a segmentation mask, a signed distance function with depth points. The residual always has the same form: predict what the detector would report for the current object and camera, and compare.

1. An ellipsoid as a quadric

The coarsest useful shape is an ellipsoid with semi-axis lengths \(\mathbf{s} \in \R^3\), centred at the origin of the object frame:

\[ \mathcal{E}_{\mathbf{s}} = \{\mathbf{x} \in \R^3 \mid \mathbf{x}\T \diag(\mathbf{s})^{-2}\, \mathbf{x} \le 1\}. \tag{16.4} \]

Appending a 1 to \(\mathbf{x}\) (homogeneous coordinates) moves the "\(\le 1\)" into a matrix:

\[ \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ 1\end{bmatrix}\T \mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{s}} \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}\\ 1\end{bmatrix} \le 0, \qquad \mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{s}} = \diag\!\left(s_1^{-2},\, s_2^{-2},\, s_3^{-2},\, -1\right). \tag{16.5} \]

A surface that is the zero set of such a quadratic form, with a symmetric \(4\times 4\) matrix, is a quadric. Its attraction is that its outline in an image has a closed form. §16.2.1.2 · pp. 458–459

2. The dual quadric: describe the surface by its tangent planes

The book switches to the dual description: the set of planes that touch the ellipsoid.

\[ \mathcal{E}^*_{\mathbf{s}} = \{\mathbf{y} \in \R^3 \mid \mathbf{y}\T \diag(\mathbf{s})^{2}\, \mathbf{y} \le 1\} = \big\{\mathbf{y} \mid [\mathbf{y}; 1]\T\, \mathbf{Q}^*_{\mathbf{s}}\, [\mathbf{y}; 1] \le 0\big\}, \tag{16.6} \]

with \(\mathbf{Q}^*_{\mathbf{s}}\) the adjugate of \(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{s}}\). A quadric does not change when its matrix is multiplied by a constant, so the adjugate \(\det(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{s}})\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{s}}^{-1}\) can be replaced by \(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{s}}^{-1} = \diag(s_1^2, s_2^2, s_3^2, -1)\). An image outline is made of rays that touch the object, so the tangent description is the one a camera sees. §16.2.1.2 · pp. 459–460

Filling the gap: why \(\mathbf{Q}^* = \mathbf{Q}^{-1}\), and why duals are easy to project

Tangent planes. Write a plane as \(\{\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{y}\T\mathbf{x} + 1 = 0\}\), which is the homogeneous vector \([\mathbf{y}; 1]\). Every point of the ellipsoid is \(\mathbf{x} = \diag(\mathbf{s})\,\mathbf{u}\) with \(\norm{\mathbf{u}} \le 1\). Then \(\mathbf{y}\T\mathbf{x} = (\diag(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{y})\T\mathbf{u}\), and as \(\mathbf{u}\) runs over the unit ball this takes every value between \(-\norm{\diag(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{y}}\) and \(+\norm{\diag(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{y}}\). The plane needs the value \(-1\). It misses the ellipsoid if \(\norm{\diag(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{y}} \lt 1\), cuts through it if \(\gt 1\), and touches it at one point exactly when

\[ \norm{\diag(\mathbf{s})\mathbf{y}}^2 = \mathbf{y}\T\diag(\mathbf{s})^2\mathbf{y} = 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}\\ 1\end{bmatrix}\T \begin{bmatrix}\diag(\mathbf{s})^{2} & \mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0}\T & -1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}\\ 1\end{bmatrix} = 0 . \]

The matrix in the middle is \(\mathbf{Q}_{\mathbf{s}}^{-1}\), and the tangent planes are the boundary of the set in (16.6).

Moving the object. If points move by a \(4\times4\) matrix \(\mathbf{H}\) (\(\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}\)), a plane \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) moves to \(\boldsymbol{\pi}' = \mathbf{H}^{-\top}\boldsymbol{\pi}\), since then \(\boldsymbol{\pi}'^{\top}\mathbf{x}' = \boldsymbol{\pi}\T\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{x} = 0\) still holds. Substituting \(\boldsymbol{\pi} = \mathbf{H}\T\boldsymbol{\pi}'\) into \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\T\mathbf{Q}^*\boldsymbol{\pi} = 0\) gives \(\boldsymbol{\pi}'^{\top}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q}^*\mathbf{H}\T)\boldsymbol{\pi}' = 0\): the dual moves as \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q}^*\mathbf{H}\T\), with no inverse.

Projecting. An image line \(\mathbf{l}\) and the camera centre span a plane, \(\boldsymbol{\pi} = \mathbf{M}\T\mathbf{l}\), where \(\mathbf{M}\) is the \(3\times4\) camera matrix. The line touches the object's outline exactly when that plane touches the object: \(\mathbf{l}\T(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Q}^*\mathbf{M}\T)\mathbf{l} = 0\). So the outline is the conic whose dual is \(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Q}^*\mathbf{M}\T\). The point form \(\mathbf{Q}\) would need the inverse of \(\mathbf{M}\), which a \(3\times4\) matrix does not have.

3. From the object to a bounding-box residual

Apply the two rules. Scaling by \(\lambda\) and placing the object with pose \(\mathbf{q}\) gives the dual quadric in the world frame (the book writes \(\mathbf{H}\) out as two matrices), and the camera at pose \(\mathbf{T}\) turns it into a dual conic, a symmetric \(3\times3\) matrix: §16.2.1.2 · p. 460

\[ \mathbf{Q}^*_{\boldsymbol{\ell}} \propto \mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{Q}^*_{\mathbf{s}}\,\mathbf{H}\T, \qquad \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{q}\,\diag(\lambda, \lambda, \lambda, 1), \tag{16.7} \] \[ \mathbf{C}^*_{\mathbf{x}} \propto \frac{1}{\beta}\,\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^*_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\mathbf{T}^{-\top}\mathbf{P}\T, \qquad \mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_3 & \mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}. \tag{16.8} \]

The detector reports a box \(\mathbf{z} = [u\ v\ w\ h]\T\) in normalized image coordinates: a corner, a width, and a height. The book turns it into the dual conic of the axis-aligned ellipse that fits inside the box, with centre \((c_u, c_v) = (u + w/2,\ v + h/2)\). Multiplying out the three matrices of (16.9) gives

\[ \mathbf{C}^*_{\mathbf{z}} \propto \begin{bmatrix} (w/2)^2 - c_u^2 & -c_u c_v & -c_u\\ -c_u c_v & (h/2)^2 - c_v^2 & -c_v\\ -c_u & -c_v & -1 \end{bmatrix}. \tag{16.9} \]

This also tells you how to read a box off any dual conic: scale it so the bottom-right entry is \(-1\); the centre is minus the last column, and the half-width is \(\sqrt{C^*_{11} + c_u^2}\). The residual compares the two conics, §16.2.1.2 · p. 461

\[ r_i(\mathbf{x}_i) = \norm{\mathbf{C}^*_{\mathbf{z}_j} - \mathbf{C}^*_{\mathbf{x}_i}}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i} = \norm{\mathbf{b}_j - \tfrac{1}{\beta_i}\mathbf{A}\,\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{T}_i, \boldsymbol{\ell}_i)}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i}. \tag{16.10, 16.11} \]

The second form flattens each symmetric matrix into the vector of its distinct entries (\(\operatorname{vech}\)): 6 in \(\mathbf{b}_j\) for the box conic, 10 in \(\mathbf{v}\) for \(\mathbf{T}_i^{-1}\mathbf{Q}^*_{\boldsymbol{\ell}_i}\mathbf{T}_i^{-\top}\). The constant \(6\times10\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) applies \(\mathbf{P}\) on both sides.

A sphere, by hand. Radius 1, centre \(\mathbf{t} = (0, 0, 5)\), camera at the origin with \(\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{I}\). Then \(\mathbf{Q}^*_{\mathbf{s}} = \diag(1,1,1,-1)\), and moving it by \(\mathbf{t}\) gives a matrix whose upper-left block is \(\mathbf{I}_3 - \mathbf{t}\mathbf{t}\T\). \(\mathbf{P}\) keeps exactly that block:

\[ \mathbf{P}\mathbf{Q}^*_{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\mathbf{P}\T = \mathbf{I}_3 - \mathbf{t}\mathbf{t}\T = \diag(1, 1, -24) \;\propto\; \diag\!\left(\tfrac{1}{24}, \tfrac{1}{24}, -1\right). \]

Here \(\beta = 24\). The centre is \((0, 0)\) and the half-width is \(1/\sqrt{24} = 0.204\). Check: a ray that grazes the sphere makes an angle \(\alpha\) with the axis where \(\sin\alpha = 1/5\), so \(\tan\alpha = 1/\sqrt{24}\). "Radius over depth", 0.2, is slightly too small, because grazing rays touch the sphere in front of its centre.

Try it: turn the yaw to 90°, then back to 30°. Then set the scale to 1.5 and the distance to 9 m.

Notice that the box depends on orientation: its width drops from 0.76 to 0.39 when the long axis points at the camera. And at \(\lambda = 1.5\), \(d = 9\) m the residual is exactly zero again: one box cannot separate a large far object from a small near one.

4. A mesh, compared with a segmentation mask

A triangle mesh with vertices \(\mathcal{V} = \{\mathbf{s}_j\}_j\) and faces \(\mathcal{F}\) can follow the real surface. It is compared with a segmentation mask \(\mathbf{z}_i \subset \R^2\), the set of pixels the detector assigns to the object. A rasterization function \(\rho(\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{F})\) fills in the pixels the mesh covers once its vertices are projected as in (16.3). Call that rendered set \(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_i = \rho(\{\pi(\mathbf{q}_i^{-1}\mathbf{T}_i, \lambda_i\mathbf{s}_{i,j})\}_j, \mathcal{F})\). The residual is the reciprocal of the intersection-over-union: §16.2.1.3 · pp. 461–462

\[ r_i(\mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{z}}_i \cup \mathbf{z}_i}{\hat{\mathbf{z}}_i \cap \mathbf{z}_i}. \tag{16.12} \]

If the rendering covers 500 pixels, the mask 400, and they share 300, the union is \(500 + 400 - 300 = 600\) and \(r = 600/300 = 2\). A perfect match gives the minimum, \(r = 1\).

The catch is the gradient. A pixel is either covered or not, so \(\rho\) is piecewise constant in the vertex positions and its derivative is zero almost everywhere. Differentiable rendering replaces it with an approximate gradient, as in Week 20 · Day 1. The vertices can be optimized directly or through the coefficients of (16.2). §16.2.1.3 · pp. 461–462

5. Implicit shapes: a function instead of a surface

The models so far are explicit: they list the surface. An implicit model is a function of space whose level set is the surface: an occupancy function, \(-1\) inside the object \(\mathcal{S}\) and \(+1\) outside (16.13), or a signed distance function, §16.2.1.4 · pp. 464–465

\[ d_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{cases} -\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\partial\mathcal{S}} \norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}}, & \mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S},\\ \phantom{-}\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\partial\mathcal{S}} \norm{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}}, & \mathbf{x}\notin\mathcal{S}. \end{cases} \tag{16.14} \]

For a unit sphere, \(d_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbf{x}) = \norm{\mathbf{x}} - 1\): \(-0.5\) at \((0.5, 0, 0)\) and \(2\) at \((3, 0, 0)\). You met SDFs as maps in Week 8 · Day 2. Here one SDF describes one object, and a network \(d_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{s})\) produces it: a decoder with weights \(\theta\) shared by a whole category, and a shape code \(\mathbf{s} \in \R^d\) that selects one shape within it.

A depth sensor at pose \(\mathbf{T}_i\) gives points \(\mathbf{y}_{i,j}\) near the object with measured distances \(z_{i,j}\) to its surface. The residual asks the decoder for the distance at each point: §16.2.1.4 · p. 465

\[ r_i(\mathbf{x}_i)^2 = \sum_j \left| z_{i,j} - d_\theta\!\left(\lambda_i^{-1}\mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{T}_i^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{i,j},\ \mathbf{s}_i\right)\right|^2_{\sigma_i}. \tag{16.15} \]

The argument of \(d_\theta\) takes the point from the robot frame to the world, then to the object frame, then divides by \(\lambda_i\), so the decoder is always queried in its canonical frame. Trust that description in words: with yesterday's conventions for \(\mathbf{T}\) and \(\mathbf{q}\) the same chain reads \(\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{q}^{-1}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{y}\).

Training, offline and once per category, holds the poses and scales fixed and optimizes \(\theta\) with one code per training object. Testing, online inside SLAM, freezes \(\theta\) and optimizes \(\mathbf{T}_i\), \(\mathbf{q}_i\), \(\lambda_i\), \(\mathbf{s}_i\) for an object never seen before. §16.2.1.4 · p. 466

Check yourself

Q1. A sphere of radius 2 m is centred on the optical axis at depth 4 m. What is the half-width of its outline in normalized image coordinates?

With radius ρ the block is ρ²I − t tᵀ = diag(4, 4, −12) ∝ diag(1/3, 1/3, −1). The half-width is √(1/3) = 0.58.

Q2. A detection is z = (u, v, w, h) = (0.1, −0.2, 0.4, 0.2). With the bottom-right entry scaled to −1, what is the top-left entry of its dual conic (16.9)?

The centre is c_u = 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3. The entry is (w/2)² − c_u² = 0.04 − 0.09 = −0.05.

Q3. A rendered mesh covers 300 pixels, the segmentation mask covers 300 pixels, and 200 pixels are in both. What is the residual (16.12)?

Union = 300 + 300 − 200 = 400, intersection = 200, so the residual is 2 (the IoU is 0.5).

Q4. A robot meets a chair it has never seen, with a chair SDF decoder already trained. What does minimizing (16.15) adjust?

Online, θ is frozen. The state is x = (T, q, λ, s).

Recap

  1. An ellipsoid is a quadric. Its dual \(\mathbf{Q}^* \propto \mathbf{Q}^{-1}\) describes it by tangent planes, moves as \(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Q}^*\mathbf{H}\T\), and projects to the dual conic (16.8).
  2. A bounding box becomes the dual conic (16.9), and the residual (16.10) is the difference of the two conics. One box leaves size and distance tied together.
  3. A mesh is fitted to a segmentation mask through the reciprocal IoU (16.12), with a differentiable rasterizer.
  4. An implicit shape is a decoder \(d_\theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{s})\): weights trained offline; code, pose, and scale estimated online with (16.15).
