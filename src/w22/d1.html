Maps that know what things are: objects as landmarks

After today you can say what a metric-semantic map adds to a geometric one, define an object landmark, and write the residual that compares projected semantic landmarks with detected keypoints.

Today's big idea

A metric-semantic map stores what things are together with where they are. In its sparse form, the landmarks stop being anonymous 3D points and become objects with a category, a pose, a scale, and a shape. The back-end stays the same least-squares machine; only the residual changes.

1. What geometry alone cannot do

A geometric map answers "is this space free?". That is enough for collision checking, but not for "go to the laptop in the office", and not for a planner that should prefer road over sidewalk over vegetation. Those need labels on the map. Ch. 16 intro · p. 454

Semantics also help SLAM itself. An object is recognizable from many viewpoints and is more distinctive than a corner, which makes data association and loop closure less ambiguous. Labels also flag things that may move (Week 21 · Day 2). Ch. 16 intro · p. 454

The labels come from learned vision models in the front-end: Ch. 16 intro · pp. 454–455

Front-end outputWhat it gives
Object detectionA class and a bounding box per object
Semantic keypoints or edgesImage locations of named parts (a wheel, a door)
Semantic segmentationA class for every pixel
Instance segmentationA mask per object, separating two objects of one class
Panoptic segmentationBoth: background classes and separate instances

This chapter assumes closed-set semantics: the labels come from a fixed dictionary of typically 100 to 1000 words such as "chair", "table", "office". Open-set semantics (language embeddings) is Chapter 17, next week. Ch. 16 intro · p. 455

Section 16.1 names three places to put the labels: on sparse landmarks, which become objects (§16.2, Days 1–3); on every point, surfel, voxel, or mesh face of a dense map (§16.3, Day 4); and on regions of free space such as rooms (§16.4, Day 5). Each needs a model of what a detector should report for a given map, and a way to fuse many noisy reports. §16.1 · pp. 455–456

2. The object landmark

Landmark-based SLAM minimizes a sum of squared residuals, one per measurement. The book writes the residual of factor \(i\) as

\[ r_i(\mathbf{x}_i) = \norm{\mathbf{z}_i - h_i(\mathbf{T}_i, \boldsymbol{\ell}_i)}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i}, \qquad \mathbf{x}_i = (\mathbf{T}_i, \boldsymbol{\ell}_i), \tag{16.1} \]

with measurement \(\mathbf{z}_i\), robot pose \(\mathbf{T}_i\), landmark \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_i\), measurement model \(h_i\), and the Mahalanobis norm \(\norm{\mathbf{e}}_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^2 = \mathbf{e}\T\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{e}\) of Week 2 · Day 2. In visual SLAM \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_i\) is a 3D point and \(\mathbf{z}_i\) a pixel. Sparse metric-semantic SLAM keeps (16.1) and changes what \(\boldsymbol{\ell}\), \(\mathbf{z}\), and \(h\) are. §16.2 · p. 456

An object landmark is a tuple \(\boldsymbol{\ell} = (\sigma, \mathbf{q}, \lambda, \mathbf{s})\). Definition 16.1 · p. 456

PartLives inMeaning
Category \(\sigma\)\(\mathbb{N}\)"car", "chair", "table": the only discrete part
Pose \(\mathbf{q}\)\(\SE(3)\)The object's own frame, expressed in the world frame
Scale \(\lambda\)\(\R_{\gt 0}\)Size relative to the category's canonical model
Shape \(\mathbf{s}\)\(\R^d\)The surface in the object frame (today keypoints; tomorrow ellipsoid, mesh, latent code)

A point landmark has 3 unknowns. An object landmark has \(6 + 1 + d\) and a discrete label. The scale is kept apart so that a car and a toy car can share one shape and differ only in \(\lambda\). §16.2.1 · p. 457

3. Shape as a set of semantic landmarks

The first shape model is a short list of 3D points \(\{\mathbf{s}_j\}_j\), called semantic landmarks, placed on named parts of the object: for a car, the wheels, mirrors, and plate. Their projections into an image are semantic keypoints \(\mathbf{z}_j \in \R^2\). A network detects them by producing, for each part, a heatmap with a bump at the part's pixel. §16.2.1.1 · p. 457

If every \(\mathbf{s}_j\) were free, the optimizer could move the left wheel to the roof. The deformable shape model prevents that by building the points from a category template:

\[ \mathbf{s}_j = \mathbf{b}_{j,0} + \sum_k c_k\, \mathbf{b}_{j,k}. \tag{16.2} \]

Here \(\mathbf{b}_{j,0}\) is the position of part \(j\) on the average car, each \(\mathbf{b}_{j,k}\) is one way cars differ from the average (a mode of variability), and the deformation coefficients \(c_k \in \R_{\ge 0}\) say how much of each mode this car has. The \(\mathbf{b}\)'s are fixed per category. The \(c_k\) are estimated per object. §16.2.1.1 · p. 457

Try it: the pink rings are detected keypoints of one car. Press "Fit shape only", then press "Gauss-Newton step" four times.

Notice that shape alone cannot explain the detections: with the wrong scale and yaw, \(c_1\) runs into its upper limit and the error stays above 130 cm. Three Gauss-Newton steps on all four unknowns bring it to about 6 cm, the noise level, at \(c_1 \approx 0.80\), \(c_2 \approx 0.53\), \(\lambda \approx 1.29\), yaw 25°; the fourth changes nothing.

Filling the gap: where the modes \(\mathbf{b}_{j,k}\) come from

The book says the modes are obtained by PCA. The recipe: take \(N\) CAD models of the category, each annotated with the same \(J\) parts. Stack the \(J\) part positions of model \(n\) into one vector \(\mathbf{m}_n \in \R^{3J}\). Then

\[ \bar{\mathbf{m}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \mathbf{m}_n, \qquad \mathbf{C} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} (\mathbf{m}_n - \bar{\mathbf{m}})(\mathbf{m}_n - \bar{\mathbf{m}})\T . \]

The mean \(\bar{\mathbf{m}}\), cut back into \(J\) triples, gives the \(\mathbf{b}_{j,0}\). The eigenvectors of \(\mathbf{C}\) with the largest eigenvalues are the directions in which the training cars differ most; cutting eigenvector \(k\) into triples gives the \(\mathbf{b}_{j,k}\).

Count the unknowns. With \(J = 12\) parts, free points cost \(36\) numbers per car. With \(K = 5\) modes the shape costs \(5\), and the estimate stays car-shaped even when half the keypoints are hidden.

4. The keypoint residual

With the shape fixed in the object frame, the residual between object \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_i\) and the keypoints \(\{\mathbf{z}_{i,j}\}_j\) detected from camera pose \(\mathbf{T}_i\) is a sum of reprojection errors, one per part:

\[ r_i(\mathbf{x}_i)^2 = \sum_j \norm{\mathbf{z}_{i,j} - \pi\!\left(\mathbf{q}_i^{-1}\mathbf{T}_i,\; \lambda_i\, \mathbf{s}_{i,j}\right)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i}. \tag{16.3} \]

\(\pi(\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{s})\) is the perspective projection of point \(\mathbf{s}\) into a camera with pose \(\mathbf{T}\). §16.2.1.1 · p. 458

Filling the gap: reading \(\pi(\mathbf{q}^{-1}\mathbf{T}, \lambda\mathbf{s})\), with numbers

Both \(\mathbf{T}\) (camera) and \(\mathbf{q}\) (object) map their own frame to the world frame. So \(\mathbf{q}^{-1}\mathbf{T}\) maps camera coordinates to object coordinates: it is the pose of the camera as seen from the object. To project a point given in object coordinates you need the opposite direction, \((\mathbf{q}^{-1}\mathbf{T})^{-1} = \mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{q}\), followed by the pinhole model of Week 11 · Day 3.

Take a camera at the world origin with \(\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{I}\), focal length 500 px, principal point \((320, 240)\). The object frame is not rotated and sits at \((1, 0, 8)\) m. Its scale is \(\lambda = 2\), and one semantic landmark is \(\mathbf{s} = (0.5, 0.25, 0)\).

\[ \lambda\mathbf{s} = (1, 0.5, 0) \;\xrightarrow{\ \mathbf{q}\ }\; (2, 0.5, 8) \;\xrightarrow{\ \mathbf{T}^{-1} = \mathbf{I}\ }\; (2, 0.5, 8) \;\xrightarrow{\ \div\, z\ }\; (0.25,\ 0.0625) \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{intrinsics}\ }\; (445,\ 271.25)\ \text{px}. \]

If the detector reports \(\mathbf{z} = (448, 270)\), the error is \(\mathbf{e} = (3, -1.25)\) px. With \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = 2^2\mathbf{I}\) px² this part contributes \((3^2 + 1.25^2)/4 = 2.64\) to \(r_i^2\). The optimizer can lower it through \(\mathbf{T}\), \(\mathbf{q}\), \(\lambda\), or the shape coefficients.

Common mistakes

  • "\(\mathbf{q}\) takes world coordinates to object coordinates." It is the pose of the object frame in the world, so it takes object coordinates to world coordinates. That is why (16.3) contains \(\mathbf{q}^{-1}\mathbf{T}\) and not \(\mathbf{q}\mathbf{T}\).

Check yourself

Q1. An image shows two chairs on a carpet. Which output gives a separate mask for each chair and also labels the carpet pixels?

Semantic segmentation does not separate the two chairs. Panoptic segmentation separates instances and labels the background.

Q2. The camera is at the world origin with identity pose. An object frame is unrotated at (0, 0, 10) m with scale λ = 3. Where does the semantic landmark s = (1, 0, 0) land in normalized image coordinates (x/z, y/z)?

Scale first: λs = (3, 0, 0). Move to the world: (3, 0, 10). Divide by depth: (0.3, 0).

Q3. A category model has J = 10 semantic landmarks and K = 4 modes. How many continuous unknowns does one object add to the problem if its shape is optimized through the coefficients?

Pose 6, scale 1, coefficients 4: 11 in total. Ten free points would cost 6 + 1 + 30 = 37.

Q4. In equation (16.3), what is the transformation q⁻¹T?

T maps camera coordinates to the world and q⁻¹ maps the world to the object, so q⁻¹T is the camera's pose seen from the object.

Recap

  1. A metric-semantic map holds labels as well as geometry, on sparse objects, on dense map elements, or on regions. Labels serve tasks and also help data association and loop closure.
  2. An object landmark is \((\sigma, \mathbf{q}, \lambda, \mathbf{s})\): category, pose, scale, shape.
  3. The deformable model (16.2) is a category mean plus a few learned modes, so a shape costs a handful of coefficients.
  4. The residual (16.3) scales each semantic landmark, moves it object → world → camera, projects it, and compares it with the detected keypoint.
