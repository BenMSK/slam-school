Discrete and continuous unknowns together: hybrid solvers

After today you can write metric-semantic SLAM as a discrete-continuous factor graph, explain why alternating between associations and geometry is tractable, and compute hard, max-mixture, and EM association weights by hand.

Today's big idea

Semantic SLAM has unknowns you can differentiate (poses, shapes) and unknowns you can only enumerate (classes, associations). Fix either kind and the other becomes easy: least squares for the continuous ones, a small choice per measurement for the discrete ones. Hybrid solvers alternate, with hard choices or soft weights.

1. Where the discrete unknowns come from

Discrete variables enter in four ways: the class \(\sigma\) of each object, which is tied to its shape; symmetries (a square table looks the same every 90°); several pose hypotheses from a learned front-end; and data association, the question of which mapped object produced a detection (Week 6 · Day 1). §16.2.2 · p. 466

Put together, the estimation problem is a MAP problem over robot poses \(X\), landmarks \(L\), and associations \(D\), given all measurements \(Z\): §16.2.2 · p. 467

\[ \hat{X}, \hat{L}, \hat{D} = \argmax_{X, L, D}\; p(X, L, D \mid Z). \tag{16.16} \]

Classes and associations belong in one problem because they inform each other: a detection labelled "table" is unlikely to be a chair. The price is a search space that is combinatorial as well as nonconvex. If 6 detections can each come from any of 6 objects there are \(6^6 = 46{,}656\) assignments, and in the worst case each needs its own continuous optimization. §16.2.2 · p. 468

2. The hybrid factor graph

Group the continuous variables into \(\Theta\) and the discrete ones into \(D\). The posterior factors as before, and taking the negative logarithm turns the product into a sum: §16.2.2 · p. 468

\[ p(\Theta, D \mid Z) \propto \prod_k \phi_k(V_k), \qquad \Theta^*, D^* = \argmin_{\Theta, D} \underbrace{\sum_k -\log \phi_k(V_k)}_{\mathcal{L}(\Theta, D)}. \tag{16.17, 16.18} \]

\(V_k\) is the set of variables factor \(k\) touches. There are three kinds of factor: discrete \(\phi_k(D_k)\), continuous \(\phi_k(\Theta_k)\), and discrete-continuous \(\phi_k(\Theta_k, D_k)\). The last kind is assumed to be a squared residual once the discrete value is fixed: §16.2.2 · p. 469

\[ -\log \phi_k(\Theta_k, D_k) = \norm{r_k(\Theta_k, D_k)}_2^2 . \tag{16.19} \]

A detection factor is the typical case. Its discrete variable \(d_k \in \{1, \dots, J\}\) picks which landmark the residual is computed against: \(r_k = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\left(\mathbf{z}_k - h(\mathbf{T}, \boldsymbol{\ell}_{d_k})\right)\).

3. Alternating minimization: fix one kind, solve the other

Two observations make a local method possible. §16.2.2 · p. 469

  • With \(D\) fixed, only continuous variables remain, and the Gauss-Newton tools of Week 2 · Day 4 apply.
  • With \(\Theta\) fixed, what remains is a discrete factor graph. If the discrete variables split into small groups \(D_j\) that are conditionally independent given \(\Theta\), \[ p(D \mid \Theta, Z) \propto \prod_j p(D_j \mid \Theta, Z) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \max_D\, p(D \mid \Theta, Z) \propto \prod_j \Big[\max_{D_j} p(D_j \mid \Theta, Z)\Big], \tag{16.20, 16.21} \] each group can be optimized on its own.

DC-SAM turns this into a loop, repeated until \(\mathcal{L}\) stops decreasing: §16.2.2 · p. 470

\[ D^{(i+1)} = \argmin_D\, \mathcal{L}(\Theta^{(i)}, D), \qquad \Theta^{(i+1)} = \argmin_\Theta\, \mathcal{L}(\Theta, D^{(i+1)}). \tag{16.22} \]

Filling the gap: why the maximum of a product splits, and what it saves

Take two groups. Each factor is non-negative and depends on its own group only, so

\[ \max_{D_1, D_2}\, f_1(D_1)\, f_2(D_2) = \max_{D_1}\Big[f_1(D_1)\,\max_{D_2} f_2(D_2)\Big] = \Big[\max_{D_1} f_1(D_1)\Big]\Big[\max_{D_2} f_2(D_2)\Big]. \]

The inner maximum is a non-negative constant as far as \(D_1\) is concerned, so it comes out of the outer one. For data association, one group is one detection's variable \(d_k\): with poses and landmarks fixed, what detection 1 saw says nothing more about detection 2. The discrete step then costs \(K \times J\) likelihood evaluations (36 for 6 detections and 6 objects) in place of \(J^K\) (46,656).

Neither step of (16.22) can increase \(\mathcal{L}\), so the loop settles, but at a local minimum: a wrong association can pull the geometry to where that association still looks best.

Max-mixture factors are the same idea folded into the factor. Maximize over \(d_k\) inside it:

\[ \phi_k(\Theta) = \max_j\; w_{kj}\, \N\!\big(\mathbf{z}_k;\, h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j),\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\log\phi_k = \min_j \Big[\tfrac12 \norm{\mathbf{z}_k - h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} - \log w_{kj}\Big] + \text{const}. \]

At every iteration the solver keeps the smallest branch at the current estimate and linearizes it like any Gaussian factor. Semantics enter through \(w_{kj}\), the probability of the reported label if the object were \(j\).

4. Expectation-maximization: soft associations

Bowman et al. keep a probability for every candidate where alternating minimization commits to one. §16.2.2 · p. 470

  • E-step. Fix poses and landmarks. For each detection \(k\) compute the probability that it came from object \(j\): \[ w_{kj} = \frac{\pi_{kj}\; \N\!\big(\mathbf{z}_k;\, h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j),\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big)}{\sum_{j'} \pi_{kj'}\; \N\!\big(\mathbf{z}_k;\, h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_{j'}),\, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big)}, \qquad \pi_{kj} = p(\text{label of } k \mid \text{class of } j). \]
  • M-step. Fix the weights. Optimize poses and landmarks with every detection-object pair counted in proportion to its weight: \[ \Theta \leftarrow \argmin_\Theta \sum_k \sum_j w_{kj}\, \norm{\mathbf{z}_k - h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} + (\text{other factors}). \]

The loop converges gradually to a local optimum. The E-step probabilities can be computed exactly through a matrix permanent, or more cheaply by approximating it. §16.2.2 · p. 470

Filling the gap: one detection, three answers

A robot in a corridor believes it is at \(p = 2.5\) m. The map has a table at \(4.0\) m and a chair at \(5.2\) m. A detection says "a chair, \(z = 2.0\) m ahead", which puts the object at \(4.5\) m: \(0.5\) m from the table and \(0.7\) m from the chair. Take \(\sigma = 0.3\) m, and a detector whose label is right 90% of the time.

The geometry term \(e^{-d^2/2\sigma^2}\) is \(e^{-1.389} = 0.249\) for the table and \(e^{-2.722} = 0.066\) for the chair. The label term \(\pi\) is \(0.1\) for the table and \(0.9\) for the chair. The products are \(0.0249\) and \(0.0592\), which normalize to weights \(0.30\) and \(0.70\).

  • Nearest neighbour looks at distance only and picks the table. The detection then says the robot is at \(4.0 - 2.0 = 2.0\) m.
  • Max-mixture picks the largest product, the chair: \(5.2 - 2.0 = 3.2\) m.
  • EM uses both: \(0.30 \times 2.0 + 0.70 \times 3.2 = 2.84\) m. Without the label the weights would be \(0.79\) and \(0.21\), and the answer \(2.25\) m.

Why the M-step is a weighted least squares: EM maximizes the log-likelihood averaged over the associations, \(\sum_k\sum_j w_{kj} \log\big[\pi_{kj}\,\N(\mathbf{z}_k; h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j), \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\big]\), and its only part that depends on \(\Theta\) is \(-\tfrac12\sum_k\sum_j w_{kj}\norm{\mathbf{z}_k - h(\Theta, \boldsymbol{\ell}_j)}^2_{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\). It is the "weights, then weighted least squares" loop of IRLS (Week 6 · Day 4) with probabilities as weights.

5. The three methods on one problem

Try it: a robot returns to a mapped room with four chairs and two tables. Its odometry has drifted 0.8 m, more than half the spacing of the row. Press Step three times. Then switch the class labels off and repeat. Then try EM with the labels still off.

Notice that with max-mixture and labels, three steps take the error from 0.62 to 0.23 to 0.03 m and the wrong associations from 3 to 1 to 0, although detection z4 carries a wrong label. Without labels the same method stays at 0.82 m with four wrong associations, and so does nearest neighbour. EM without labels still escapes, in about six steps, because each wrong pairing leaves some weight on the right object. At \(\sigma = 0.15\) m all three are stuck: an overconfident noise model turns soft weights into hard ones. At \(\sigma = 0.8\) m the wrong label on z4 outvotes the geometry.

The book names three more families. Multi-hypothesis solvers (MH-iSAM2, iMHS) keep several assignments alive and prune the search. Continuous relaxations replace a label by a probability vector on the simplex and optimize it as a continuous variable. Marginalization sums the discrete variables out, which leaves a non-Gaussian mixture over \(\Theta\) for specialized solvers. §16.2.2 · pp. 470–471

Check yourself

Q1. Ten detections can each come from one of four mapped objects. How many likelihood evaluations does one discrete step of (16.22) need when (16.20) holds, and how many joint assignments exist?

Each detection is decided on its own: 10 × 4 = 40 evaluations. The joint space has 4¹⁰ = 1,048,576 assignments.

Q2. A detection is at distance 0.3 m from candidate A and 0.6 m from candidate B, with σ = 0.3 m and no label information. What is the EM weight of A?

Geometry terms: exp(−0.09/0.18) = 0.607 for A and exp(−0.36/0.18) = 0.135 for B. Weight of A = 0.607/(0.607 + 0.135) = 0.82.

Q3. In the same situation the detection is labelled "table", A is a chair, B is a table, and the label is right 90% of the time. Which candidate does a max-mixture factor select?

Products: A 0.1 × 0.607 = 0.061, B 0.9 × 0.135 = 0.122. Max-mixture keeps the single largest, B. EM would keep both, with weights 0.33 and 0.67.

Q4. Why can alternating minimization end with wrong associations even though every step lowers the cost?

Block coordinate descent stops when neither block can improve alone, as in the widget with labels off.

Recap

  1. Classes, symmetries, pose hypotheses, and associations are discrete unknowns. With poses and shapes they form the MAP problem (16.16), a hybrid factor graph with cost \(\mathcal{L}(\Theta, D)\).
  2. Fixing \(D\) leaves least squares. Fixing \(\Theta\) leaves a discrete problem that splits into conditionally independent groups.
  3. Alternating minimization and max-mixtures make hard choices, re-made every iteration. EM keeps weights \(w_{kj}\) and solves a weighted least squares.
  4. All of them are local. Labels and a sensible noise model decide whether the local answer is the right one.
