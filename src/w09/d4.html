Week 9 · Day 4

The semidefinite relaxation of pose-graph optimization and its certificate

After today you can write the semidefinite relaxation of rotation-only pose-graph optimization, derive the dual of a QCQP, and use the certificate matrix to prove that a candidate solution is globally optimal.

Read first: §6.1.2.2 "Forming the Relaxation" and Theorem 6.1, pp. 160–161

Only two pages today, because the lesson adds the duality theory the book leaves out. While you read, look for:

  • The two relaxation steps: from \(\SO(d)\) to \(\operatorname{O}(d)\), then from \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\) to \(\mathbf{Z}\) (p. 160).
  • The chain of three optimal values after Problem 6.3 (p. 160).
  • The condition in Theorem 6.1 under which the relaxation is exact (p. 161).

Today's big idea

Shor's relaxation of Problem 6.2 is an SDP in a matrix \(\mathbf{Z}\) that stands for \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\). Its dual hands you a test: from any candidate \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}\) build a matrix \(\mathbf{S}=\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\); if \(\mathbf{S}\) has no negative eigenvalue, \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}\) is a global optimum. Theorem 6.1 says that for small enough noise the test succeeds at the true optimum.

1. Two relaxation steps

Yesterday ended with Problem 6.2, \(\min\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})\) over \(\mathbf{R}=(\mathbf{R}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{R}_n)\in\SO(d)^n\). The book relaxes it twice. §6.1.2.2 · p. 160

Step 1: allow reflections. A rotation is an orthogonal matrix, \(\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_i=\mathbf{I}_d\), with determinant \(+1\). The orthogonal group \(\operatorname{O}(d)\) keeps the first condition and drops the second, so it also contains reflections (determinant \(-1\)). Orthonormality is a set of quadratic equations in the entries, so the problem over \(\operatorname{O}(d)^n\) is a QCQP with no linear terms (the book says homogeneous). The map \(\operatorname{BlockDiag}_{d\times d}\) of (6.19) just reads off the \(n\) diagonal blocks of a \(dn\times dn\) matrix, and the diagonal blocks of \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\) are \(\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_i\): eq. (6.20) · p. 160

\[ p^\star_{\mathrm{O}}=\min_{\mathbf{R}\in\R^{d\times dn}}\tr\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\bigr)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\operatorname{BlockDiag}_{d\times d}(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})=(\mathbf{I}_d,\dots,\mathbf{I}_d). \]

Step 2: lift and drop the rank. The unknown enters only through \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\). This is Day 2 with a matrix in place of the vector \(\mathbf{x}\): \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\) is symmetric, positive semidefinite, and has rank \(d\) (not 1, because \(\mathbf{R}\) has \(d\) rows). Call it \(\mathbf{Z}\), keep "positive semidefinite", delete "rank \(d\)": Problem 6.3, eq. (6.21) · p. 160

\[ p^\star_{\text{SDP}}=\min_{\mathbf{Z}\succeq0}\tr\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{Z}\bigr)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\operatorname{BlockDiag}_{d\times d}(\mathbf{Z})=(\mathbf{I}_d,\dots,\mathbf{I}_d). \]

That is the chain \(p^\star_{\text{MLE}}\ge p^\star_{\mathrm{O}}\ge p^\star_{\text{SDP}}\). And as on Day 2: if the SDP minimizer happens to be \(\mathbf{Z}^\star=\mathbf{R}^{\star\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^\star\) with \(\mathbf{R}^\star\in\SO(d)^n\), then \(\mathbf{R}^\star\) is a global optimum of Problem 6.2. pp. 160–161

It helps to see what \(\mathbf{Z}\) contains. Its \((i,j)\) block is \(\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_j\), the relative rotation between poses \(i\) and \(j\). For two planar poses with headings \(\theta_1,\theta_2\),

\[ \mathbf{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}_2 & \mathbf{R}(\theta_2-\theta_1)\\ \mathbf{R}(\theta_1-\theta_2) & \mathbf{I}_2\end{bmatrix}. \]

Only the difference of the headings appears. Turning the whole solution by a common rotation \(\mathbf{G}\) replaces \(\mathbf{R}\) by \(\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}\) and leaves \(\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{G}\T\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}\) unchanged. The lifted variable stores all pairwise relative rotations and has no gauge freedom. The cost is its size: for 1000 poses in 3-D, \(\mathbf{Z}\) is \(3000\times3000\).

2. Lagrangian duality for a QCQP

Day 2 got the lower bound by enlarging the feasible set. There is a second route to the same bound, and it is the one that produces certificates. Take the generic problem (QCQP): minimize \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}\) subject to \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{A}_i\mathbf{x}=b_i\). Introduce one number \(\lambda_i\) per constraint, a Lagrange multiplier, and form the Lagrangian

\[ L(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}-\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i\bigl(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{A}_i\mathbf{x}-b_i\bigr)=\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\,\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\T\boldsymbol{\lambda},\qquad \mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\triangleq\mathbf{C}-\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i\mathbf{A}_i . \]

Two observations do all the work.

  • At any feasible \(\mathbf{x}\), the brackets are zero, so \(L(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}\) whatever \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) is.
  • Minimize \(L\) over all \(\mathbf{x}\), ignoring the constraints. If \(\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\succeq0\), the quadratic term is never negative and the minimum is \(\mathbf{b}\T\boldsymbol{\lambda}\), at \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}\). If \(\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\) has a direction \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}\lt0\), scaling \(\mathbf{x}=s\mathbf{v}\) sends \(L\) to \(-\infty\).

The unconstrained minimum is called the dual function \(q(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\). An unconstrained minimum is at most the value at any particular point, so for every feasible \(\mathbf{x}\): \(q(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\le L(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}\). Hence \(q(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\le p^\star\) for every \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\). This is weak duality. The best such bound is the dual problem:

\[ \max_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\R^m}\ \mathbf{b}\T\boldsymbol{\lambda}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{C}-\sum_{i=1}^m\lambda_i\mathbf{A}_i\succeq0 . \]

It is again an SDP, with only \(m\) unknowns. Doing the same construction on Shor's (SDP) gives the very same dual, so the two routes meet: \(\text{dual value}\le d^\star\le p^\star\). For a convex problem with a strictly feasible point (Slater's condition; here \(\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{I}\) is one for Problem 6.3) the first inequality is an equality, which is called strong duality.

Example: minimize \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}\) over unit vectors. One constraint, \(\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}\), \(b=1\), so \(\mathbf{S}(\lambda)=\mathbf{C}-\lambda\mathbf{I}\), which is positive semidefinite exactly when \(\lambda\) is at most the smallest eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{C}\). The dual optimum is \(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{C})\), the same \(-1\) that the disk picture gave for \(\mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}3 & 2\\ 2 & 0\end{bmatrix}\) on Day 2.

3. The certificate matrix

Filling the gap: a sufficient condition for global optimality

Claim. Let \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) be feasible for (QCQP). If some \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\) gives a matrix \(\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\) with

\[ \text{(i)}\ \ \mathbf{S}\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{(ii)}\ \ \mathbf{S}\succeq0, \]

then \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) is a global minimizer.

Proof. For any feasible \(\mathbf{x}\), using \(\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{S}+\sum_i\hat\lambda_i\mathbf{A}_i\) and the constraints,

\[ \mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}+\sum_i\hat\lambda_i\,\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{A}_i\mathbf{x}=\underbrace{\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}}_{\ge0\text{ by (ii)}}+\mathbf{b}\T\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\ \ge\ \mathbf{b}\T\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}} . \]

For \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) itself the first term is zero by (i), so \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\T\mathbf{C}\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{b}\T\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\). No feasible point costs less than \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\).

Finding \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\). Condition (i) reads \(\sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf{A}_i\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{C}\hat{\mathbf{x}}\): linear equations in \(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\). It says the gradient of the Lagrangian, \(2\mathbf{S}\mathbf{x}\), vanishes at \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), which is the first-order condition that a local solver's output satisfies. So: take the local solution, solve (i) for the multipliers, and test (ii) by computing the smallest eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{S}\). Conditions (i), (ii), and feasibility are the KKT conditions of the relaxation.

Try the test by hand on the three \(\pm1\) variables of Day 2, where \(\mathbf{A}_i\) has a single 1 at \((i,i)\), so \(\sum\lambda_i\mathbf{A}_i=\operatorname{Diag}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\) and (i) becomes \(\lambda_i=(\mathbf{C}\hat{\mathbf{x}})_i/\hat x_i\).

Case\(\mathbf{C}\) and candidateMultipliers\(\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{C}-\operatorname{Diag}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\)Verdict

The second row shows the one-sidedness of the test. Passing proves optimality. Failing proves nothing.

For pose graphs. In (6.20) the constraints are the \(d\times d\) matrix equations \(\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_i=\mathbf{I}_d\). Each gets a symmetric \(d\times d\) block of multipliers \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i\), and \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\operatorname{Diag}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_1,\dots,\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_n)\) plays the role of \(\sum\lambda_i\mathbf{A}_i\). The certificate matrix is

\[ \mathbf{S}=\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda},\qquad\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i=\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\hat{\mathbf{R}}\bigr)_{ii}\ \ \text{(the } i\text{th diagonal block, symmetrized)} . \]

The formula for \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i\) is condition (i), \(\mathbf{S}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T=\mathbf{0}\): block \(i\) of \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\) must equal \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i\hat{\mathbf{R}}_i\T\), and multiplying on the right by \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}_i\) isolates \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i\). The proof is the same as above with traces: for any feasible \(\mathbf{Z}\), \(\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{Z})=\tr(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{Z})+\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{Z})\ge\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\), because \(\tr(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{Z})\ge0\) for two positive semidefinite matrices and \(\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{Z})=\sum_i\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i\mathbf{Z}_{ii})=\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\). At \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}\) the cost equals \(\tr(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\). So if \(\mathbf{S}\succeq0\), then \(p^\star_{\text{SDP}}=\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\hat{\mathbf{R}})\), and \(\hat{\mathbf{R}}\) is optimal for all three problems of section 1 at once.

In the plane with rotations only, the blocks come out as multiples of the identity, \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{I}_2\) with

\[ \lambda_i=\sum_{\text{edges } e \text{ at pose } i}\kappa_e\,(1-\cos r_e), \]

where \(r_e\) is the edge's angular residual from Day 1. Each pose's multiplier adds up the misfit of the edges that touch it. A pose whose two edges have residuals 60° and 90° (\(\kappa=1\)) gets \(\lambda_i=0.5+1=1.5\).

4. Try the test, and when it is guaranteed to pass

The widget runs gradient descent on the headings of the Day 1 graph, builds \(\mathbf{S}\) at the point where it stops, and plots its 20 eigenvalues.

Notice that the first run (cost 11.595) has \(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S})=-0.738\): no certificate. Press "New start": cost 0.091, and the smallest eigenvalue is 0.000, a pair of zeros with everything else positive: certified. Now raise the noise, pressing "New start" a few times at each level. Up to 75° some start always earns the certificate. At 80° and above none does: there the relaxation is no longer tight.

The book's guarantee for when the relaxation is tight is Theorem 6.1. Let \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\) be the data matrix built from the true, noise-free relative poses. There is a constant \(\beta\gt0\), depending on \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\), such that if \(\norm{\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}-\bar{\mathbf{Q}}}_2\lt\beta\), then Problem 6.3 has a unique solution \(\mathbf{Z}^\star=\mathbf{R}^{\star\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^\star\), where \(\mathbf{R}^\star\in\SO(d)^n\) is a global minimizer of Problem 6.2. Theorem 6.1 · p. 161 The norm is the spectral norm, the largest singular value, so the condition says "the noise has not moved the data matrix much". The global minimizer of the full Problem 6.1 then follows from (6.16).

Intuition: why small noise is enough

This is the idea, not the proof (the book refers to the SE-Sync paper for that). With perfect measurements the true rotations have cost 0, the lowest any sum of squares can go. All multipliers are zero, so \(\mathbf{S}=\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\), which is positive semidefinite: the certificate holds. For a connected graph \(\bar{\mathbf{Q}}\) has exactly \(d\) zero eigenvalues, and the rest are strictly positive. Add a little noise. The optimum and \(\mathbf{S}\) move a little. The \(d\) zeros stay at zero, because \(\mathbf{S}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T=\mathbf{0}\) by construction, and the positive eigenvalues cannot jump below zero. So \(\mathbf{S}\) stays positive semidefinite until the noise is large enough to close that gap. You saw the pair of zeros (\(d=2\)) and the gap in the widget.

Common mistakes

  • "A negative eigenvalue of S proves the solution is a bad local minimum." It proves only that this test cannot certify it. The solution may be suboptimal, or optimal for a problem whose relaxation is not tight.
  • "S should be positive definite." It never is: \(\mathbf{S}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T=\mathbf{0}\) gives it at least \(d\) zero eigenvalues. The test is \(\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{S})\ge0\), in practice "not below a small negative tolerance".
  • "The SDP optimizes over rotations." It optimizes over \(\mathbf{Z}\), a table of would-be relative rotations. Only when \(\mathbf{Z}^\star\) has rank \(d\) do actual rotations come out of it.

Check yourself

Q1. A pose graph has 100 poses in 3-D. How large is the matrix Z of Problem 6.3?

Z stands for RᵀR with R of size d × dn = 3 × 300, so Z is dn × dn = 300 × 300.

Q2. Why is p*_O never larger than p*_MLE?

SO(d) is a subset of O(d). A minimum over a superset is at most the minimum over the subset. O(d) is not convex; convexity appears only after the lift.

Q3. In a planar rotation-only pose graph with κ = 1, a pose has three edges with residuals 0°, 60°, and 180°. What is its multiplier λ_i?

λ_i = (1 − cos 0°) + (1 − cos 60°) + (1 − cos 180°) = 0 + 0.5 + 2 = 2.5.

Q4. A local solver stops at R̂. You build S and find λmin(S) = −0.7. What follows?

The condition S ⪰ 0 is sufficient for optimality, not necessary. When it fails you have no certificate, and you cannot tell which of the two reasons applies without more work (Day 5).

Recap

  1. Relax \(\SO(d)\) to \(\operatorname{O}(d)\), then replace \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\) by a positive semidefinite \(\mathbf{Z}\) with identity diagonal blocks: Problem 6.3, with \(p^\star_{\text{MLE}}\ge p^\star_{\mathrm{O}}\ge p^\star_{\text{SDP}}\).
  2. The Lagrangian of a QCQP is \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\T\boldsymbol{\lambda}\); its dual maximizes \(\mathbf{b}\T\boldsymbol{\lambda}\) subject to \(\mathbf{S}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\succeq0\) and always bounds \(p^\star\) from below.
  3. If a feasible candidate has multipliers with \(\mathbf{S}\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{0}\) and \(\mathbf{S}\succeq0\), it is globally optimal. For PGO, \(\mathbf{S}=\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\) with \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i=(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\mathbf{R}}\T\hat{\mathbf{R}})_{ii}\).
  4. The test is one-sided: passing proves optimality, failing proves nothing.
  5. Theorem 6.1: for noise below a graph-dependent threshold the relaxation is exact and its unique solution is \(\mathbf{R}^{\star\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}^\star\).
