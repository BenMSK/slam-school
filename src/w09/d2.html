Shor's relaxation: from a QCQP to a semidefinite program

After today you can lift a quadratically constrained quadratic program to a matrix problem, drop the rank constraint to get a semidefinite program, and say what its solution proves about the original problem.

Today's big idea

A quadratic function of a vector \(\mathbf{x}\) is a linear function of the matrix \(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T\). Rewrite the problem in \(\mathbf{X}\) and all the non-convexity collects in one statement, "\(\mathbf{X}\) has rank 1". Delete that statement and what is left is a convex problem whose optimum bounds the original from below, and often equals it.

1. Quadratically constrained quadratic programs

A quadratically constrained quadratic program (QCQP) minimizes a quadratic function subject to quadratic equations. The book's generic form is §6.1.1 · p. 152

\[ p^\star = \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\R^n}\ \mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x} \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{A}_i\mathbf{x} = b_i,\quad i=1,\dots,m, \tag{QCQP} \]

where \(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{A}_1,\dots,\mathbf{A}_m\) are symmetric \(n\times n\) matrices (the book writes \(\mathbb{S}^n\) for that set) and \(p^\star\) is the optimal value. The constraints matter for SLAM because they can describe the sets SLAM variables live in:

RequirementAs quadratic equationsThe matrices
\(\mathbf{x}\) is a unit vector\(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{x}=1\)\(\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}\), \(b=1\)
each \(x_i\) is \(+1\) or \(-1\)\(x_i^2=1\) for every \(i\)\(\mathbf{A}_i\) has a single 1 at position \((i,i)\), \(b_i=1\)
\(\begin{bmatrix}x_1 & x_3\\ x_2 & x_4\end{bmatrix}\) has orthonormal columns\(x_1^2+x_2^2=1\), \(x_3^2+x_4^2=1\), \(x_1x_3+x_2x_4=0\)for the last one, \(\mathbf{A}\) has \(\tfrac12\) at positions \((1,3),(3,1),(2,4),(4,2)\), \(b=0\)

The third row is why QCQPs reach SLAM: the conditions that make a matrix a rotation are quadratic in its entries. Each of these feasible sets is curved or disconnected, so none is convex (Day 1), and a QCQP is hard in general.

2. The trace trick and the lift

The trace \(\tr(\mathbf{M})\) of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries. Two facts: a \(1\times1\) matrix equals its own trace, and \(\tr(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B})=\tr(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A})\) whenever both products are defined (the cyclic property; both sides equal \(\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}\)). Apply them to the number \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\), moving the leading \(\mathbf{x}\T\) to the back: eq. (6.1) · p. 152

\[ \mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x} = \tr\bigl(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\bigr) = \tr\bigl(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T\bigr) = \tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}), \qquad \mathbf{X}\triangleq\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T. \]

The matrix \(\mathbf{X}\) holds all products \(X_{ij}=x_ix_j\), and \(\tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X})=\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}X_{ij}\) is a weighted sum of them: linear in \(\mathbf{X}\). Check with numbers. For \(\mathbf{x}=(1,2)\) and \(\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}3 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\):

\[ \mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x} = 3\cdot1 + 2\cdot(1\cdot1\cdot2) + 0\cdot4 = 7, \qquad \mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & 4\end{bmatrix},\quad \tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{X}) = 3\cdot1+1\cdot2+1\cdot2+0\cdot4 = 7 . \]

Moving from \(\mathbf{x}\) to \(\mathbf{X}\) is called lifting. Every lifted matrix has three properties: it is symmetric, it is positive semidefinite (written \(\mathbf{X}\succeq0\)) because \(\mathbf{v}\T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}=(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{v})^2\ge0\), and it has rank 1 because every column is a multiple of \(\mathbf{x}\). The book states that the converse also holds: eq. (6.3) · p. 152

\[ \mathbf{X}\succeq0 \ \text{ and } \ \rank(\mathbf{X})=1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T \ \text{ for some } \mathbf{x}\in\R^n . \]

Filling the gap: why a rank-1 positive semidefinite matrix is an outer product

The book says this is "easily shown" by an eigendecomposition. Here it is. A symmetric matrix can be written \(\mathbf{X}=\sum_k\lambda_k\mathbf{u}_k\mathbf{u}_k\T\) with real eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\) and orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{u}_k\) (Week 1 · Day 5). Its rank is the number of nonzero eigenvalues, and it is positive semidefinite exactly when no eigenvalue is negative. Rank 1 and \(\mathbf{X}\succeq0\) together leave one eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\gt0\) and the rest zero, so

\[ \mathbf{X}=\lambda_1\mathbf{u}_1\mathbf{u}_1\T = \mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T \quad\text{with}\quad \mathbf{x}=\sqrt{\lambda_1}\,\mathbf{u}_1 . \]

This is also the recipe for getting \(\mathbf{x}\) back from \(\mathbf{X}\). Note that \(-\mathbf{x}\) gives the same \(\mathbf{X}\); the sign is lost in the lift, and it does not matter because \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\) cannot see it either.

3. Drop the rank constraint

Using (6.1) on the objective and on every constraint, and (6.3) to say which \(\mathbf{X}\) are allowed, (QCQP) becomes the equivalent problem (6.4). Deleting its last line gives Shor's relaxation: eq. (6.4), (SDP) · p. 153

\[ \begin{aligned} p^\star = \min_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{S}^n}\ & \tr(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}) \\ \text{s.t.}\ & \tr(\mathbf{A}_i\mathbf{X})=b_i,\ \ i=1,\dots,m, \\ & \mathbf{X}\succeq0, \\ & \rank(\mathbf{X})=1 \end{aligned} \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad \begin{aligned} d^\star = \min_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{S}^n}\ & \tr(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}) \\ \text{s.t.}\ & \tr(\mathbf{A}_i\mathbf{X})=b_i,\ \ i=1,\dots,m, \\ & \mathbf{X}\succeq0 . \end{aligned} \]

Look at what remains on the right. The objective is linear in \(\mathbf{X}\). The equality constraints are linear, so they cut out a flat (affine) set. The set \(\mathbf{X}\succeq0\) is convex (Day 1, section 4). An intersection of convex sets is convex, and a linear function is convex. So the right-hand problem is a convex problem. Problems of this shape are called semidefinite programs (SDPs) and can be solved in polynomial time. §6.1.1 · p. 153

The price is size. \(\mathbf{x}\) has \(n\) entries; a symmetric \(\mathbf{X}\) has \(n(n+1)/2\) free entries (footnote 1 on p. 153). For \(n=3\) that is 6 instead of 3; for \(n=3000\) it is about 4.5 million.

Filling the gap: see the relaxation for n = 2

Take the QCQP "minimize \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}\) over unit vectors \(\mathbf{x}\in\R^2\)". Write \(\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}a & b\\ b & c\end{bmatrix}\). The constraint \(\tr(\mathbf{X})=1\) says \(a+c=1\). A symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix with positive trace is positive semidefinite exactly when its determinant is non-negative, \(ac-b^2\ge0\). Change coordinates to \(u=a-c\) and \(v=2b\). Then \(ac=\tfrac14\bigl((a+c)^2-(a-c)^2\bigr)=\tfrac14(1-u^2)\), and

\[ ac-b^2\ge0 \iff u^2+v^2\le1 . \]

So the feasible set of the SDP is a disk. Rank 1 means determinant zero, which is the boundary circle. A unit vector \(\mathbf{x}=(\cos t,\sin t)\) lifts to \(u=\cos^2t-\sin^2t=\cos2t\), \(v=2\sin t\cos t=\sin2t\): the point at angle \(2t\) on that circle. Shor's relaxation fills the circle in.

The objective becomes \(\tr(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{X}) = \tfrac12(C_{11}+C_{22}) + \tfrac12(C_{11}-C_{22})\,u + C_{12}\,v\), a linear function on the disk. A linear function on a disk takes its smallest value at a boundary point, so here the SDP always has a rank-1 solution and \(d^\star=p^\star\). Example: \(\mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}3 & 2\\ 2 & 0\end{bmatrix}\) gives \(1.5+1.5u+2v\), smallest at \((u,v)=-(1.5,2)/2.5=(-0.6,-0.8)\) with value \(1.5-2.5=-1\). Then \(a=0.2\), \(c=0.8\), \(b=-0.4\), which is \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T\) for \(\mathbf{x}=(1,-2)/\sqrt5\). And \(-1\) is indeed the smallest eigenvalue of \(\mathbf{C}\).

4. What the relaxation tells you

Every feasible \(\mathbf{x}\) of (QCQP) lifts to a feasible \(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T\) of (SDP) with the same cost, so the SDP minimizes the same objective over a larger set: eq. (6.5), (6.6) · p. 153

\[ d^\star\le p^\star, \qquad\text{and for any feasible } \hat{\mathbf{x}}:\quad f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})-p^\star \ \le\ f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})-d^\star, \qquad f(\mathbf{x})\triangleq\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}. \]

The left side of the second inequality is the suboptimality of \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), which nobody can compute because \(p^\star\) is unknown. The right side can be computed. This is the altimeter of Day 1.

What you findWhat it proves
The SDP minimizer \(\mathbf{X}^\star\) has rank 1Factor it as \(\mathbf{x}^\star\mathbf{x}^{\star\mathsf{T}}\). Then \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is feasible for (QCQP) and \(f(\mathbf{x}^\star)=d^\star\le p^\star\), so \(\mathbf{x}^\star\) is a global minimizer. The relaxation is exact. p. 154
Some feasible \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), from any method, has \(f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})=d^\star\)\(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) is a global minimizer, by (6.6).
\(\mathbf{X}^\star\) has rank above 1 and the best \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) you have costs more than \(d^\star\)Only that \(p^\star\) lies in \([d^\star,f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})]\). The relaxation may not be tight.

The disk example is always exact. Here is one that is not. Three variables, each \(\pm1\), every pair penalized for agreeing:

\[ \min\ 2(x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3) \quad\text{s.t.}\quad x_1^2=x_2^2=x_3^2=1 . \]

Since \((x_1+x_2+x_3)^2 = 3+2(x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3)\), the cost equals \((x_1+x_2+x_3)^2-3\). Three signs cannot sum to zero; the best is a sum of \(\pm1\), so \(p^\star=1-3=-2\). In the relaxation, \(\mathbf{X}\) is positive semidefinite with ones on its diagonal, and the cost is \(2(X_{12}+X_{13}+X_{23})=\mathbf{1}\T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{1}-3\), where \(\mathbf{1}\) is the vector of ones. Because \(\mathbf{X}\succeq0\), \(\mathbf{1}\T\mathbf{X}\mathbf{1}\ge0\), so the cost is at least \(-3\); and \(X_{12}=X_{13}=X_{23}=-\tfrac12\) reaches it with a matrix whose eigenvalues are \(0,\tfrac32,\tfrac32\). So \(d^\star=-3\lt p^\star=-2\), and \(\mathbf{X}^\star\) has rank 2.

Try it: change the three couplings and compare the two optimal values.

Notice that at (1, 1, 1) the relaxed arrows sit 120° apart, d* = −3 is below p* = −2, and X* has eigenvalues 0, 1.5, 1.5. Press "Consistent (1, 1, −1)": now all three wishes can be met, the relaxed arrows line up, d* = p* = −6, and X* has rank 1.

Intuition

Any positive semidefinite \(\mathbf{X}\) with ones on the diagonal is a table of dot products \(X_{ij}=\mathbf{y}_i\cdot\mathbf{y}_j\) of unit vectors. The QCQP forces those vectors onto a line, where a unit vector can only be \(+1\) or \(-1\). The relaxation lets them turn into extra dimensions. If the best arrangement still fits on a line, the relaxation is exact. For three variables a plane is always enough room, which is why the widget can draw the answer.

One more practical remark from the book: general-purpose SDP software becomes slow and memory-hungry on large problems, so robotics uses specialized solvers such as SE-Sync. §6.1.1 · p. 154 That is Day 5.

Common mistakes

  • "The SDP solution is a better solution of the QCQP." If its rank is above 1 it is not a solution of the QCQP at all. Its value is a bound, not an achievable cost.
  • "A relaxation that is not tight is useless." The bound \(f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})-d^\star\) still limits how wrong any candidate can be. In the three-variable example it says "at most 1 above optimal".
  • "Lifting changes the problem." Problem (6.4) with the rank constraint is exactly (QCQP). Only deleting the rank constraint changes it.

Check yourself

Q1. What is the lift X = xxᵀ of x = (2, −1)?

The entries are the products x_i x_j: 2·2 = 4, 2·(−1) = −2, (−1)·(−1) = 1. Its determinant is 4 − 4 = 0, as it must be for rank 1.

Q2. A QCQP has n = 4 unknowns. How many free numbers does the matrix variable of its Shor relaxation have?

A symmetric 4 × 4 matrix has 4 diagonal and 6 off-diagonal free entries: n(n+1)/2 = 10.

Q3. A local solver returns a feasible point with cost 12.0. The Shor relaxation of the same problem has optimal value 11.4. Which statement is certain?

d* ≤ p* ≤ f(x̂) gives 11.4 ≤ p* ≤ 12.0. Whether the point is optimal depends on whether the relaxation is tight, which these two numbers alone do not settle.

Q4. An SDP solver returns X* with eigenvalues 5, 0, 0 and eigenvector u for the eigenvalue 5. What can you do with it?

One positive eigenvalue means rank 1, so X* = xxᵀ with x = √5 · u. That x is feasible and attains the lower bound d*, so it is globally optimal.

Recap

  1. A QCQP has a quadratic objective and quadratic equality constraints; unit vectors, signs, and orthonormal columns all fit.
  2. \(\mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}=\tr(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T)\), so in the lifted variable \(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}\T\) everything is linear.
  3. \(\mathbf{X}\) is an outer product exactly when it is positive semidefinite with rank 1. Shor's relaxation drops the rank condition and leaves an SDP, which is convex.
  4. \(d^\star\le p^\star\), and \(f(\hat{\mathbf{x}})-d^\star\) bounds the suboptimality of any feasible \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\).
  5. If the SDP solution has rank 1, its factor is a global minimizer of the QCQP.
