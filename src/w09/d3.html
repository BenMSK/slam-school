Pose-graph optimization as maximum likelihood, and its rotation-only form

After today you can derive the pose-graph cost from its noise model, build the incidence and Laplacian matrices of a pose graph, and eliminate the translations to get a problem in the rotations alone.

Today's big idea

With the right noise model, pose-graph optimization has a cost that is quadratic in the entries of the rotations and translations. For fixed rotations the best translations solve a linear least-squares problem, so they can be written down and substituted away. What remains is \(\min\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})\) over rotations: one data matrix, a quadratic objective, and rotation constraints. That is a QCQP waiting for Shor's relaxation.

1. Poses, relative poses, and the pose graph

Pose-graph optimization (PGO) estimates \(n\) poses \(\mathbf{T}_1,\dots,\mathbf{T}_n\in\SE(d)\), with \(d=2\) or \(3\), from noisy measurements of relative poses. §6.1.2.1 · p. 154 A pose is a pair \(\mathbf{T}_i=(\mathbf{t}_i,\mathbf{R}_i)\) of a position \(\mathbf{t}_i\in\R^d\) and a rotation \(\mathbf{R}_i\in\SO(d)\), or as a matrix (Week 4 · Day 1)

\[ \mathbf{T}_i=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_i & \mathbf{t}_i\\ \mathbf{0} & 1\end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{T}_i^{-1}\mathbf{T}_j=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_i\T & -\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{t}_i\\ \mathbf{0} & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_j & \mathbf{t}_j\\ \mathbf{0} & 1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_j & \mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i)\\ \mathbf{0} & 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

So the true relative pose "pose \(j\) seen from pose \(i\)" has rotation \(\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}=\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_j\) and translation \(\bar{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}=\mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i)\). Example in the plane: pose \(i\) is at \((1,0)\) facing 90° (up), pose \(j\) is at \((1,2)\). Then \(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i=(0,2)\), and rotating by \(-90^\circ\) gives \(\bar{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}=(2,0)\): two meters straight ahead, as it should be.

A measurement \(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{ij}=(\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij},\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij})\) is a noisy version of \(\mathbf{T}_i^{-1}\mathbf{T}_j\). The pose graph has one node per pose and one directed edge \(i\to j\) per measurement \(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{ij}\). The book assumes the graph is connected; otherwise the problem splits into independent pieces. §6.1.2.1 · p. 155

2. The noise model, and the cost it produces

To do maximum likelihood (Week 2 · Day 2) you need a probability density for the noise. For translations the book uses a Gaussian. For rotations it uses the isotropic Langevin distribution \(\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{M},\kappa)\) on \(\SO(d)\): eq. (6.7) · p. 155

\[ p(\mathbf{R};\mathbf{M},\kappa)=\frac{1}{c_d(\kappa)}\exp\bigl(\kappa\tr(\mathbf{M}\T\mathbf{R})\bigr). \]

\(\mathbf{M}\) is the most likely rotation (the mode), \(\kappa\ge0\) is the concentration, and \(c_d(\kappa)\) normalizes. In the plane, \(\mathbf{M}\T\mathbf{R}\) is a rotation by the angle \(\theta\) between the two, whose trace is \(2\cos\theta\). The density is then proportional to \(\exp(2\kappa\cos\theta)\), the von Mises distribution on the circle with concentration \(2\kappa\). p. 156 For large \(\kappa\) only small \(\theta\) matter, \(\cos\theta\approx1-\theta^2/2\), and the density is close to \(\exp(-\kappa\theta^2)\): a Gaussian with variance \(1/(2\kappa)\). So \(\kappa=50\) means a standard deviation of \(0.1\) rad, about 5.7°. With \(\kappa=0\) every rotation is equally likely.

The generative model for each edge is eq. (6.8) · p. 156

\[ \tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}=\bar{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}+\mathbf{t}^\epsilon_{ij},\quad \mathbf{t}^\epsilon_{ij}\sim\N\bigl(\mathbf{0},\tau_{ij}^{-1}\mathbf{I}_d\bigr), \qquad\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}=\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}\mathbf{R}^\epsilon_{ij},\quad \mathbf{R}^\epsilon_{ij}\sim\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{I}_d,\kappa_{ij}). \]

\(\tau_{ij}\) is the translation precision (one over the variance per axis). The book says "a straightforward calculation" turns this into Problem 6.1: eq. (6.9) · p. 156

\[ p^\star_{\text{MLE}}=\min_{\mathbf{t}_i\in\R^d,\ \mathbf{R}_i\in\SO(d)}\ \sum_{(i,j)}\ \kappa_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{R}_j-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}}_F^2+\tau_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}}_2^2 . \]

Filling the gap: the straightforward calculation

Maximum likelihood minimizes the sum of negative log-densities of the measurements. Do one edge.

Translation. The noise is \(\mathbf{t}^\epsilon=\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}-\mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i)\), and \(-\log\) of the Gaussian is \(\tfrac{\tau_{ij}}{2}\norm{\mathbf{t}^\epsilon}^2\) plus a constant. Multiplying a vector by the rotation \(\mathbf{R}_i\) does not change its length, so

\[ \norm{\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}-\mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i)}^2=\norm{\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}-(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i)}^2=\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}}^2 . \]

Rotation. The noise is \(\mathbf{R}^\epsilon=\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}\T\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}=\mathbf{R}_j\T\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}\), and \(-\log\) of the Langevin density with mode \(\mathbf{I}\) is \(-\kappa_{ij}\tr(\mathbf{R}_j\T\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij})\) plus a constant. Now expand a squared Frobenius norm (the sum of squared entries, \(\norm{\mathbf{A}}_F^2=\tr(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A})\)) of two matrices that are both orthogonal, so each has \(\norm{\cdot}_F^2=d\):

\[ \norm{\mathbf{R}_j-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}}_F^2=d+d-2\tr\bigl(\mathbf{R}_j\T\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}\bigr) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\kappa_{ij}\tr\bigl(\mathbf{R}_j\T\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}\bigr)=\tfrac{\kappa_{ij}}{2}\norm{\mathbf{R}_j-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}}_F^2-\kappa_{ij}d . \]

Add the two, drop the constants, and multiply everything by 2 (which does not move the minimizer): that is (6.9).

In the plane. For rotations by angles \(a\) and \(b\), \(\tr(\mathbf{R}(a)\T\mathbf{R}(b))=2\cos(a-b)\), so \(\norm{\mathbf{R}(a)-\mathbf{R}(b)}_F^2=4-4\cos(a-b)\). The rotation term of an edge is \(4\kappa_{ij}(1-\cos r_{ij})\) with \(r_{ij}=\theta_j-\theta_i-\tilde\theta_{ij}\). With \(\kappa=1\) this is the cost of the Day 1 widget.

The reason for choosing Langevin noise over the Gaussian-in-the-tangent-space model of Chapter 2 is this algebraic form: every term of (6.9) is a squared norm of something linear in the entries of \(\mathbf{R}_i\) and \(\mathbf{t}_i\). §6.1.2.1 · p. 156 No logarithm map appears.

3. The graph as matrices

Box 6.1 introduces two matrices. Here they are on a graph of our own with three poses and edges \(e_1=(1,2)\), \(e_2=(2,3)\), \(e_3=(1,3)\).

The incidence matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) has one row per edge, with \(-1\) in the column of the tail and \(+1\) in the column of the head. The Laplacian is \(\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\): Box 6.1 · pp. 157–158

\[ \mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}-1 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & -1 & 1\\ -1 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{L}=\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1 & -1\\ -1 & 2 & -1\\ -1 & -1 & 2\end{bmatrix}. \]

The diagonal of \(\mathbf{L}\) counts the edges at each node, an off-diagonal entry is \(-1\) where an edge exists, and edge directions have disappeared. Every row sums to zero, so \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}\): zero is always an eigenvalue. A connected graph has exactly one zero eigenvalue.

Why this matrix shows up in estimation: put a number \(x_i\) on each node. Row \(e\) of \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\) is \(x_j-x_i\), so

\[ \mathbf{x}\T\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}=\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}}^2=\sum_{(i,j)}(x_j-x_i)^2 . \]

The Laplacian is the matrix of "sum of squared differences across edges", which is what a relative measurement penalizes. Two weighted versions are needed for PGO:

MatrixSizeDiagonal block \(i\)Off-diagonal block for an edge \(i\to j\)
\(\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{W}^\tau)\), Laplacian of the translational weight graph, eq. (6.12)\(n\times n\)\(\sum\tau\) over the edges at \(i\)\(-\tau_{ij}\)
\(\mathbf{L}(\tilde{G}^\rho)\), the connection Laplacian, eq. (6.13)\(dn\times dn\), in \(d\times d\) blocks\(\bigl(\sum\kappa\bigr)\mathbf{I}_d\) over the edges at \(i\)\(-\kappa_{ij}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}\) at \((i,j)\) and its transpose at \((j,i)\)

With weights \(\tau=(2,5,1)\) on \(e_1,e_2,e_3\), the first is \(\begin{bmatrix}3 & -2 & -1\\ -2 & 7 & -5\\ -1 & -5 & 6\end{bmatrix}\). The second is the same pattern with the measured rotations written into the off-diagonal blocks. Stack the unknown rotations side by side, \(\mathbf{R}\triangleq(\mathbf{R}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{R}_n)\in\R^{d\times dn}\). Then \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\) has blocks \(\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_j\), and multiplying out block by block gives

\[ \tr\bigl(\mathbf{L}(\tilde{G}^\rho)\,\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\bigr)=\sum_{(i,j)}\kappa_{ij}\bigl(2d-2\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}\mathbf{R}_j\T\mathbf{R}_i)\bigr)=\sum_{(i,j)}\kappa_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{R}_j-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{ij}}_F^2 , \]

the rotation half of (6.9). (Each edge puts \(\kappa_{ij}\mathbf{I}_d\) on two diagonal blocks, which gives the \(2d\), and the two off-diagonal blocks give the same trace once each.)

Common mistakes

  • "The incidence matrix in (6.17b) is the one from Box 6.1." Box 6.1 puts edges on rows, so \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(m\times n\). In (6.17b) and (6.18) the product \(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{1/2}\) with the \(m\times m\) matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}\) only has matching sizes if edges are on columns. Read \(\mathbf{A}(\overrightarrow{\mathcal{G}})\) there as the \(n\times m\) transpose of the box's matrix. We do so below.
  • "The letter L in (6.18) is a Laplacian." There it is a lower-triangular factor from an LQ decomposition.

4. Eliminating the translations

Fix the rotations. The rotation terms of (6.9) are then constants, and the translation terms \(\tau_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}}^2\) are squared norms of expressions that are linear in the unknown \(\mathbf{t}\)'s. That is linear least squares (Week 1 · Day 4), and it has a closed-form solution: eq. (6.16) · p. 159

\[ \mathbf{t}^\star(\mathbf{R})=-\operatorname{vec}\bigl(\mathbf{R}\,\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\T\,\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{W}^\tau)^{\dagger}\bigr). \]

Here \(\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\in\R^{n\times dn}\) is built from the translation measurements (6.14), \({}^\dagger\) is the pseudo-inverse, and \(\operatorname{vec}\) stacks the columns of the resulting \(d\times n\) matrix, which are \(\mathbf{t}_1,\dots,\mathbf{t}_n\). (The book prints \(\mathbf{R}^*\) inside (6.16); it is the \(\mathbf{R}\) we fixed.) Substituting \(\mathbf{t}^\star(\mathbf{R})\) back gives Problem 6.2: eq. (6.17) · p. 159

\[ p^\star_{\text{MLE}}=\min_{\mathbf{R}\in\SO(d)^n}\tr\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\bigr),\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathbf{L}(\tilde{G}^\rho)+\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\tilde{\mathbf{D}} . \]

Filling the gap: where (6.16) and (6.17b) come from

Collect the translations as columns of \(\mathbf{T}=(\mathbf{t}_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf{t}_n)\in\R^{d\times n}\), and let \(\mathbf{A}\) be the \(n\times m\) incidence matrix (column \(e\) has \(-1\) at the tail \(i\) and \(+1\) at the head \(j\)). Then column \(e\) of \(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A}\) is \(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i\). The matrix \(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\) of (6.15) has \(-\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}\T\) in row \(e\), block \(i\), so column \(e\) of \(\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\) is \(-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}\). With \(\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\operatorname{Diag}(\tau_{e_1},\dots,\tau_{e_m})\), the whole translation cost is one Frobenius norm:

\[ \sum_{(i,j)}\tau_{ij}\norm{\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i-\mathbf{R}_i\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_{ij}}^2=\norm{\bigl(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\bigr)\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}}_F^2 . \]

Set the derivative with respect to \(\mathbf{T}\) to zero (the normal equations):

\[ \bigl(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\bigr)\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{A}\T=\mathbf{0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{T}\,\underbrace{\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{A}\T}_{\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{W}^\tau)}=-\mathbf{R}\,\underbrace{\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\boldsymbol{\Omega}\mathbf{A}\T}_{\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\T}. \]

You can check entry by entry that \(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Omega}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\) is the matrix \(\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\) defined in (6.14). The Laplacian is singular because \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}\): shifting every position by the same vector changes no residual. The pseudo-inverse picks the solution whose positions average to zero, \(\mathbf{T}=-\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{V}}\T\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{W}^\tau)^\dagger\), which is (6.16).

Put this \(\mathbf{T}\) back into the residual matrix and factor:

\[ \bigl(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\bigr)\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}=\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\,\underbrace{\bigl(\mathbf{I}_m-\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{L}(\mathcal{W}^\tau)^\dagger\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\bigr)}_{\boldsymbol{\Pi}} . \]

\(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\) is the identity minus the orthogonal projector onto the row space of \(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{1/2}\), so it is the orthogonal projector onto the kernel of \(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{1/2}\), exactly as the book defines it. This is least squares as you know it: the best fit removes the part of the data the model can explain and leaves the part it cannot. A projector satisfies \(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{\Pi}\T=\boldsymbol{\Pi}\), so the leftover cost is

\[ \norm{\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\boldsymbol{\Pi}}_F^2=\tr\bigl(\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\mathbf{R}\T\bigr)=\tr\bigl(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\T\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\boldsymbol{\Pi}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{\frac12}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}\ \mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R}\bigr), \]

using the cyclic property of the trace. Adding the rotation half from section 3 gives \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\) in (6.17b).

Try it: the widget builds all of these matrices for six poses. Choose a pose, twist its heading, and watch the positions and the cost.

Notice that the last two tiles always agree: at the start both are 17.83, and with pose 2 twisted by 30° both are 187.43. The left one needed the positions from (6.16); the right one, \(\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})\), never computed a position. Notice also that twisting one pose moves the other poses, and that the cost curve over a full turn is one non-convex wave.

5. What Problem 6.2 looks like

Problem 6.2 has \(n\) rotations as unknowns and a single \(dn\times dn\) data matrix \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\) that is computed once from the measurements. \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\) is positive semidefinite, since \(\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})\) is a sum of squared norms for any \(d\times dn\) matrix \(\mathbf{R}\). The book points out that the problem now has the form of rotation averaging, with a more complicated data matrix. §6.1.2.2 · p. 160

One practical point. \(\boldsymbol{\Pi}\) is a dense \(m\times m\) matrix, which would be expensive for a large graph. Equation (6.18) rewrites it using a sparse triangular factor of the (reduced) weighted incidence matrix, so that products with \(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\) stay cheap. eq. (6.18) · p. 160 The solver of Day 5 needs only such products.

The objective is quadratic in the entries of \(\mathbf{R}\). The only obstacle left is the constraint set \(\SO(d)^n\). Tomorrow it gets the treatment of Day 2.

Check yourself

Q1. In the plane, pose i is at (3, 1) with heading 180° and pose j is at (1, 1). What is the true relative translation of j seen from i?

t_j − t_i = (−2, 0). Multiplying by R_iᵀ, a rotation by −180°, flips both signs: (2, 0). Pose j is two meters straight ahead of a robot that faces in the −x direction.

Q2. A planar edge has κ = 10 and a rotation residual of 60°. How much does its rotation term contribute to the cost (6.9)?

In the plane the term is 4κ(1 − cos r) = 4 · 10 · (1 − 0.5) = 20.

Q3. A node has three edges with translation precisions τ = 2, 5, and 1. What is its diagonal entry in L(W^τ)?

By (6.12) the diagonal entry is the sum of the weights of the edges at that node: 2 + 5 + 1 = 8.

Q4. Why can the translations be eliminated in closed form, but not the rotations?

Translations are free vectors that enter the residuals linearly, so the normal equations solve them. Rotations enter linearly too, but must satisfy quadratic constraints, so no single linear solve gives the optimum.

Q5. A rotation measurement has concentration κ = 200. Roughly what angular standard deviation is that?

For large κ the angle is nearly Gaussian with variance 1/(2κ) = 1/400, so the standard deviation is 0.05 rad, which is 2.9°.

Recap

  1. A measurement \(\tilde{\mathbf{T}}_{ij}\) is a noisy \(\mathbf{T}_i^{-1}\mathbf{T}_j\), with rotation \(\mathbf{R}_i\T\mathbf{R}_j\) and translation \(\mathbf{R}_i\T(\mathbf{t}_j-\mathbf{t}_i)\).
  2. Gaussian translation noise and Langevin rotation noise give the cost (6.9), a sum of squared norms that are linear in the entries of the unknowns.
  3. The Laplacian \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) encodes sums of squared differences across edges; its weighted and block versions carry \(\tau\), \(\kappa\), and the measured rotations.
  4. For fixed rotations the optimal translations are \(\mathbf{t}^\star(\mathbf{R})\) from (6.16); substituting them leaves \(\min\tr(\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{R})\) over \(\SO(d)^n\).
