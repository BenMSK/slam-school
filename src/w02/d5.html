Levenberg-Marquardt and Powell's dogleg

After today you can explain how Levenberg-Marquardt damps a Gauss-Newton step, run its accept-or-reject loop by hand, and describe how Powell's dogleg builds a step inside a trust region.

Today's big idea

The Gauss-Newton model is only trustworthy close to the current estimate. A trust-region method limits the step to the region where the model can be believed, checks afterwards whether the cost really went down, and adjusts the region. Levenberg-Marquardt does this with a damping number \(\lambda\). Powell's dogleg does it with an explicit radius.

1. Damping the normal equations

Levenberg's change to Gauss-Newton is one term: add a non-negative constant \(\lambda\) to the diagonal of the normal equations, equation (1.37). §1.4.3 · p. 34

\[ \bigl(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\bigr)\,\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{lm}} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}. \tag{1.37} \]

Filling the gap: what problem does (1.37) solve?

It is the normal equation of the linearized cost plus a penalty on the length of the step:

\[ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{lm}} = \argmin_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\; \norm{\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}-\mathbf{b}}_2^2 + \lambda\,\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_2^2 . \]

Check: the gradient of this expression is \(2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}-2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}+2\lambda\boldsymbol{\delta}\), and setting it to zero gives (1.37). So \(\lambda\) is the price of moving. A larger \(\lambda\) gives a shorter step.

This is how LM is a trust-region method. Minimizing the model subject to a hard limit \(\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\le\Delta\) leads, whenever the limit is active, to the same equation (1.37), with \(\lambda\) playing the role of the Lagrange multiplier of the constraint. Each \(\lambda\) corresponds to some radius \(\Delta\), and raising \(\lambda\) shrinks it. LM steers the radius indirectly, through \(\lambda\).

It is also the book's Bayesian reading. The penalty \(\lambda\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^2\) is a squared Mahalanobis distance with covariance \(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{I}\). In factor-graph terms, LM adds to every variable a unary Gaussian factor that says "the update is zero, give or take \(1/\sqrt{\lambda}\)". §1.4.3 · p. 34

2. Between Gauss-Newton and steepest descent

With \(\lambda=0\), (1.37) is Gauss-Newton. With \(\lambda\) much larger than the entries of \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\), the matrix on the left is almost \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) and the step is almost \(\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\). Since \(\nabla J=-2\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\), that is a steepest-descent step with \(\alpha=1/(2\lambda)\). LM blends the two methods, and \(\lambda\) is the dial. §1.4.3 · p. 34

Filling the gap: the blend, direction by direction

Write the eigen-decomposition \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=\sum_i\lambda_i\mathbf{v}_i\mathbf{v}_i\T\), with orthonormal eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_i\) and eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\gt 0\). Adding \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) keeps the eigenvectors and adds \(\lambda\) to every eigenvalue, so

\[ \boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{lm}} = \sum_i \frac{\mathbf{v}_i\T\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}}{\lambda_i+\lambda}\,\mathbf{v}_i . \]

Gauss-Newton is the same sum with denominators \(\lambda_i\). In a steep direction (\(\lambda_i\gg\lambda\)) the damping changes almost nothing. In a flat direction (\(\lambda_i\ll\lambda\)) the denominator is about \(\lambda\) and the component becomes a gradient step. Flat directions are where GN divides by a small number and takes its wildest steps, so the damping acts where it is needed.

Numbers. \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=\diag(100,1)\) and \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}=(100,\,1)\), so the GN step is \((1,\,1)\).

Marquardt's scaling. Marquardt replaced the identity by the diagonal of \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\), equation (1.38): §1.4.3 · p. 34

\[ \bigl(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}+\lambda\,\diag(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A})\bigr)\,\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{lm}} = \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}. \tag{1.38} \]

Now each variable is damped in proportion to its own diagonal entry, which measures how sharply the cost curves along that variable. A variable with small curvature gets little damping and may take a larger step; a variable with large curvature is held more tightly. In the table's example the step becomes \((1,1)/(1+\lambda)\): both directions shrink by the same factor. A practical benefit is that the result no longer depends on the units chosen for each variable, whereas \(\lambda\mathbf{I}\) penalizes one radian and one meter alike.

3. The accept-or-reject loop

The second difference from Gauss-Newton is that LM checks its work. After computing a step it evaluates the true cost at the new point. §1.4.3 · p. 34

x ← initial estimate,  λ ← starting value
repeat
    A, b ← linearize and whiten at x
    solve (AᵀA + λ diag(AᵀA)) δ = Aᵀb
    if J(x + δ) < J(x):   x ← x + δ,  λ ← λ / 10      accept: trust the model more
    else:                 λ ← λ × 10                  reject: x stays, solve again
until converged

The factor 10 is the book's example of a heuristic. After a rejection \(\mathbf{x}\) has not moved, so \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) are still valid, but the damped matrix has changed and must be factorized again.

By hand. Yesterday's one-unknown problem from \(y=2\), where Gauss-Newton diverged. There \(A=2\), \(b=-11.07\), \(J=122.6\). Use (1.37), which for one unknown is \(\delta=Ab/(A^2+\lambda)\), and start with \(\lambda=0.1\).

This is the warning about local minima from Day 2. §1.2.3 · p. 29

5. Powell's dogleg

Every rejected LM step costs a new factorization of the damped matrix, and factorization is the most expensive part of an iteration. Powell's dogleg (PDL) avoids that. It computes the Gauss-Newton step and the steepest-descent step once, separately, and builds the actual step out of the two. If that step is rejected, the two ingredients are still valid and are combined differently. Each update of the estimate needs only one factorization. §1.4.4 · p. 35

PDL keeps an explicit trust region: a ball of radius \(\Delta\) around the current estimate inside which the linearization is trusted. The step runs along the steepest-descent step, bends sharply toward the Gauss-Newton step (the bend is the "dogleg"), and stops where it meets the boundary. Figure 1.6 shows one case. The complete rule has three:

Filling the gap: the three cases, with numbers

The steepest-descent step used here goes to the lowest point of the quadratic model along the gradient direction \(\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\). Minimizing \(\norm{\mathbf{A}(s\,\mathbf{g})-\mathbf{b}}^2\) over the scalar \(s\) gives \(s=\norm{\mathbf{g}}^2/\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}}^2\) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}=s\,\mathbf{g}\). It is never longer than the Gauss-Newton step. Then:

  • If \(\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{gn}}}\le\Delta\): take \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{gn}}\).
  • Otherwise, if \(\norm{\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}}\ge\Delta\): take \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}\) shortened to length \(\Delta\).
  • Otherwise: take \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}+\beta\,(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{gn}}-\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}})\) with \(\beta\) between 0 and 1 chosen so that the length is exactly \(\Delta\).

Example. Again \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=\diag(100,1)\) and \(\mathbf{g}=(100,1)\), so \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{gn}}=(1,1)\) with length \(1.41\). For the SD step, \(\norm{\mathbf{g}}^2=10001\) and \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}}^2=\mathbf{g}\T\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}=100\cdot100^2+1=1000001\), so \(s=0.0100\) and \(\boldsymbol{\delta}^{\text{sd}}=(1.00,\,0.01)\) with length \(1.00\). With \(\Delta=2\) the step is \((1,1)\). With \(\Delta=0.5\) it is \((0.5,\,0.005)\). With \(\Delta=1.2\) we are in the third case: \((1,\;0.01+0.99\beta)\) must have length \(1.2\), so \(0.01+0.99\beta=\sqrt{1.44-1}=0.663\), \(\beta=0.66\), and the step is \((1,\,0.663)\).

Whether to trust the model more or less is decided by the gain ratio, equation (1.39): §1.4.4 · p. 35

\[ \rho = \frac{J(\mathbf{x}^t)-J(\mathbf{x}^t+\boldsymbol{\delta})}{L(\mathbf{0})-L(\boldsymbol{\delta})}. \tag{1.39} \]

The numerator is how much the true cost fell. The denominator is how much the linearized model said it would fall: read \(L(\boldsymbol{\delta})\) as the linearized cost \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}-\mathbf{b}}^2\) at the current estimate. If \(\rho\lt 0.25\) the model promised much more than it delivered, and the trust region is reduced. If \(\rho\gt 0.75\) the model was right or pessimistic, the step is accepted, and the trust region is enlarged, by an amount that depends on the length of the step.

Example. At \(y=2\) in the one-unknown problem, \(L(0)=b^2=122.6\). The full Gauss-Newton step predicts \(L(\delta)=0\), a reduction of \(122.6\). The true cost rose to \(167.7\), a reduction of \(-45.2\). So \(\rho=-0.37\): shrink. For the damped step \(\delta=-1.58\), the model gives \(L(\delta)=(2\cdot(-1.58)+11.07)^2=62.5\), a predicted reduction of \(60.0\). The true reduction was \(122.6-15.7=106.9\), so \(\rho=1.78\): accept and grow.

In plain words

Gauss-Newton trusts its map of the terrain completely and jumps to where the map says the valley floor is. Levenberg-Marquardt and dogleg trust the map only within a certain distance, take a step no longer than that, and then look around. If the ground is lower, as promised, they trust the map a bit farther next time. If not, they stay put and trust it less.

Common mistakes

  • "λ is a step size." It works the other way round: a larger λ gives a shorter, more gradient-like step.
  • "LM finds the global minimum." It only refuses steps that raise the cost. It converges to whichever local minimum its path leads to, as the widget shows after New noise.
  • "LM is a slower Gauss-Newton." Near the solution every step is accepted, λ falls toward zero, and LM takes Gauss-Newton steps. The extra cost arises only when steps are rejected.

Check yourself

Q1. One unknown, A = 2 and b = 6. What is the Levenberg step (1.37) with λ = 5?

δ = Ab/(A² + λ) = 12/(4 + 5) = 1.33. The Gauss-Newton step would be 12/4 = 3, so the damping has shortened it.

Q2. An LM step gives a higher cost than the current estimate. What does the algorithm do next?

A rejected step means the model was trusted too far. The estimate is not updated, λ is raised, and the new, shorter step is tried.

Q3. Why can Powell's dogleg be cheaper than LM when steps get rejected?

LM must refactorize AᵀA + λ·diag after every change of λ. Dogleg factorizes once per linearization and only changes how the two steps are combined.

Q4. A step reduces the true cost by 2, while the linearized model predicted a reduction of 20. What does the gain ratio say?

ρ = actual/predicted = 2/20 = 0.1, below 0.25. The linearization does not hold over a step this long, so the region shrinks.

Recap

  1. LM solves (AᵀA + λI)δ = Aᵀb, or Marquardt's (AᵀA + λ diag(AᵀA))δ = Aᵀb: Gauss-Newton with a penalty on the step length.
  2. λ = 0 gives Gauss-Newton. Large λ gives a short steepest-descent step. Flat directions are damped first.
  3. LM evaluates the true cost after each step: lower means accept and divide λ by 10, higher means reject and multiply λ by 10.
  4. Powell's dogleg combines the SD and GN steps inside an explicit trust region, so a rejection needs no new factorization.
  5. The gain ratio compares actual and predicted reduction: below 0.25 shrink the region, above 0.75 grow it.
