Week 2 review: from factor graph to solved estimate

After today you can take a small SLAM problem from its factor graph to a least-squares cost to a solved linear system, and say what steepest descent, Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt, and dogleg each do with that system.

About 60 min · No new pages

The week in one paragraph

The posterior over all poses and landmarks is a product of factors, one per prior or measurement, each touching only a few unknowns; the factor graph draws that product. With Gaussian noise, the negative logarithm of the product is a sum of squared errors, each measured in standard deviations, so the most probable state is the minimizer of a nonlinear least-squares cost. Linearizing every measurement function at the current estimate and whitening gives \(\norm{\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\delta}-\mathbf{b}}^2\), which is solved exactly by factoring \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}\) (Cholesky) or \(\mathbf{A}\) (QR) into a triangle. Nonlinear solvers repeat that solve. Steepest descent uses only the gradient, Gauss-Newton takes the full linear solution, and Levenberg-Marquardt and Powell's dogleg limit the step to a region where the linear model can be trusted.

2. Mixed quiz

Check yourself

Q1. A planar pose graph has 6 poses, odometry between consecutive poses, 2 loop closures, and a prior on the first pose. Every factor measures 3 numbers. What is the size of the whitened Jacobian A?

Factors: 5 odometry + 2 loop closures + 1 prior = 8, each with 3 rows, so 24 rows. Unknowns: 6 poses × 3 = 18 columns.

Q2. A GPS-like sensor measures the absolute position of pose p4. How does it enter the factor graph?

A factor touches exactly the unknowns its measurement depends on. The measured value is a known number inside the factor, not a node.

Q3. A scalar factor has error 0.3 and σ = 0.1. How much does it add to the cost (1.18)?

The squared Mahalanobis distance is (0.3/0.1)² = 9: the error is three standard deviations.

Q4. You double the σ assigned to one sensor. What happens to the weight of its squared errors in the cost?

The weight is 1/σ². Doubling σ divides it by 4, and the estimate moves toward what the other factors say.

Q5. Two direct measurements of a scalar x: 2 with σ = 1, and 5 with σ = 0.5. After whitening, A is the column (1, 2) and b = (2, 10). What is the least-squares estimate?

AᵀA = 1 + 4 = 5 and Aᵀb = 2 + 20 = 22, so x = 22/5 = 4.4, four fifths of the way toward the more precise measurement.

Q6. Back substitution: R has rows (2, 1) and (0, 3), and d = (5, 6). What is δ* in Rδ* = d?

Bottom row first: 3δ₂ = 6 gives δ₂ = 2. Then 2δ₁ + 2 = 5 gives δ₁ = 1.5.

Q7. In which case is the Gauss-Newton matrix 2AᵀA exactly the Hessian of J, so that one step reaches the minimum?

The dropped term contains second derivatives of the measurement functions. For linear functions they are zero, the cost is exactly quadratic, and GN is the linear solve of Day 3.

Q8. One unknown, A = 3 and b = 6. What is Marquardt's step (1.38) with λ = 1?

(A² + λA²)δ = Ab gives (9 + 9)δ = 18, so δ = 1: half the Gauss-Newton step 18/9 = 2.

3. Pen-and-paper problems

  1. A planar robot has poses \(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3\) and sees one landmark \(\boldsymbol{\ell}\) by bearing from \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and from \(\mathbf{p}_3\). There is a prior on \(\mathbf{p}_1\), odometry between consecutive poses, and a loop closure between \(\mathbf{p}_3\) and \(\mathbf{p}_1\). Write the factorization \(\phi(\mathbf{x})\), and give the number of columns and rows of \(\mathbf{A}\).

    Solution

    One factor per piece of information, each a function of the variables it involves:

    \[ \phi(\mathbf{x})=\phi_1(\mathbf{p}_1)\,\phi_2(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2)\,\phi_3(\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3)\,\phi_4(\mathbf{p}_3,\mathbf{p}_1)\,\phi_5(\mathbf{p}_1,\boldsymbol{\ell})\,\phi_6(\mathbf{p}_3,\boldsymbol{\ell}). \]

    Columns: \(3\cdot3+2=11\) unknown numbers. Rows: the prior, the two odometry factors, and the loop closure measure 3 numbers each (12 rows), and each bearing measures 1, so \(\mathbf{A}\) is \(14\times11\). The graph is one unary factor on \(\mathbf{p}_1\), a triangle of pose-pose factors, and two factors reaching up to \(\boldsymbol{\ell}\).

  2. A scalar \(x\) has a Gaussian prior with mean 1 and \(\sigma=2\). One direct measurement gives \(z=4\) with \(\sigma=1\). Find the MAP estimate by whitening and solving the normal equation, with linearization point \(x^0=0\).

    Solution

    Both factors have Jacobian \(H=1\). Dividing each row by its \(\sigma\): the prior row is \(A_1=1/2=0.5\), \(b_1=(1-0)/2=0.5\). The measurement row is \(A_2=1\), \(b_2=4\).

    \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=0.25+1=1.25\) and \(\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}=0.25+4=4.25\), so \(\delta^*=4.25/1.25=3.4\) and \(x^{\text{MAP}}=x^0+\delta^*=3.4\).

    Check with weights \(1/\sigma^2\): \((0.25\cdot1+1\cdot4)/(0.25+1)=3.4\). The estimate sits between prior and measurement, four times closer to the measurement.

  3. A robot on a line has two unknown positions \(x_1,x_2\). Three factors, all with \(\sigma=1\): a prior \(x_1=0\), odometry \(x_2-x_1=2\), and an absolute measurement \(x_2=3\). Build \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\), then solve by Cholesky factorization and the two substitutions. What cost is left?

    Solution
    \[ \mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0\\ -1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\2\\3\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1\\ -1 & 2\end{bmatrix},\quad \mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\5\end{bmatrix}. \]

    Cholesky: \(r_{11}=\sqrt2=1.414\), \(r_{12}=-1/1.414=-0.707\), \(r_{22}=\sqrt{2-0.707^2}=\sqrt{1.5}=1.225\).

    Forward, \(\mathbf{R}\T\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\T\mathbf{b}\): \(y_1=-2/1.414=-1.414\), \(y_2=(5-(-0.707)(-1.414))/1.225=4/1.225=3.266\).

    Back, \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\delta}^*=\mathbf{y}\): \(\delta_2=3.266/1.225=2.667\), \(\delta_1=(-1.414+0.707\cdot2.667)/1.414=0.333\).

    So \(x_1=1/3\) and \(x_2=8/3\). The three residuals are \(1/3\), \((8/3-1/3)-2=1/3\), and \(8/3-3=-1/3\): the disagreement of 1 between "0 + 2" and "3" is shared equally by the three equally trusted factors. The cost left is \(3\cdot(1/3)^2=1/3\).

  4. A robot is on the \(x\)-axis at unknown position \(x\). A beacon at \((0,3)\) reports range \(z=5\) with \(\sigma=1\), so \(h(x)=\sqrt{x^2+9}\) and the true answer is \(x=4\). Start at \(x^0=1\). (a) Compute \(A\), \(b\), and \(J\). (b) Take one Gauss-Newton step and evaluate \(J\). (c) Take instead one Levenberg step (1.37) with \(\lambda=0.1\). (d) Compute the gain ratio of the step in (c).

    Solution

    (a) \(dh/dx=x/\sqrt{x^2+9}\). At \(x=1\): \(A=1/\sqrt{10}=0.316\), \(b=5-\sqrt{10}=1.838\), \(J=b^2=3.377\).

    (b) \(\delta^{\text{gn}}=b/A=5.811\), so \(x=6.811\). There \(h=\sqrt{46.39+9}=7.443\) and \(J=(5-7.443)^2=5.97\). The cost went up: the slope of \(h\) at \(x=1\) is only 0.316, but it grows toward 1 as \(x\) grows, so the straight-line model demanded far too long a step.

    (c) \(A^2=0.1\), so \(\delta^{\text{lm}}=Ab/(A^2+\lambda)=0.581/0.2=2.906\), and \(x=3.906\). There \(h=\sqrt{15.25+9}=4.925\) and \(J=(5-4.925)^2=0.006\). Lower than 3.377: accept, and reduce \(\lambda\).

    (d) Predicted: \(L(0)-L(\delta)=b^2-(A\delta-b)^2=3.377-(0.919-1.838)^2=3.377-0.844=2.533\). Actual: \(3.377-0.006=3.371\). So \(\rho=3.371/2.533=1.33\), above 0.75: the model was pessimistic, and a trust-region method would enlarge its region.
