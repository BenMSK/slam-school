Factor graphs: a picture of what depends on what

After today you can write the posterior of a small SLAM problem as a product of factors, draw its factor graph, and read off which unknowns each measurement constrains.

Today's big idea

The belief about all poses and landmarks, given all measurements, is one huge probability density. It splits into a product of small pieces, each involving only one or two unknowns. A factor graph draws that product: one node per unknown, one node per piece, and an edge wherever a piece depends on an unknown. Everything the back-end does later this week is computed on this graph.

1. Smoothing: estimate the whole trajectory

Chapter 1 is about the back-end only. The front-end has already extracted the measurements and decided which measurement belongs to which landmark (data association). Ch. 1 · p. 19

The chapter takes the smoothing view: the unknowns are every pose the robot has had so far, not only the latest one. If the unknowns are poses alone, the problem is pose-based SLAM. If landmark parameters are estimated too, it is the full SLAM problem. Historical note · p. 19

Keeping all the old poses sounds wasteful. It pays off because of structure. A filter that keeps only the latest pose ends up with a covariance or information matrix in which every entry is non-zero. With the whole trajectory kept, the information matrix has few non-zero entries and stays that way. Historical note · p. 19 The method this chapter builds toward factorizes that matrix, or the measurement Jacobian, into a triangular "square root", hence the name square-root smoothing and mapping (SAM). Historical note · p. 20 You meet the matrix on Day 3 and its sparsity in Week 3.

2. The toy example: unknowns and measurements

The book's running example has a robot at three successive poses \(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3\) and two landmarks \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1,\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\). The robot measures the bearing (the direction) to \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1\) from \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{p}_2\), and to \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\) from \(\mathbf{p}_3\). There is also one absolute measurement of \(\mathbf{p}_1\). §1.1.1 · pp. 20–21

The state \(\mathbf{x}\) stacks all unknowns into one vector, equation (1.2):

\[ \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}_1 \\ \mathbf{p}_2 \\ \mathbf{p}_3 \\ \boldsymbol{\ell}_1 \\ \boldsymbol{\ell}_2 \end{bmatrix}. \]

In the plane a pose is three numbers (position \(x, y\) and heading \(\theta\)) and a landmark is two, so \(\mathbf{x}\) has \(3\cdot 3 + 2\cdot 2 = 13\) entries. In this chapter the book treats a pose as an ordinary vector and postpones the special handling that rotations need to Chapter 2. §1.1.1 · p. 20, footnote 1

Measurements are noisy, so the true \(\mathbf{x}\) cannot be recovered. What can be computed is a probability density function (PDF) \(p(\mathbf{x})\): a non-negative function that integrates to one, equation (1.1), and says how much belief each value of \(\mathbf{x}\) deserves. The density we want is the posterior \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})\), the belief about the unknowns after seeing the measurements \(\mathbf{z}\), equation (1.3). Computing it, or its peak, is called probabilistic inference. §1.1.1 · p. 21

3. The posterior is a product of small pieces

The book rewrites the posterior with Bayes' law and arrives at equation (1.4):

\[ \begin{aligned} p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) \propto{} & p(\mathbf{p}_1)\,p(\mathbf{p}_2\mid\mathbf{p}_1)\,p(\mathbf{p}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_2) \\ & \times p(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\,p(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2) \\ & \times p(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{p}_1) \\ & \times p(\mathbf{z}_2\mid\mathbf{p}_1,\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\,p(\mathbf{z}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_2,\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\,p(\mathbf{z}_4\mid\mathbf{p}_3,\boldsymbol{\ell}_2). \end{aligned} \tag{1.4} \]

The symbol \(\propto\) means "equal up to a constant factor that does not depend on \(\mathbf{x}\)". §1.1.2 · p. 22

Filling the gap: where equation (1.4) comes from

The book gives the result in one line. Here are the steps.

Step 1, Bayes' law. \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) = p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x})/p(\mathbf{z})\). The measurements are fixed numbers, so \(p(\mathbf{z})\) is a constant, and \(p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) \propto p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x})\). The first factor is the likelihood (how probable the measurements are if the state were \(\mathbf{x}\)). The second is the prior (what we believed before measuring).

Step 2, split the prior. Assume that, before any measurement, the landmarks are independent of the trajectory and of each other: \(p(\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3)\,p(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\,p(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2)\). The chain rule of probability, which is always true, gives

\[ p(\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_3) = p(\mathbf{p}_1)\,p(\mathbf{p}_2\mid\mathbf{p}_1)\,p(\mathbf{p}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2). \]

The Markov assumption says the next pose depends on the past only through the current pose: \(p(\mathbf{p}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_1,\mathbf{p}_2) = p(\mathbf{p}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_2)\). That gives the first two lines of (1.4).

Step 3, split the likelihood. Assume the noise of each measurement is independent of the noise of the others. Then, once the state is given, the measurements are independent: \(p(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \prod_k p(\mathbf{z}_k\mid\mathbf{x})\). Each measurement depends only on the variables it physically involves, so \(p(\mathbf{z}_2\mid\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{z}_2\mid\mathbf{p}_1,\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\), and likewise for the others. That gives the last two lines.

All the sparsity of SLAM comes from steps 2 and 3: motion is local in time, and a sensor reading involves one pose and at most a few other variables.

Before introducing factor graphs, the book draws this model as a Bayesian network (Figure 1.2): a node for every variable, the measurements included, each carrying a prior or conditional density. Known quantities are drawn as squares. §1.1.1 · pp. 21–22 A Bayes net is good for describing how the data were generated. For optimization we want a picture that contains only what we solve for.

4. The factor graph

A factor graph has two kinds of nodes. A variable node stands for one unknown. A factor node, drawn as a small black dot, stands for one term of the product, and is joined only to the variables that term depends on. The measurements do not appear as nodes: they are known numbers, already inside the factors. §1.1.2 · pp. 22–23

Renaming each density in (1.4) as a factor \(\phi_i\) gives equation (1.5), \(\phi(\mathbf{x}) = \phi_1(\mathbf{p}_1)\,\phi_2(\mathbf{p}_2,\mathbf{p}_1)\cdots\phi_9(\mathbf{p}_3,\boldsymbol{\ell}_2)\). The nine factors of the toy example are: §1.1.2 · p. 23

FactorProportional toTouchesWhat it says
\(\phi_1\)\(p(\mathbf{p}_1)\)\(\mathbf{p}_1\)Prior belief about the first pose
\(\phi_2,\ \phi_3\)\(p(\mathbf{p}_2\mid\mathbf{p}_1),\ p(\mathbf{p}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_2)\)Two consecutive posesHow the robot moved between them
\(\phi_4,\ \phi_5\)\(p(\boldsymbol{\ell}_1),\ p(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2)\)One landmarkPrior belief about that landmark
\(\phi_6\)\(p(\mathbf{z}_1\mid\mathbf{p}_1)\)\(\mathbf{p}_1\)The absolute measurement of the first pose
\(\phi_7,\ \phi_8,\ \phi_9\)\(p(\mathbf{z}_2\mid\mathbf{p}_1,\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\), \(p(\mathbf{z}_3\mid\mathbf{p}_2,\boldsymbol{\ell}_1)\), \(p(\mathbf{z}_4\mid\mathbf{p}_3,\boldsymbol{\ell}_2)\)One pose and one landmarkA bearing measurement

A factor joined to one variable is called unary, and one joined to two is binary. §1.1.3 · p. 25, footnote 2 Two details matter later. A factor only has to be proportional to its density: constants that do not depend on the state can be dropped, because they change neither the shape of the posterior nor the location of its peak. And although \(\mathbf{z}_4\) is not drawn, \(\phi_9\) depends on it, which the book sometimes writes as \(\phi_9(\mathbf{p}_3,\boldsymbol{\ell}_2;\mathbf{z}_4)\). §1.1.2 · p. 23

In plain words

Each dot is one clue. "I started about here." "I then moved about this far." "From where I stood, that tree was in this direction." Each clue mentions only one or two things. The graph is the list of clues, drawn so that you can see at a glance which things each clue talks about.

Notice the count of undetermined directions. At the start it is 1: \(\boldsymbol{\ell}_2\) is seen once, and a single bearing only places it somewhere along a line of sight. A landmark prior, a range measurement, or a second sighting (sensing range 2.0) brings it to 0. With no prior on \(\mathbf{p}_1\) and no landmark priors it never drops below 3: the whole picture can shift in \(x\), shift in \(y\), and rotate without changing any measurement.

The last tile is computed: it is the number of unknowns minus the rank of the Jacobian matrix that you will build on Day 3. It makes the book's remark concrete: without the absolute measurement on \(\mathbf{p}_1\) there is no information about absolute position, because the other measurements are all relative. §1.1.1 · p. 20

5. Factor graphs as a language

Each factor can be read as one equation in the variables it touches. There are usually more equations than unknowns, and they contradict each other slightly because of noise. That is why each equation needs a statement of how much it is trusted, and why the solution will be a least-squares one (tomorrow). §1.1.3 · p. 24

Different kinds of SLAM are different patterns of variables and factors. Figure 1.4 shows four: §1.1.3 · pp. 24–25

The graph also shows how well each unknown will be determined before any number is computed. In Figure 1.5, a simulated robot moves for about 100 steps and observes about 20 landmarks. The odometry factors form a chain, and the landmark factors hang off it. A landmark joined to the chain by many factors will be pinned down well. A landmark with a single factor will not. §1.1.3 · p. 25 That is the effect you produced in the widget.

Common mistakes

  • "The black dots are the measurements." A dot is a function of the unknowns. A measurement is a number stored inside that function. Priors are dots too, and they involve no measurement at all.
  • "A factor must be a properly normalized probability." It only needs to be proportional to one. Constants that do not depend on \(\mathbf{x}\) are dropped.
  • "More factors than variables means the problem is solvable." Counting is not enough. In the widget's starting state there are 6 factors for 5 variables, and one landmark is still not determined.

Check yourself

Q1. In the book's toy example, equation (1.5), how many factors are joined to the variable p1?

Four: the prior φ1, the motion factor φ2 (shared with p2), the absolute measurement φ6, and the bearing factor φ7 (shared with ℓ1).

Q2. A planar robot has 4 poses and 3 landmarks. There is one prior on the first pose, odometry between consecutive poses, and each landmark is seen from exactly two poses. How many factors does the graph have?

1 prior + 3 odometry factors + 3 × 2 = 6 landmark factors = 10. (18 is the number of unknown scalars: 4 × 3 + 3 × 2.)

Q3. Which assumption allows p(p3 | p1, p2) to be replaced by p(p3 | p2)?

The chain rule gives p(p3 | p1, p2). Dropping p1 from the condition is the Markov assumption about the motion model. It is why motion factors join only consecutive poses.

Q4. You take a landmark-based SLAM factor graph and delete every factor that joins two poses. Which problem is left?

Bundle adjustment has pose and landmark variables and landmark measurements, but no motion prior between poses.

Recap

  1. The state x stacks every pose and landmark. The goal is the posterior p(x | z).
  2. Bayes' law, the Markov assumption on motion, and independent measurement noise turn the posterior into a product of small densities, equation (1.4).
  3. A factor graph has a node per unknown and a dot per factor, joined only to the unknowns that factor depends on. Measurements are inside the factors.
  4. Landmark-based SLAM, BA, PGO, and STEAM differ only in which variables and factors the graph contains.
  5. The graph's connectivity tells you which unknowns are well constrained, and why something must anchor the first pose.
